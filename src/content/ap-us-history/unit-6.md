# Period 6 — The Gilded Age (1865–1898)

---

## 6.1 — Contextualizing Period 6

### A Nation Rebuilding and Industrializing
- Reconstruction's collapse (1877, the **Compromise of 1877**) left the South economically stagnant and racially segregated, while the North's wartime industrial base kept expanding.
- Completion of the transcontinental railroad (1869) opened the West to settlement, resource extraction, and conflict with Native nations.
- New technologies (Bessemer steel, electricity) and abundant capital, labor (including mass immigration), and natural resources fueled the **Second Industrial Revolution**.
- Government largely embraced **laissez-faire** policy, allowing industrial capitalism to grow with minimal regulation — setting up the labor and reform conflicts of this period.

### The Civil War as Industrial Launchpad
- The war itself was a dress rehearsal for industrial capitalism: the Union's wartime need for uniforms, weapons, and railroads built large-scale manufacturing and organizational know-how that carried straight into peacetime.
- **National Banking Acts (1863–1864)** created a uniform national currency and a system of federally chartered banks — supplying the stable credit that large corporations needed to raise capital.
- Wartime and postwar **protective tariffs** (kept high through the period) shielded young American industries — especially steel — from British competition, letting domestic manufacturers dominate the home market.
- The **Homestead Act (1862)**, **Pacific Railway Acts (1862, 1864)**, and **Morrill Land-Grant Act (1862)** — all passed once southern Democrats left Congress — show how a wartime Republican majority reoriented federal policy toward western development, railroad subsidy, and higher education, laying groundwork this period built on.

### Why "Gilded," Not "Golden"
- Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner's satirical novel ***The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today* (1873)** gave the era its name: a thin layer of gold over a cheaper metal — glittering prosperity and self-made-man mythology on the surface, concealing corruption, extreme inequality, and exploitation underneath.
- That tension — dazzling industrial and technological achievement paired with corrupt politics, brutal working conditions, and unresolved questions about the proper role of government — is the throughline connecting every module in this period.

### Scale of the Transformation (by the numbers)
- U.S. population: ~35 million (1865) → ~76 million (1900).
- Roughly **13.5 million immigrants** arrived between 1865 and 1900, an increasing share from Southern and Eastern Europe after 1880.
- Railroad mileage: ~35,000 miles (1865) → over **193,000 miles** (1900) — the largest rail network in the world.
- By 1894 the U.S. had become the **world's leading industrial power**, surpassing Britain in manufacturing output — a reversal of centuries of European economic dominance.

### College Board Key Concepts for Period 6
| Key Concept | Core Idea |
|---|---|
| **6.1** | Technological advances, large-scale production methods, and new markets fueled the rise of industrial capitalism. |
| **6.2** | Migrations tied to industrialization (immigration + rural-to-urban and westward movement) transformed both urban and rural America and drove social/cultural change. |
| **6.3** | The Gilded Age produced new cultural and intellectual movements, public reform efforts, and political debates over the proper role of government in the economy. |

> Big picture: Period 6 is the story of a single underlying force — industrial capitalism — radiating outward into western settlement, southern race relations, labor conflict, immigration, urban culture, and national politics. Every module below traces one of those ripples.

## 6.2 — Westward Expansion: Economic Development

### Transcontinental Railroads
- **Union Pacific** (built west from Omaha, NE, heavily by Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans) and **Central Pacific** (built east from Sacramento, CA, heavily by Chinese immigrants — roughly 90% of its workforce by the mid-1860s) met at **Promontory Summit, Utah** (commonly, but incorrectly, called "Promontory Point" in many textbooks — Promontory Point is a different location 35 miles south), on **May 10, 1869**, joined by a ceremonial golden spike — the completion of the first transcontinental railroad.
- Financed through massive federal **land grants** (checkerboard sections along the right-of-way) and government bonds — a direct legacy of the wartime Pacific Railway Acts (see 6.1) — meaning taxpayers underwrote much of the risk while private companies (and insiders, via schemes like **Crédit Mobilier**; see 6.13) captured the profit.
- By 1883, four more transcontinental lines connected the country, cementing a truly national market (see 6.6).
- **Cultural/ecological impact:** railroads brought hunters who nearly exterminated the American **bison** (from tens of millions to a few hundred by the 1880s) both for hides/sport and as a deliberate U.S. Army strategy to destroy the Plains nations' food and economic base, accelerating their confinement to reservations (see 6.3).

### Mining Frontier
- **California Gold Rush (1849)** was the first of a series of boom-and-bust mineral rushes — Colorado ("Pikes Peak or Bust," 1859), Nevada's **Comstock Lode** (1859, the richest silver strike in U.S. history, which underwrote San Francisco's growth), Black Hills of the Dakotas (1874, gold — see 6.3), and Montana/Idaho.
- Pattern: individual prospectors staked early claims, but within a few years **corporate, capital-intensive hard-rock mining** (deep shafts, hydraulic mining, stamp mills) pushed out small miners — mining quickly became big business, not a romantic solo endeavor.
- Boomtowns → often ghost towns once easily accessible ore ran out (exceptions where a diversified economy took root: San Francisco, Denver, Helena).

### Cattle Frontier
- Vast open grasslands from Texas to Canada; ranching techniques (branding, roundups, the lariat) borrowed directly from **Mexican vaquero** traditions.
- Railroads enabled the **long drives**: Texas longhorns driven north along trails like the **Chisholm Trail** to railheads such as Abilene and later **Dodge City, Kansas**, then shipped east to Chicago's stockyards — a national beef market made possible only by rail.
- Declined rapidly in the mid-to-late 1880s: overstocking of the open range, brutal winters (1886–1887 "Great Die-Up" killed huge portions of Plains cattle herds), and homesteaders fencing off the range with cheap **barbed wire** (patented by Joseph Glidden, 1874) all combined to end the open-range cattle era within about two decades of its start.

### Homestead Act (1862) and Settling the Plains
- 160 acres free to any head of household who settled and improved the land for 5 years (or could be purchased after 6 months).
- In practice, the best land near rail lines was often already claimed by railroad companies (who received land grants) or bought up by speculators; roughly **two-thirds of homestead claims failed** by 1900 given the harsh, arid conditions of the Plains — the 160-acre unit, sized for humid eastern farming, was often too small to be viable on the dry Plains.
- **Exodusters:** beginning around 1879, tens of thousands of Black southerners — fleeing Redemption-era violence, sharecropping debt, and the collapse of Reconstruction (see 6.4) — migrated to Kansas using the Homestead Act, led in part by promoter **Benjamin "Pap" Singleton**; they founded all-Black towns such as **Nicodemus, Kansas**. It was the first large organized Black migration out of the South, a preview of the later Great Migration.
- Plains farming also drew large numbers of women homesteaders filing claims independently, and immigrant farming communities (Germans, Scandinavians, Mennonites — the latter bringing hardy Russian wheat strains with them).

### Problems and Solutions

| Challenge | Solution |
|---|---|
| No wood for homes | Sod houses ("soddies") built from cut prairie turf |
| No fencing | Barbed wire |
| No water | Windmills for wells |
| Poor yields / drought | "Dry farming" techniques, drought-resistant Russian wheat strains (brought by Mennonite immigrants), federal irrigation projects |
| Isolation, harsh weather, locust plagues | Mail-order catalogs (Sears, Roebuck — see 6.5), extended kin networks, county fairs |

### Falling Farm Prices
- Increased global wheat/cotton production (competition from Argentina, Australia, Russia, Canada) plus railroad-driven overproduction at home → persistent agricultural **deflation** from the 1870s through the 1890s.
- **Deflation's vicious cycle:** falling crop prices meant farmers had to grow and sell *more* just to make the same fixed-dollar mortgage and debt payments (debts didn't shrink with prices) — driving still more production and pushing prices down further. This is the core economic grievance behind the Grange, Farmers' Alliances, and ultimately Populism.
- Farmers also blamed middlemen they could not avoid: railroads (high, often discriminatory freight rates), grain elevator operators, and eastern banks charging high interest on farm mortgages — all monopolistic actors positioned between the farmer and the market.

### Farmers Organize

**National Grange (founded 1867–68, Oliver H. Kelley)**
- Started as a social/educational fraternal organization for isolated rural families → moved into economic action (cooperative grain elevators and stores to bypass middlemen) and political lobbying for railroad regulation.
- Legislative victory: several Midwestern states passed **"Granger Laws"** regulating railroad and grain-elevator rates; upheld in ***Munn v. Illinois* (1877)**, which established that businesses "affected with a public interest" (like railroads and grain elevators) could be regulated by the states.
- That precedent was narrowed in ***Wabash v. Illinois* (1886)**, which ruled states could not regulate *interstate* commerce — a gap Congress filled with the Interstate Commerce Act the following year (see 6.12).

**Farmers' Alliances**
- Regional networks (Southern Alliance, Northwestern Alliance, and a separate **Colored Farmers' Alliance** for Black farmers, since the Southern Alliance excluded them) that together claimed over **1 million members by 1890**.
- Built large-scale cooperatives for buying supplies and selling crops collectively to escape debt to merchants and railroads; pushed for a federal **subtreasury plan** (government-run warehouses and low-interest crop loans).
- **Ocala Platform (1890):** direct election of senators, a graduated income tax, lower tariffs, federal banking/currency reform, and free coinage of silver.
- These economic grievances and this platform directly fed into the **People's (Populist) Party**, formed in 1892 (see 6.13) — the Alliance was Populism's organizational and ideological seedbed.

## 6.3 — Westward Expansion: Social and Cultural Development

### Turner's Frontier Thesis (1893)
- Historian **Frederick Jackson Turner**, in his essay "The Significance of the Frontier in American History" (delivered 1893, prompted by the 1890 census declaring the frontier line effectively closed), argued that the ongoing process of westward settlement — not European inheritance — was the defining force that shaped American democracy, individualism, self-reliance, and innovation.
- He warned that the closing of the frontier removed a **"safety valve"** for social discontent: with no more "free land" for the unemployed or dissatisfied to escape to, class tensions in eastern cities would only intensify — a fear that fed directly into the era's labor unrest (6.7) and Populist anxiety (6.13).
- **Historiographical critique (commonly tested):** later historians (notably the "New Western History" school led by scholars like Patricia Nelson Limerick) attacked the thesis as ethnocentric — it framed the West as "empty" land awaiting White settlement, erasing Native nations, Mexican Americans, and Asian immigrants already there or essential to its development. Critics also showed the "safety valve" was largely a myth: few unemployed factory workers had the capital needed to travel west and start a farm, and westward migration didn't reliably fall during industrial depressions.

### American Indians in the West

| Policy / Event | Details |
|---|---|
| **Reservation Policy** | Tribes confined to defined reservations from the 1850s–60s, often on the Plains' least desirable land; treaties (e.g., Fort Laramie, 1868) frequently broken once resources (like Black Hills gold) were found on reservation land |
| **Indian Wars** | Recurring conflict as the U.S. Army enforced removal onto reservations; **Battle of Little Bighorn ("Custer's Last Stand," June 1876)** — Lt. Col. George Custer and roughly 200 soldiers of the 7th Cavalry were wiped out by Lakota, Cheyenne, and Arapaho forces under **Sitting Bull** and **Crazy Horse**, provoked by an 1874 gold rush into the Black Hills that violated the Fort Laramie Treaty; the U.S. responded with a much larger campaign that broke Lakota resistance within a year |
| **Ghost Dance movement** | A spiritual revival begun by Paiute prophet **Wovoka**, promising that ritual dancing would restore the buffalo, bring back ancestors, and cause White settlers to vanish; spread rapidly among the Lakota by 1890 and alarmed federal authorities, who banned it and moved to suppress it |
| **Death of Sitting Bull (Dec. 15, 1890)** | Reservation police, sent to arrest him amid Ghost Dance fears, shot and killed him during a scuffle — sharply raising tensions at Pine Ridge |
| **Wounded Knee Massacre (Dec. 29, 1890)** | U.S. 7th Cavalry surrounded Chief **Big Foot's** band near Wounded Knee Creek, South Dakota, to disarm them; a shot (origin disputed) triggered indiscriminate fire that killed an estimated 150–300 Lakota men, women, and children — traditionally treated as the symbolic end of the Indian Wars |
| **Assimilation policy** | Off-reservation boarding schools, notably the **Carlisle Indian Industrial School** in Pennsylvania (founded 1879 by Richard Henry Pratt, motto "Kill the Indian, save the man") — forced abandonment of Native language, dress, and religion in favor of English, vocational training, and Christianity |
| **Reform sympathy** | **Helen Hunt Jackson's** ***A Century of Dishonor*** (1881) exposed decades of broken U.S. treaties and government mistreatment of Native peoples, building Eastern reform pressure that — somewhat ironically — helped produce the assimilationist Dawes Act |
| **Dawes Severalty Act (1887)** | Broke up communally-held reservation land into individual allotments (160 acres per family head, 80 for single adults, 40 for children); U.S. citizenship granted to those who accepted an allotment and "adopted the habits of civilized life"; "surplus" reservation land left over after allotment was sold off to White settlers and railroads. Total Native landholding fell from about **138 million acres (1887) to only 48 million acres by 1934** — a loss of roughly two-thirds, and the policy's failure led to its reversal in the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 |

### Women and Community Life in the West
- Homesteading and mining-camp life gave many western women greater practical authority than eastern norms allowed — running farms alone, taking paid work in boomtowns — which fed into the West's early leadership on women's suffrage: **Wyoming Territory (1869)** and **Utah (1870)** granted women full voting rights decades before the Nineteenth Amendment (see 6.11).
- Western settlement was ethnically diverse in practice even as Turner's thesis ignored it: Mexican American communities in the Southwest, Chinese communities tied to mining and railroad work, and Mormon settlement of Utah all shaped regional culture and drew nativist backlash (see 6.9).

### Conservation Movement
- Rapid resource extraction (mining, logging, overgrazing) across the West provoked an early conservation reaction even as industrial capitalism celebrated resource use.
- **Forest Reserve Act (1891)** authorized presidents to set aside forest land as federally protected reserves; the **Forest Management Act (Organic Act, 1897)** established rules for managing them (regulated logging/grazing, not pure preservation).
- **John Muir**, a naturalist and writer, championed pure **preservation** (keeping nature untouched) and founded the **Sierra Club (1892)** to advocate for it — a philosophy that would later clash with Progressive-Era **conservationists** like Gifford Pinchot, who favored regulated, sustainable *use* of resources.
- **Yellowstone (1872)**, established as the world's first national park, and **Yosemite** (protected 1864, national park 1890) were the era's flagship preserved landscapes.

## 6.4 — The "New South"

### The "New South" Vision vs. Reality
- Journalist **Henry Grady**, editor of the *Atlanta Constitution*, promoted a "New South" creed (from an 1886 speech) envisioning a diversified, industrialized Southern economy modeled on the North, reconciled with former enemies, and free of dependence on cotton monoculture.
- Reality fell well short: the South remained the nation's poorest, least industrialized region throughout the period — the "New South" was more a booster slogan than an accomplished transformation.

### Industrial Growth
- Rise of Birmingham, Alabama ("the Pittsburgh of the South" — steel, exploiting nearby coal and iron ore), Memphis (lumber, cotton processing), Richmond (tobacco, notably the Duke family's cigarette empire).
- Southern industry was overwhelmingly **low-wage and extractive**: textile mills paid roughly half of comparable Northern wages, relied heavily on child labor, and processed raw materials that were often shipped north for higher-value finishing — profits and capital largely flowed to Northern investors and banks.
- Chronic underinvestment in public education (especially for Black Southerners, whose schools received a small fraction of already-low per-pupil funding) left a workforce with low literacy, reinforcing the region's dependence on unskilled, low-wage industry — a self-reinforcing cycle.

### Agriculture and Poverty
- Most Black (and many poor White) farmers worked as **sharecroppers** (paid a share of the crop for using someone else's land) or **tenant farmers** (rented land, more independence but still cash-poor), both trapped by the **crop-lien system**: merchants extended credit for seed/supplies against a lien on the future crop, at high interest, locking farmers into perpetual debt they could rarely escape — a system many historians describe as replicating aspects of slavery's economic coercion under a different legal form.
- Cotton overproduction (the crop lien system pushed farmers to plant cotton for cash even when prices fell, since it was the crop merchants would extend credit against) → chronic price decline, deepening the debt cycle — mirroring the Plains farmers' deflation problem (see 6.2).
- **George Washington Carver**, head of the agricultural department at **Tuskegee Institute**, promoted crop diversification — peanuts, sweet potatoes, soybeans — to restore soil nutrients depleted by cotton and to reduce farmers' dependence on a single, price-volatile crop.

### Segregation
- **Civil Rights Cases (1883):** the Supreme Court struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875, ruling the Fourteenth Amendment restrains only *state* action, not discrimination by private individuals or businesses — opening the door to legal segregation by private actors.
- ***Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896):** upheld a Louisiana law requiring separate railway cars for Black and White passengers, establishing the "**separate but equal**" doctrine that gave constitutional cover to segregation for the next 58 years (until *Brown v. Board*, 1954); Justice **John Marshall Harlan** was the lone dissenter, famously writing that "the Constitution is color-blind."
- **Jim Crow laws** — a comprehensive legal system of racial segregation covering schools, transportation, restaurants, theaters, and virtually all public accommodations across the South.
- **Disenfranchisement** tools used together to strip Black men of the vote despite the Fifteenth Amendment: literacy tests, poll taxes, White-only Democratic primaries, and "**grandfather clauses**" (exempting from these tests any man whose grandfather could vote before Reconstruction — protecting poor illiterate White voters while excluding Black voters). By the early 1900s, Black voter turnout in many Southern states had collapsed to near zero.
- **Lynching** was the violent enforcement mechanism underlying the whole system: thousands of Black Southerners were murdered by mobs, often with little or no legal consequence, typically justified by unproven accusations. Journalist **Ida B. Wells**, after a close friend was lynched in Memphis (1892), used investigative reporting to demonstrate that most lynching victims were not accused of any actual crime; her pamphlet ***Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases* (1892)** launched a national anti-lynching campaign — a mob destroyed her press in retaliation, forcing her to continue the campaign from the North.

### Responses to Segregation

| Leader | Philosophy |
|---|---|
| **Booker T. Washington** | Economic self-help and vocational/industrial training over immediate political agitation; founded and led **Tuskegee Institute** (1881); the **"Atlanta Compromise"** speech (1895) proposed that Black Southerners would accept segregation and forgo demands for immediate political/social equality in exchange for White support of Black economic opportunity and education — widely accepted by White America but criticized by some Black leaders as too accommodating |
| **W.E.B. Du Bois** | First Black American to earn a Harvard Ph.D.; demanded immediate civil and political rights and championed higher education for a "**Talented Tenth**" of Black leadership rather than only vocational training; publicly criticized Washington's accommodationism as surrendering rights that shouldn't be bargained away; co-founded the **Niagara Movement** (1905) and later the **NAACP** (1909) — both technically just after Period 6, but a direct outgrowth of this era's debate |

> Big picture: the Washington–Du Bois debate over strategy — economic accommodation now vs. immediate political equality — is one of the most frequently tested comparison points in Period 6, and it foreshadows civil rights strategy debates that recur throughout 20th-century Black history.

## 6.5 — Technological Innovation

### Key Inventions and Innovations

| Innovation | Inventor / Company | Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Telegraph | Samuel Morse (perfected earlier, but the network exploded after the Civil War) | Near-instant long-distance communication; let railroads and businesses coordinate nationally |
| Telephone | Alexander Graham Bell (patented 1876) | Transformed business communication; Bell Telephone Company (1877) became a nationwide network |
| Bessemer / open-hearth process | Henry Bessemer (English innovation, adopted and scaled by Andrew Carnegie) | Mass-produced cheap, strong steel — the essential input for rails, bridges, skyscrapers, and machinery; underpinned the entire industrial economy |
| Electric lightbulb, phonograph, motion picture camera | **Thomas Edison** (Menlo Park, NJ "invention factory," and later the Edison Electric Light Co.) | Extended the workday and social life past sunset; Edison's Pearl Street Station (1882, NYC) was the first commercial power plant |
| Alternating current (AC) power / transformers | **George Westinghouse** (with engineer Nikola Tesla) | Won the "**War of the Currents**" against Edison's direct current (DC) because AC could transmit electricity efficiently over long distances — made citywide and eventually regional electrification practical |
| Skyscrapers (steel-frame construction, elevators) | **William Le Baron Jenny** (Home Insurance Building, Chicago, 1885, often called the first skyscraper); elevator safety brake by Elisha Otis (earlier, but essential) | Reshaped city skylines; allowed dense vertical growth of downtown business districts as urban land grew scarce and expensive |
| Refrigerated railroad car | **Gustavus Swift** (Chicago meatpacking) | Let meat be slaughtered centrally in Chicago and shipped nationwide, destroying local butchering and cementing Chicago's stockyards as a national hub — also killed off the long-distance cattle drive (see 6.2) |
| Typewriter, cash register, adding machine | Various (Remington, National Cash Register) | Created a wave of new clerical office jobs — a major driver of women entering the "pink collar" workforce (see 6.10) |
| Department stores | R.H. Macy's (NYC), Marshall Field's (Chicago), Wanamaker's (Philadelphia) | Concentrated a huge range of goods under one roof with fixed prices — symbol of a new mass **consumer culture** |
| Mail-order catalogs | **Sears, Roebuck & Co.** and Montgomery Ward | Brought the same national consumer market to isolated rural and Plains families who had no access to department stores (see 6.2) |

### Why It Mattered: Technology as an Engine of National Integration
- The telegraph and later the telephone let businesses coordinate production, pricing, and shipping across vast distances in real time — a precondition for truly national corporations.
- Railroads themselves drove standardization: to keep timetables coherent, American railroads adopted **four standard time zones in 1883** (before federal law made it official in 1918) — an example of private industry reshaping a basic feature of everyday life.
- Together, cheap steel + electrification + rail + telegraph/telephone created the infrastructure that made **big business and the national market** (see 6.6) possible in the first place — the technological and economic stories of Period 6 are really one story told from two angles.

## 6.6 — The Rise of Industrial Capitalism

### Railroads: "The First Big Business"
- After the Civil War, mileage expanded from ~35,000 to over 193,000 miles (see 6.1), fueled by federal and state subsidies (loans, land grants) as well as private and foreign (mostly British) investment capital.
- Railroads created the first truly **national market** — goods, raw materials, and finished products could move cheaply and predictably coast to coast — and their operational needs (scheduling, financing, coordinating thousands of employees across huge distances) forced them to pioneer modern **corporate management structures** (departments, middle managers, standardized accounting) that other industries later copied.
- **Cornelius Vanderbilt** consolidated numerous local lines into the New York Central Railroad — an early, large-scale example of **horizontal consolidation** in rail — while extracting huge profits; a figure historians place on both sides of the "robber baron" line.
- Problems that provoked public backlash and eventually regulation (see 6.12): overbuilding beyond real demand, financial fraud, "**watering stock**" (issuing shares far exceeding a company's real asset value — associated especially with **Jay Gould**'s manipulation of the Erie Railroad), and secret **rebates** to large, favored shippers (like Standard Oil) that smaller shippers and farmers couldn't get, effectively taxing everyone else to subsidize the biggest customers.
- The **Panic of 1893** (see 6.13) bankrupted roughly a quarter of the nation's rail mileage; **J.P. Morgan** and other bankers reorganized ("Morganized") failed lines into a handful of regional monopolies under tight financial control — a preview of Morgan's broader role consolidating American industry.

### Key Industrialists

| Person | Industry | Method | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Andrew Carnegie** | Steel | **Vertical integration** — owned iron/coal mines, ore ships, coke ovens, rail lines, and steel mills (like the Homestead Works) outright, controlling every stage from raw material to finished rail; used ruthless cost-cutting and the latest technology (Bessemer process) to undercut competitors | Both "captain of industry" (philanthropy — see 6.10) and "robber baron" (Homestead Strike, see 6.7) |
| **John D. Rockefeller** | Oil (Standard Oil, founded 1870) | **Horizontal integration/combination** — bought out or drove out competing refineries until Standard Oil controlled roughly **90–95% of U.S. oil refining** by the 1880s; organized the **Standard Oil Trust (1882)**, the first major U.S. trust, in which stockholders of many nominally separate companies handed control to a single board of trustees | Widely seen as the archetypal "robber baron"; broken up by the Supreme Court in 1911 (*Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States*, Progressive Era) |
| **J.P. Morgan** | Banking/Finance | Used **holding companies** and interlocking directorates to consolidate competing firms under centralized financial control — railroads (above), and later U.S. Steel (1901, the first billion-dollar corporation, formed by buying out Carnegie) | "Robber baron"; but also the figure whose personal gold reserves twice propped up the U.S. Treasury (1895) and the banking system (Panic of 1907) in the absence of a central bank |

### Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Trust** | Legal arrangement where stockholders of separate, competing companies transfer their shares to a single board of trustees, who then run the companies as one — used to evade state laws against one company owning stock in a "competitor" |
| **Horizontal integration** | Buying out or merging with direct competitors to dominate one stage/industry (e.g., Rockefeller and refining) |
| **Vertical integration** | Owning every stage of production and distribution, from raw materials to the finished, delivered product (e.g., Carnegie and steel) |
| **Holding company** | A corporation that exists mainly to own controlling shares of stock in other companies, centralizing control without a formal merger |
| **Interlocking directorate** | The same small group of financiers/directors sitting on the boards of many "competing" companies, coordinating behavior without formal combination |

### Laissez-Faire and Social Darwinism — and Its Critics
- **Adam Smith's** "invisible hand" (1776) supplied the classical-economics case that unregulated markets self-correct and allocate resources efficiently — the intellectual foundation for Gilded Age laissez-faire policy.
- **Social Darwinism** (Herbert Spencer in Britain, popularized in the U.S. by **William Graham Sumner**) applied a distorted version of Darwin's biological evolution to society and the economy: competition weeds out the unfit, wealth is evidence of fitness, and government aid to the poor or regulation of business only interferes with beneficial "natural" selection. This gave industrialists a scientific-sounding justification for opposing labor laws, welfare, and antitrust action.
- **Counter-argument — "Reform Darwinism":** sociologist **Lester Frank Ward** directly rebutted Sumner, arguing that human intelligence and cooperation — not raw competition — drove real social progress, and that active government planning could improve society faster and more humanely than laissez-faire ever would. This tension (does progress come from unrestrained competition, or from government-guided cooperation?) is the same debate underlying the political conflicts of 6.12–6.13, and it re-emerges as the core justification for Progressive Era reform.
- **Historiographical framing (commonly tested):** historians remain split on how to label Gilded Age industrialists — "**captains of industry**" (visionary builders whose efficiency, price-cutting, and philanthropy expanded the economy and ultimately benefited consumers and workers) vs. "**robber barons**" (monopolists who crushed competitors, corrupted politics, and exploited labor to enrich themselves). The most defensible exam answer treats this as a real historical debate rather than picking one label — Carnegie in particular is cited on both sides.
- By the 1890s: the richest ~10% of Americans controlled roughly 70–90% of the nation's wealth (estimates vary by source), the starkest wealth concentration the country had yet seen — the material reality "Gilded Age" was coined to describe (see 6.1).

## 6.7 — Labor in the Gilded Age

### Conditions
- Typical factory schedule: 10-hour days, 6 days/week (some industries, like steel, ran 12-hour shifts); wages kept low in part by a steady supply of immigrant labor willing to work for less (see 6.8).
- Workplaces were largely unregulated: no minimum wage, no maximum hours laws for adult men, no mandated safety standards — industrial accidents were common and workers' compensation essentially didn't exist, so an injury could mean total loss of income.
- ~20% of children under 15 were working for wages by 1900, often in mines (as "breaker boys" sorting coal), textile mills, and canneries, typically for a fraction of adult wages — child labor was cheap and children could fit into small machine spaces.
- **Why conditions stayed harsh (cause and effect):** a constant influx of immigrant labor kept the labor supply high (and individual bargaining power low); courts consistently sided with employers under a laissez-faire reading of "**freedom of contract**" (striking down early state maximum-hours and safety laws as unconstitutional interference with workers' and employers' right to contract freely); and neither major party wanted to alienate business donors by regulating wages, hours, or safety — the same political stalemate that shows up in 6.13.
- **Mary Harris "Mother Jones"** organized coal miners beginning in this period and became a fixture of the labor movement (her most famous action, the 1903 "Children's Crusade" march against child labor, falls just into the Progressive Era, but her organizing work traces back to the 1870s–80s).

### Management Tactics Against Labor

| Tactic | Description |
|---|---|
| Lockout | Employer closes the factory preemptively to break an organizing drive before workers can strike |
| Blacklist | Employers share names of pro-union workers so none of them can find work elsewhere in the industry |
| Yellow-dog contract | Workers forced to sign a pledge not to join a union as a condition of being hired |
| Court injunction | Employers obtained court orders declaring a strike illegal (often framed as an unlawful "restraint of trade" under the Sherman Antitrust Act — see *In re Debs*, 6.13), letting them call in police, state militia, or federal troops to break it |
| Private security / strikebreakers | Firms like the **Pinkerton National Detective Agency** supplied armed guards, and companies hired replacement workers ("scabs") to keep operating during a strike |

### Why Unions Struggled
- Employers held most of the structural advantages: courts, government troops, and (before the Sherman Act was turned on labor) simple economic leverage were all more often on management's side than labor's.
- Labor itself was divided — by skill level (skilled craftsmen vs. unskilled factory hands), by race, by sex, and increasingly by the ethnic and language divides among "new" immigrant workers — making solidarity across an entire industry hard to sustain.
- Public opinion could turn sharply against organized labor whenever a strike involved violence, especially after incidents like Haymarket, which let opponents tar the whole labor movement as radical or foreign-influenced (see below).

### Major Labor Organizations

| Organization | Founded | Key Facts |
|---|---|---|
| National Labor Union | 1866 (led by **William Sylvis**) | First attempt to organize a national federation across trades; pushed for an 8-hour day (won for federal workers in 1868) and greenback currency reform; collapsed after the Panic of 1873 |
| Knights of Labor | Founded 1869, opened to public leadership 1881 under **Terence Powderly** | Uniquely inclusive for its time — open to skilled and unskilled workers, women, and Black workers (though not Chinese immigrants, reflecting the era's nativism); peaked at ~730,000 members (1886); collapsed rapidly after being wrongly associated with the Haymarket bombing |
| **American Federation of Labor (AFL)** | 1886, led by **Samuel Gompers** | Organized only skilled workers into craft unions; pursued narrow "**bread-and-butter unionism**" — higher wages, shorter hours, better conditions through collective bargaining and strikes, deliberately avoiding broader political/socialist goals; grew to roughly 1 million members by 1901 by being seen as less radical and more durable than the Knights |

> Cause and effect: the Knights of Labor's inclusive, reform-minded, quasi-political model dominated in the mid-1880s, but its association (however unfair) with anarchist violence at Haymarket let the AFL's narrower, more conservative "pure and simple unionism" — organize only skilled workers, fight only for wages/hours, avoid revolutionary politics — become the dominant model of American unionism for decades.

### Major Strikes

| Strike | Date | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Great Railroad Strike** | 1877 | B&O cut wages 10% (3rd cut in a year) amid the depression following the Panic of 1873; 100,000+ workers walked out from Martinsburg, WV to Pittsburgh, Chicago, and St. Louis; Hayes used federal troops (first major use of the U.S. Army against strikers); ~100 deaths; first nationwide strike in U.S. history and a wake-up call that labor unrest was now a national, not local, problem |
| **Homestead Strike** | 1892 | Carnegie/Frick cut wages; private guards defeated union; 16 deaths; set back steel unions until New Deal |
| **Pullman Strike** | 1894 | Eugene Debs/ARU; Cleveland used army to deliver mail; injunction broke strike; *In re Debs* (1895) upheld injunctions against strikes; Debs → founded American Socialist Party |

### Haymarket Bombing (1886)
- Chicago, May 4, 1886: a rally in support of striking McCormick Harvester workers (part of the national **8-hour-day movement**) turned violent when someone threw a bomb at police; the blast and resulting gunfire killed 7 police officers and several civilians.
- 8 anarchists were tried for conspiracy despite no evidence linking any of them to the bomb itself; 7 were sentenced to death. Four were hanged and one died by suicide in his cell; the remaining three were pardoned in 1893 by Illinois Governor **John Peter Altgeld**, who condemned the trial as unjust — a decision that effectively ended his own political career.
- Public opinion wrongly conflated **anarchism, socialism, and the labor movement** as a whole; the Knights of Labor (which had no real connection to the bombing) lost popularity and membership rapidly, while the more conservative, "pure and simple unionism" of the AFL benefited by contrast.

## 6.8 — Immigration and Migration in the Gilded Age

### Immigration Waves

| Wave | Origins | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **"Old" Immigrants** (dominant through the 1880s) | Northern/Western Europe (British Isles, Germany, Scandinavia) | Mostly Protestant; often English-speaking or from cultures already familiar to Anglo-America; higher literacy; many had enough capital to move west and farm; easier assimilation and less nativist hostility |
| **"New" Immigrants** (surging from the 1880s–90s onward) | Southern/Eastern Europe (Italy, Greece, Croatia, Slovakia, Poland, Russia — including large numbers of Jewish immigrants fleeing pogroms in the Russian Empire) | Often poor and illiterate; mostly Catholic, Orthodox, or Jewish rather than Protestant; settled disproportionately in crowded urban ethnic neighborhoods rather than farms; many were "**birds of passage**" — young men who intended to earn money and return home, and a substantial share actually did |
| **Asian Immigrants** | China (accelerating with the Gold Rush, 1849, and Central Pacific Railroad construction — see 6.2), later Japan | Concentrated in Hawaii and California; met with the harshest legal exclusion of any immigrant group (see 6.9) |

### Why They Came, Why It Changed (Push/Pull)
- **Push factors:** land shortages and famine in parts of rural Europe, religious persecution (notably anti-Jewish pogroms in the Russian Empire after 1881), military conscription, and the collapse of traditional artisan trades under industrialization at home.
- **Pull factors:** the promise of industrial-wage work in booming American cities, cheaper and faster steamship travel (which replaced the far slower/deadlier sailing-ship crossing by the 1870s–80s), and — for some — recruitment by American companies or letters/remittances from relatives already in the U.S.
- The shift from "old" to "new" immigrant origins after ~1880 reflected which parts of Europe were industrializing and urbanizing (pushing out surplus agricultural labor) later than Britain/Germany/Scandinavia had — southern and eastern Europe simply hit that transition a generation later.
- Immigration processing: **Ellis Island** opened in New York Harbor in **1892** to process the surge of "new" immigrants arriving on the East Coast (Castle Garden had handled the job before that).

### Urbanization
- By 1900, roughly 40% of Americans lived in towns/cities (up sharply from ~20% in 1860); by 1920, for the first time, more Americans lived in urban than rural areas.
- Cities grew explosively: Chicago rebuilt bigger and denser after the Great Fire of 1871; New York's population passed 3 million by 1900.
- Housing couldn't keep pace — New York's notorious **"dumbbell" tenements** (narrow, airless multi-family buildings, so-called for their shape) packed immigrant families into small, disease-prone spaces. Journalist **Jacob Riis** documented these conditions in ***How the Other Half Lives* (1890)**, using photography to shock middle-class readers and spark early housing-reform efforts — a direct forerunner of Progressive Era muckraking.
- Ethnic neighborhoods ("Little Italy," Chinatown, the Lower East Side's Jewish quarter, etc.) let immigrant communities maintain language, religion, and mutual-aid networks even as they gradually assimilated — creating the multi-ethnic urban fabric that defined the modern American city.

## 6.9 — Responses to Immigration in the Gilded Age

### Opposition to Immigration
- Labor unions (including some AFL leaders) argued that immigrants depressed wages and were sometimes used by employers directly as strikebreakers, undercutting organizing efforts.
- **Nativists** feared "new" immigrants would dilute an imagined Anglo-Protestant American culture; strong prejudice specifically targeted Catholics (a majority-Protestant country reacting to a wave of Catholic Italians, Poles, and Irish) and Jews.
- **Social Darwinists** and eugenics-influenced thinkers ranked southern/eastern Europeans and non-Europeans as biologically or culturally inferior to "old stock" Americans — giving pseudo-scientific cover to prejudice that was really about culture, religion, and economic competition.
- Organized nativist groups formed to act on this hostility: the **American Protective Association (1887)** was an anti-Catholic secret society claiming over a million members at its peak; the **Immigration Restriction League (founded 1894)** — led by Boston elites — lobbied specifically for a literacy test to screen out "new" immigrants, a goal not achieved nationally until 1917.

### Restriction Laws

| Law | Year | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Chinese Exclusion Act** | 1882 | The first major U.S. law to restrict immigration by nationality/ethnicity outright — banned nearly all new Chinese immigration and barred Chinese immigrants already here from naturalized citizenship; renewed repeatedly, made permanent in 1902, not repealed until 1943 |
| Foran Act (Contract Labor Law) | 1885 | Banned employers from prepaying immigrants' passage in exchange for a contracted period of labor — aimed at stopping companies from importing cheap labor to undercut wages |
| Literacy test proposals | 1890s+ | Repeatedly passed by Congress starting in the 1890s (aimed at "new" immigrants, who had lower literacy rates) but vetoed by presidents of both parties until it finally became law in 1917 over Wilson's veto |

### Boss and Machine Politics
- **Political machines** — most famously New York's **Tammany Hall**, run in this era by figures like "Boss" Tweed's successors — traded jobs, housing help, coal in winter, and small favors for immigrant votes, filling a social-welfare gap that no level of government otherwise addressed.
- Machine politician **George Washington Plunkitt** unapologetically defended what he called "**honest graft**" — profiting from advance knowledge of city business (like where a new park would be built) rather than outright theft — a window into how machine politicians rationalized systemic corruption.
- Machines were bad for honest government (patronage, vote fraud, kickbacks on city contracts) but often *responsive* to immigrant constituents in ways reform-minded elites were not — a genuine trade-off historians weigh when assessing them.

### Settlement Houses and the Social Gospel Response
- **Hull House (Chicago, 1889)**, founded by **Jane Addams** (and Ellen Gates Starr), offered English classes, childcare, job training, healthcare referrals, and cultural programs directly in immigrant neighborhoods — a hands-on, secular alternative to both nativist exclusion and machine-politics patronage.
- By 1910, **over 400 settlement houses** operated in American cities, staffed disproportionately by educated middle-class women (one of the era's few socially acceptable public roles for them — see 6.10) and closely tied to the **Social Gospel** movement's belief that Christian charity demanded direct social action (see 6.11).
- Settlement houses fed directly into Progressive Era reform (child labor laws, housing codes, public health) — Jane Addams became a leading Progressive and, in 1931, the first American woman to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

## 6.10 — Development of the Middle Class

### Who Made Up the New Middle Class
- Industrialization created a genuinely new social layer between wage laborers and the ultra-wealthy: managers, engineers, accountants, salesmen, and a growing corps of clerical workers needed to run large corporations, plus professionals (doctors, lawyers) who benefited from new licensing/credentialing standards.
- This class had disposable income and leisure time in ways factory workers didn't, and its tastes and anxieties (about status, respectability, "proper" gender roles) shaped much of the era's culture and reform politics (see 6.11).

### The Gospel of Wealth (Andrew Carnegie, 1889 essay)
- Carnegie argued the wealthy have a **moral responsibility (a "trust")** to use their fortunes for the broader public good during their lifetimes, rather than passing it all to heirs or hoarding it — but he opposed direct handouts, preferring to fund institutions (libraries, universities) that let people improve themselves.
- Carnegie personally distributed over **$350 million** to causes like the 2,500+ public libraries built in his name, Carnegie Mellon University, and Carnegie Hall — the philanthropic argument for why some historians count him as a "captain of industry" (see 6.6) even as his own steelworkers struck at Homestead (see 6.7) for better pay — a contradiction the exam likes to test directly.
- Critics then and now point out the philanthropy came from wealth built partly on low wages and suppressed unions — Gospel of Wealth philanthropy and harsh labor practices existed side by side in the same men and companies.

### Working Women
- By 1900, roughly **1 in 5 adult women** worked for wages outside the home, a share rising steadily across the period.
- New technology (typewriter, telephone switchboard) opened a wave of "**pink collar**" clerical jobs — secretaries, bookkeepers, telephone operators — that hadn't existed before and were seen as respectable for unmarried middle-class women.
- Women were also concentrated in "feminized" professional fields like nursing and teaching, which paid consistently less than comparable "male" professions — an early, durable example of the gender pay gap institutionalized by occupational segregation rather than identical jobs paying differently.
- Working women's growing public and economic presence fed directly into the women's suffrage movement's momentum in this period (see 6.11) — economic independence made political independence feel more urgent and more plausible.

### Suburban Growth
- Falling land costs at the urban fringe, cheap new transportation (streetcars, and later electric trolleys), and standardized/cheaper construction methods let the growing middle class move out of crowded city cores into new residential suburbs.
- Landscape architect **Frederick Law Olmsted** (also designer of NYC's Central Park) planned suburban communities with curved roads and green space explicitly to evoke a rural retreat from the noise and density of the industrial city — suburbanization was, from the start, partly a flight from the immigrant, working-class city (see 6.8–6.9).

### Changes in Education
- Growing compulsory-education laws (state by state) pushed the national literacy rate to roughly 90% by 1900.
- College enrollment expanded from about 50,000 (1870) to 600,000+ by 1920, aided by land-grant universities (a legacy of the 1862 Morrill Act — see 6.1) and, for the first time in large numbers, women's and historically Black colleges.
- Universities embraced new academic social sciences — psychology, sociology, political science, and professional economics — reflecting a broader confidence that industrial society's problems could be studied and managed scientifically (the same impulse behind Reform Darwinism, see 6.6).

### Popular Culture
- **Mass-circulation press:** cheap, high-speed printing plus a newly literate urban readership let papers like Pulitzer's *New York World* and Hearst's papers reach unprecedented circulations through sensationalist "**yellow journalism**" — a style and business model that would shape coverage of the Spanish-American War just past this period.
- **Sports** became a genuinely mass, commercialized phenomenon for the first time: baseball as the quintessential urban/industrial-era pastime (professional leagues consolidated in the 1870s–1900s), football (organized college play from 1869), and basketball (invented 1891 by James Naismith) — all products of an era with more urban leisure time and disposable income than any before it.

## 6.11 — Reform in the Gilded Age

### Social Gospel Movement
- Protestant clergy and theologians argued that Christianity's core mission included addressing social problems — poverty, unsafe labor, slums — created by industrial capitalism, not just saving individual souls.
- **Walter Rauschenbusch**, ministering in a poor immigrant New York neighborhood, became the movement's leading theologian, arguing sin was social/structural as well as personal and calling on churches to fight for economic justice; his ideas link the Social Gospel directly to Progressive Era reform politics just after this period.
- The Social Gospel supplied much of the moral energy and personnel behind settlement houses (see 6.9) and temperance (below) — reform in this era was disproportionately organized and staffed through Protestant church networks.
- Related institution: the **Salvation Army** (British origin, active in the U.S. from 1880) combined evangelism with direct urban relief — food, shelter, addiction ministry — for the poorest city dwellers.

### Women's Suffrage
- The movement re-unified after decades of split: the rival **National Woman Suffrage Association** (Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Susan B. Anthony — focused on a federal constitutional amendment) and **American Woman Suffrage Association** (Lucy Stone — focused on state-by-state campaigns) merged in **1890** to form the **National American Woman Suffrage Association (NAWSA)**, combining both strategies under one organization.
- The West led on actual voting rights: **Wyoming Territory** granted full women's suffrage in **1869** (kept it at statehood in 1890, becoming the first suffrage state) and **Utah** followed in 1870 — a pattern historians link to western territories wanting to attract women settlers and to Mormon Utah's particular politics (see 6.3).
- By 1900, a handful of additional western states (Colorado 1893, Idaho 1896) had granted full suffrage, and many more states allowed women to vote in local elections (especially school-board elections) — incremental gains well short of the federal Nineteenth Amendment, which wouldn't come until 1920.
- The suffrage movement's growth in this period paralleled — and drew strength from — women's expanding public roles as wage earners (6.10), settlement-house reformers (6.9), and temperance organizers (below): each front gave women practical organizing experience and a stronger public voice.

### Temperance
- **Woman's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU)**, founded 1874 and led from 1879 by **Frances Willard**, grew to roughly 500,000 members by the late 1890s — the era's largest women's organization — by framing alcohol abuse as a direct threat to the home, arguing that a drunken husband's wages, violence, and neglect were fundamentally a women's and family issue, which let temperance work double as an entry point into broader political activism (including suffrage) for women otherwise excluded from politics.
- **Anti-Saloon League** (founded 1893) took a narrower, more pragmatic approach than WCTU — a single-issue pressure group that endorsed whichever candidates of either party supported prohibition, pioneering the modern single-issue lobbying model; by 1916, some 21 states had passed some form of prohibition or local-option saloon closure, building the political momentum that led to national Prohibition (Eighteenth Amendment, 1919/1920).

## 6.12 — Controversies over the Role of Government

### Interstate Commerce Act (1887)
- Direct response to ***Wabash v. Illinois* (1886)**, in which the Supreme Court ruled that states could not regulate railroad rates on *interstate* traffic (narrowing the earlier *Munn v. Illinois*, 1877 — see 6.2), leaving a regulatory gap only Congress could fill.
- Required railroad rates to be "reasonable and just" and banned practices like rebates to favored shippers and rate discrimination between short and long hauls.
- Created the **Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC)** — the first federal regulatory agency of its kind, but with weak enforcement power (it could investigate and sue in court, but courts frequently ruled against it); in practice it initially helped stabilize rates for railroads themselves more than it protected farmers and small shippers, since railroads used the ICC's public rate-setting to coordinate and avoid the destructive rate wars that had been cutting into their own profits.

### Sherman Antitrust Act (1890)
- First federal legislation to curb monopolistic practices, passed almost unanimously (part of the same "**Billion-Dollar Congress**" — the 51st, 1889–1891 — that also passed the McKinley Tariff and Sherman Silver Purchase Act below).
- Prohibited any "**contract, combination... or conspiracy, in restraint of trade**" — deliberately broad, vague language that Congress left for courts to interpret.
- Initially **ineffective against its intended targets**: ***U.S. v. E.C. Knight Co.* (1895)** ruled that a sugar-refining monopoly controlling ~98% of U.S. refining capacity was still just "manufacturing," not "commerce," and therefore outside federal regulatory reach under the Constitution's commerce clause — gutting the law's practical use against trusts.
- Ironically **more effective against labor** in this period: courts and employers used the Act's broad "restraint of trade" language against striking unions instead of the trusts it was written for (see ***In re Debs*, 1895**, 6.13) — a textbook case of unintended consequences and a law being turned against the group it wasn't designed to target.
- Reinterpreted and strengthened during the Progressive Era ("trust-busting" under Theodore Roosevelt, the Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914) once courts read the commerce clause more broadly.

### The Money Question: Gold vs. Silver
- At the core of the era's biggest economic policy fight: whether U.S. currency should be backed only by gold (a fixed, limited supply) or by gold **and** silver ("**bimetallism**," which would expand the money supply and cause mild inflation).
- **Debtors** (farmers, especially — see 6.2) wanted "easy" money / inflation, because it would let them pay back fixed-dollar debts with cheaper dollars and raise crop prices; **creditors** (bankers, bondholders, industrialists) wanted the tight, stable gold standard, which protected the value of money they were owed.
- **Bland-Allison Act (1878)** required the Treasury to purchase a limited amount of silver monthly and coin it — a modest concession to silver interests that satisfied no one fully.
- **Sherman Silver Purchase Act (1890)** increased the required government silver purchases — but was blamed for helping trigger a run on the Treasury's gold reserves and was repealed in 1893 amid the Panic (see 6.13).
- Silver advocates popularized their case with pamphlets like William "Coin" Harvey's ***Coin's Financial School* (1894)**, arguing free silver coinage at a fixed ratio (16:1) would restore prosperity — the same "free silver" cause Bryan championed in 1896 (see 6.13).

### Political Issues

| Issue | Details |
|---|---|
| **Civil Service Reform** | Pendleton Act (1883) — required competitive exams for many federal jobs and prohibited forcing political donations from civil servants; passed directly in response to Garfield's assassination by a disgruntled office-seeker (see 6.13) |
| **Tariff** | High protective tariffs (raised further by the **McKinley Tariff, 1890**, then lowered modestly by the **Wilson-Gorman Tariff, 1894**) shielded American industry from foreign competition and benefited industrialists/industrial workers, but raised consumer prices and hurt farmers, who sold crops on an unprotected world market but had to buy manufactured goods at protected, inflated prices — another version of the era's basic industry-vs.-agriculture conflict |

> Big picture: 6.12's controversies — antitrust, currency, tariffs, civil service — are really all the same underlying question in different clothes: should the federal government actively referee industrial capitalism, or let it run largely unregulated? Every answer in this period (ICC, Sherman Act, Pendleton Act) was a tentative, often weak first step toward the much more assertive federal role of the Progressive Era and New Deal.

## 6.13 — Politics in the Gilded Age

### Political Stalemate
- Neither party addressed industrial/urban issues head-on; campaigns focused heavily on patronage, tariffs, and cultural/sectional identity rather than regulating big business or labor conditions.
- Republicans: northern base (Union veterans mobilized by the **Grand Army of the Republic**, Black voters where still enfranchised, industrialists, Protestants); Democrats: the South (post-Redemption "Solid South") + urban immigrant, Catholic voters, often organized through city machines (see 6.9).
- Presidential elections were extremely close (popular vote margins under 1% in several — 1880, 1884, 1888, 1892 all razor-thin); divided government (one party rarely held the White House and both houses of Congress for long) was the norm, which reinforced gridlock and made the **spoils system**/patronage — not policy — the main currency of politics.
- The seven Gilded Age presidents (**Hayes, Garfield, Arthur, Cleveland, Harrison, Cleveland again — the only president elected to non-consecutive terms — and McKinley**) are collectively remembered as forgettable and largely reactive precisely because the era's political structure rewarded caution and patronage management over bold policy.

### Grant-Era Scandals and the Push for Civil Service Reform
- **Crédit Mobilier scandal (1872):** railroad insiders overcharged the Union Pacific for construction and bribed congressmen (including future president Garfield) with discounted stock to avoid investigation — exposed the corrupt ties between government subsidies and the railroad industry.
- **Whiskey Ring (1875):** distillers bribed Treasury officials to avoid liquor taxes, defrauding the government of millions; implicated Grant's own private secretary.
- These scandals under President **Ulysses S. Grant** discredited his administration and fed a growing reform movement even before Garfield's death made reform unstoppable.
- **Garfield's assassination (1881)** by a disappointed, mentally unstable office-seeker shocked the public into action against the spoils system, directly producing the **Pendleton Act (1883)** (see 6.12).

### Rise of the Populists
- The **People's (Populist) Party** formed in 1892 out of the Farmers' Alliances (see 6.2), uniting indebted farmers — especially in the South, Great Plains, and West — with some labor support around a shared critique of banks, railroads, and the gold standard.
- **Omaha Platform (1892):**
  - Direct election of senators.
  - Initiatives and referendums.
  - Unlimited coinage of silver (expand money supply).
  - Graduated income tax.
  - Government ownership of railroads, telegraphs, telephones.
  - Loans and federal warehouses for farmers.
  - 8-hour workday.

### Key Political Events

| Event | Details |
|---|---|
| Election of 1892 | Populist **James B. Weaver** won over 1 million votes (~8.6% — the strongest third-party showing since the Civil War) and 22 electoral votes (carrying Kansas, Colorado, Idaho, Nevada); Cleveland defeated incumbent Harrison to reclaim the presidency |
| Panic of 1893 | Triggered by railroad overbuilding/bankruptcies, a run on the Treasury's gold reserves (worsened by the Sherman Silver Purchase Act, repealed in response), and British investors pulling capital out of American markets; produced the worst depression the U.S. had yet experienced, with unemployment reaching roughly 20% in some estimates — Cleveland's insistence on defending the gold standard through the crisis (including borrowing gold from J.P. Morgan's banking syndicate in 1895) split his own Democratic Party and fed directly into the free-silver insurgency of 1896 |
| **Coxey's Army (1894)** | Businessman **Jacob Coxey** led a march of several hundred unemployed workers from Ohio to Washington, D.C., demanding a federal public-works jobs program funded by paper money — an early, direct ancestor of later unemployment-relief demands; Coxey himself was arrested for walking on the Capitol lawn, and the march dispersed without winning any relief, but it put the question of federal responsibility for unemployment on the national stage for the first time |
| **Election of 1896** | William Jennings Bryan (Democrat, endorsed by the Populists) delivered the **"Cross of Gold" speech** at the Democratic convention (July 9, 1896), demanding free coinage of silver at 16:1 and closing with "you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold"; ran against **William McKinley** (Republican; gold standard, high tariffs, backed by industrialist Mark Hanna's fundraising machine); McKinley won → Republican dominance for a generation; triumph of urban/industrial interests over agrarian ones |

> Bryan's defeat ended the Populist Party as a major force. McKinley's presidency set the stage for U.S. emergence as a world power.

## 6.14 — Continuity and Change in Period 6

Period 6 is best understood by comparing it against **Period 5 (1844–1877)**, the era immediately before it — the Civil War and Reconstruction. Some Period 5 trends accelerated dramatically; others reversed outright; and some deep structural continuities persisted underneath all the surface transformation.

### Economy: Dramatic Change
- The U.S. shifted from a still-largely agrarian economy (even the industrializing North of the 1860s) to the **world's leading industrial power** by 1894 (see 6.1), with a fully **national market** connected by rail, telegraph, and standardized time (see 6.5–6.6).
- The scale and organizational form of business changed qualitatively, not just quantitatively: the small, owner-run firm of the antebellum era gave way to the modern **corporation** — vertically/horizontally integrated, professionally managed, financed by Wall Street — a genuinely new kind of economic institution (see 6.6).
- **Continuity underneath the change:** the underlying American commitment to private property, individual enterprise, and capitalism itself did not change — Period 6 intensified and industrialized the existing capitalist economy rather than replacing it with something else (contrast with European movements toward stronger state control or socialism in the same decades).

### Labor and Class: Change
- A new industrial **working class** — permanently wage-dependent, often immigrant, concentrated in factories and cities — emerged at a scale unknown before the war, alongside a new managerial **middle class** (see 6.10) and an unprecedented concentration of wealth at the top (see 6.6).
- Organized labor went from essentially nonexistent nationally to a recurring, sometimes militant force (National Labor Union, Knights of Labor, AFL — see 6.7), even though it kept losing most major confrontations with capital and the state through 1898.
- **Continuity:** the federal government's basic posture toward labor-capital conflict didn't change — from the 1877 Railroad Strike through the 1894 Pullman Strike, the state consistently sided with employers and property rights over strikers, continuing a pattern set even before the war.

### The West: Change
- The frontier line was declared closed by the 1890 census, and the federal government completed the conquest and forced assimilation of Plains nations (Little Bighorn to Wounded Knee, 1876–1890; Dawes Act, 1887) — a much more total subjugation than the more contested, ongoing conflicts of Period 5's earlier removal policies (see 6.3).
- The West shifted from a contested, only partially incorporated frontier to fully settled states integrated into the national economy via railroads, mining corporations, and commercial agriculture (see 6.2).

### Race Relations in the South: Change *and* Continuity — the Great Reversal
- **Sharpest reversal of the period:** Reconstruction (1865–1877) had briefly extended real political power to Black Southerners — the Fifteenth Amendment, Black officeholders, biracial state governments. The end of Reconstruction (Compromise of 1877, see 6.1) opened the way for Period 6 to systematically **dismantle** those gains: *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), Jim Crow, poll taxes, literacy tests, and lynching (see 6.4) built a segregation system in some ways more comprehensive and legally entrenched than anything under slavery-era law.
- **Continuity:** white supremacy as the organizing principle of Southern society never actually disappeared between the two periods — Reconstruction suppressed its *political* expression temporarily through federal enforcement, but Period 6 shows how quickly it reasserted itself once that enforcement was withdrawn. Economically, most Black Southerners moved from enslaved to sharecropping/tenant status — a change in legal form but a continuity in poverty, debt, and dependence on white landowners (see 6.4).

### Immigration: Change in Composition, Continuity in Hostility
- The *source* of immigration changed sharply — from a Period 5 immigration wave dominated by Irish and Germans to a Period 6 wave increasingly from Southern/Eastern Europe and Asia (see 6.8) — and the *scale* grew enormously alongside industrialization's demand for labor.
- **Continuity:** nativist hostility to immigrants was not new to Period 6 — anti-Catholic, anti-immigrant politics (the antebellum Know-Nothing movement) had already established the template; Period 6's American Protective Association and Chinese Exclusion Act (see 6.9) simply redirected the same nativist impulse at new, browner targets and, for the first time, wrote ethnic exclusion directly into federal law.

### The Role of Government: Contested Change
- The federal government took its **first tentative regulatory steps** into the economy — the Interstate Commerce Act (1887) and Sherman Antitrust Act (1890) — a real break from the pure laissez-faire norm, even though both were initially weak and poorly enforced (see 6.12).
- **Continuity:** **laissez-faire ideology** and a general belief in limited government remained dominant in practice throughout the period despite growing inequality and these early regulatory experiments — courts (*E.C. Knight*, freedom-of-contract rulings) repeatedly narrowed the new laws, and neither party pursued a fundamentally larger federal role. The real expansion of federal economic regulation was left to the Progressive Era and New Deal that followed.
- Political parties also remained closely and stubbornly balanced nationally (razor-thin elections, divided government — see 6.13) throughout Period 6, which is itself a continuity: it kept limiting major policy shifts right through the mounting industrial and agrarian discontent that peaked in the Populist revolt and the 1896 election.

### Reform and Ideas: Change, Setting Up the Next Period
- New intellectual and reform currents — Social Gospel, Reform Darwinism, settlement houses, the Populist and early labor platforms (see 6.6, 6.9, 6.11) — represented a genuinely new critique of unregulated industrial capitalism that hadn't existed in this form before the war.
- These movements mostly failed to win their immediate goals in Period 6 itself (Populism collapsed after 1896; antitrust and labor law stayed weak), but they supplied the ideas, personnel, and political language that the **Progressive Era (Period 7)** would draw on directly — Period 6 is best understood as the incubation period for reforms that only became law after 1900.

> Big picture for the CCOT essay: Period 6's central irony is that its most visible change — spectacular industrial growth — ran alongside a government that changed remarkably little in its basic laissez-faire commitments, and alongside a South where the *direction* of racial policy reversed sharply even as the underlying reality of white supremacy continued unbroken. Don't just list changes and continuities — explain *why* each one happened (or didn't) as a direct consequence of industrial capitalism's rise.
