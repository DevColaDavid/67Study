# Period 2 — Colonial America (1607–1754)

---

## 2.1 — Contextualizing Period 2

### From Contact to Colonization
- Period 1 established European claims and set the Columbian Exchange in motion; Period 2 covers permanent settlement and the maturation of colonial societies.
- **Shift in approach:** Spain's extraction-based conquest model gave way to England, France, and the Netherlands founding permanent settlements built on trade, agriculture, and (for England) family migration.
- **New financing tool:** Joint-stock companies (e.g., Virginia Company) spread the financial risk of colonization among investors, making sustained settlement economically viable.
- **Continuity:** Competition among European powers for territory, resources, and Native alliances continued from Period 1 and intensified as colonies grew.

### Labor Systems: A Spectrum
- Colonial labor ranged from **free wage labor** → **indentured servitude** (temporary, contract-based) → **chattel slavery** (permanent, hereditary, race-based).
- The Caribbean sugar economy (Barbados especially) pioneered large-scale African chattel slavery decades before it took root on the mainland; mainland planters borrowed the model once sugar-island profits proved it viable.
- **Big picture:** Period 2's central story is how English colonies moved from a labor system based on *class and contract* (indentured servitude) to one based on *race and birth* (hereditary slavery) — see 2.6.

## 2.2 — European Colonization

### General Motivations
- **Wealth** — agriculture, mining, fur trade.
- **Religion** — spread of Christianity.
- **Escape from Persecution** — religious and political refugees.

### Colonies by Power

| Power | Key Colonies / Notes |
|---|---|
| **Spanish** | Florida (St. Augustine, 1565 — *oldest European city in mainland US*), New Mexico (Santa Fe, 1610), California (missions, 1769) |
| **French** | Quebec (1608, Champlain), New Orleans (1718); focused on fur trade; relied on Native American alliances |
| **Dutch** | New Amsterdam (later NYC); Dutch West India Company; trade-focused |
| **British** | Diverse settlers; higher proportion of families; claimed Native lands → more conflict |

### Labor & Governance Systems by Power (comparison)

| Power | Labor System | Native Relations | Governance Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spanish** | **Encomienda**/**repartimiento** — forced Native labor tribute to Spanish landholders; later African slavery in Caribbean/coastal areas | Conquest & conversion; brutal labor demands sparked resistance (Pueblo Revolt, 2.5); *casta* system ranked people by racial mixture | Centralized, Crown- and Church-controlled (viceroys, missions, presidios) |
| **French** | Small settler population; relied on **coureurs de bois** ("runners of the woods") who lived among and traded with Native peoples | Alliance-based, not conquest-based — French needed Native partners for the fur trade; Jesuit missionaries sought conversion without displacement | Loose, trade-post model; few permanent farming settlements |
| **Dutch** | **Patroonships** — large land grants to wealthy investors ("patroons") who recruited tenant farmers, in exchange for bringing settlers | Traded firearms to the Iroquois for furs; generally pragmatic/commercial rather than religious | Dutch West India Company-run; New Netherland (founded 1614/1624) captured by England in 1664, renamed New York |
| **British** | Headright system → indentured servitude → hereditary chattel slavery (see 2.6) | Displacement — English colonists farmed the land themselves and pushed Native peoples out rather than incorporating them | Decentralized; colonial assemblies, charters, and (relatively) more local self-rule |

> [!note] The "Middle Ground"
> Historian Richard White's concept of the **Middle Ground** describes the Great Lakes borderland where French traders and Native nations, neither able to dominate the other, developed a shared world of mutual accommodation — intermarriage, gift diplomacy, and blended customs. It contrasts sharply with the British pattern of displacement and the Spanish pattern of forced labor/conversion.

## 2.3 — The Regions of British Colonies: New England, the Middle Colonies, and the South

### Charter Types
- **Corporate colonies** (e.g., Jamestown) — managed by joint-stock companies.
- **Royal colonies** (e.g., Virginia post-1624) — ruled directly by the Crown.
- **Proprietary colonies** (e.g., Maryland, Pennsylvania) — granted to individuals.

### Jamestown: Founding and Early Crisis
- **1607:** Virginia Company colonists land at Jamestown — first permanent English settlement in North America. Site chosen for defensibility, not farmland; swampy location bred disease (malaria, dysentery, typhoid).
- Early colonists (many were gentlemen unaccustomed to labor) nearly starved; **Captain John Smith** imposed discipline ("he who does not work shall not eat") and traded with the **Powhatan Confederacy** for food.
- **The "Starving Time" (winter 1609–1610):** After Smith returned to England injured, the population collapsed from roughly 500 to about 60 survivors amid famine and conflict with the Powhatan; colonists reportedly resorted to eating shoe leather and, in extreme cases, cannibalism.
- **Tobacco (John Rolfe, ~1612):** Rolfe cultivated a marketable strain of tobacco, giving Virginia a profitable cash crop and securing the colony's economic survival — but it demanded intensive land and labor, driving both westward expansion onto Native land and the demand for a captive workforce.
- **Anglo-Powhatan Wars:**
  - *First (1610–1614):* Ended with the marriage of Rolfe and **Pocahontas** (daughter of Chief Powhatan), bringing a temporary peace.
  - *Second (1622):* Chief **Opechancanough** led a surprise attack killing roughly a quarter of the colony's English population; the Virginia Company's charter was revoked in 1624 and Virginia became a **royal colony**.
  - *Third (1644–1646):* Ended with a treaty forcing the Powhatan to cede land and acknowledge English authority, effectively breaking Powhatan power in the Tidewater.

### New England Colonies

| Colony | Founded | Key Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Plymouth** | 1620 | Pilgrims (Separatists); Mayflower Compact; fishing/fur/lumber |
| **Massachusetts Bay** | 1630 | Puritans; John Winthrop; "Great Migration" (~15,000 settlers); self-governance |
| **Rhode Island** | 1636 | Roger Williams & Anne Hutchinson (expelled from MA); religious tolerance; church/state separation |
| **Connecticut** | 1636 | Thomas Hooker; *Fundamental Orders* — one of the first written constitutions |

### Puritan New England: Belief, Governance, and Dissent
- **Puritans vs. Separatists:** Puritans wanted to "purify" (reform) the Church of England from within; **Separatists** (the Plymouth Pilgrims) believed the Church was beyond saving and broke away entirely.
- **John Winthrop's "City upon a Hill" (1630):** In his sermon *A Model of Christian Charity*, delivered aboard the *Arbella*, Winthrop declared Massachusetts Bay would be a model Christian society watched by the world — an early articulation of American **exceptionalism**.
- **Congregationalism:** Each Puritan church was self-governing (no bishops); only "visible saints" (those who could testify to a conversion experience) could be full church members and, in Massachusetts Bay, vote in colony elections.
- **Antinomian Controversy (1636–1638):** **Anne Hutchinson** held Bible study meetings arguing that salvation came through faith and God's grace alone, not through ministers' authority or good works — a direct challenge to Puritan clergy. Tried and banished, she fled to Rhode Island with followers.
- **Halfway Covenant (1662):** As fewer second- and third-generation colonists could testify to a conversion experience, this compromise allowed partial church membership (baptism, but not communion) for their children — a sign of declining religious intensity over time.
- **Pequot War (1636–1638):** Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, and Connecticut colonists, aided by Narragansett and Mohegan allies, nearly annihilated the Pequot people (over 700 killed or captured, including a massacre at Mystic Fort) — secured English control of the Connecticut River Valley and demonstrated the destructive potential of English-Native conflict decades before Metacom's War.

### Middle Colonies

| Colony | Founded | Key Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **New York** | 1664 | Originally Dutch New Amsterdam; diverse population; major trade hub |
| **New Jersey** | 1664 | Split from NY; unified as royal colony 1702; religious freedom |
| **Pennsylvania** | — | William Penn; **"The Holy Experiment"**; Quaker ideals; *Frame of Government* (1682–83); *Charter of Liberties* (1701); Philadelphia planned on grid |
| **Delaware** | 1704 | Separated from Pennsylvania |

### Southern Colonies

| Colony | Founded | Key Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Maryland** | 1634 | Lord Baltimore (Catholics); *Act of Toleration* — religious freedom to all Christians |
| **Virginia** | 1607 | First permanent English colony (Jamestown); tobacco (John Rolfe); **headright system**; Virginia Company → royal colony |
| **The Carolinas** | 1663 | Split into North (small farms) & South (plantations) in 1729 |
| **Georgia** | 1732 | James Oglethorpe; buffer colony vs. Spanish Florida; social experiment for debtors; initially banned slavery; → royal colony 1752 |

- **Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina (1669):** Drafted with input from philosopher **John Locke**, it envisioned a quasi-feudal aristocracy with hereditary nobility — mostly failed in practice, but South Carolina's rice-and-slavery plantation economy (settlers came directly from Barbados, bringing Caribbean-style slave codes) developed exactly the harsh, hierarchical society the document anticipated.
- **Georgia's Trustee period:** Oglethorpe's original charter banned both slavery and hard liquor to encourage a colony of industrious small farmers/debtors; both bans proved unenforceable and were lifted by 1750, and Georgia converted to a conventional plantation slave society soon after becoming a royal colony (1752).

### Chesapeake vs. New England: Demographics and Society
- **Chesapeake (Virginia, Maryland):** Skewed gender ratio (roughly 6 men to 1 woman in the early 1600s); disease-ridden environment (malaria, dysentery) meant short life expectancy (often into the 40s at best); high mortality and imbalanced sex ratios discouraged family formation and produced weaker, less stable communities dependent on continual new immigration (first indentured servants, later enslaved Africans) to sustain the labor force.
- **New England:** Healthier climate, clean water, and a more balanced gender ratio meant colonists lived longer (often into their 60s–70s) and reproduced rapidly; New England's population grew mainly through natural increase rather than immigration, producing tight-knit, multigenerational towns organized around the church and family farm.
- **Why it matters (cause and effect):** New England's stable, longer-lived families built durable communal institutions (town meetings, congregational churches); the Chesapeake's unstable, short-lived, male-heavy population relied ever more heavily on coerced labor, which is part of why the shift to slavery took root there first and most completely.
- *New Haven Colony* (founded 1638 by Puritans seeking stricter religious governance than Massachusetts Bay) was absorbed into Connecticut in the 1660s.

### Early Political Institutions

- **Virginia House of Burgesses (1619)** — First representative assembly in America.
- **Mayflower Compact (1620)** — Early form of self-government via majority rule.
- **Town Meetings** — Local governance in New England; voted for governor and assembly.
- **Limits:** Women, landless men, indentured servants, enslaved people excluded from politics.

## 2.4 — The Eighteenth-Century Atlantic Economy

### Triangular Trade
- **New England → West Africa:** Rum
- **West Africa → West Indies:** Enslaved Africans
- **West Indies → New England:** Sugarcane → more rum
- African elites and rival African states played a key role in supplying captives, often taken as prisoners of war or through raiding — the trade could not have operated at scale without African participation on the supply side.
- This was only one route among many; ships also sailed directly between Africa, the Caribbean, and Britain. The **Middle Passage** — the Atlantic crossing of enslaved Africans — killed an estimated 15–20% of captives aboard ship from disease, dehydration, and brutal conditions (see 2.6).

### Mercantilism & Navigation Acts
- **Mercantilism:** Promote exports over imports; colonies supply raw materials, serve as markets; a nation's wealth measured by its stock of gold/silver (bullion).
- **Navigation Acts** (beginning 1651, expanded through the 1660s–70s): Required English (and colonial-built) ships and crews; certain "enumerated goods" (tobacco, sugar) had to be shipped through English ports even if bound elsewhere. Boosted colonial shipbuilding (New England benefited) but restricted colonial manufacturing and trading partners.
- **Molasses Act (1733):** Placed a heavy duty on molasses imported from non-British Caribbean colonies (mainly French) to protect British West Indies sugar planters. Widely evaded through **smuggling** and bribery of customs officials — Parliament's later attempt to actually enforce it (the Sugar Act, 1764) would help spark revolutionary resistance.

### Effects
- Constrained colonial economies; limited trading partners and prices.
- **Smuggling** and economic disobedience → strained relations with England; laid the groundwork for a colonial culture comfortable with evading British trade law.
- **Consumer Revolution / "Anglicization":** Rising colonial prosperity fueled growing demand for British manufactured goods — tea, ceramics, textiles, furniture. Paradoxically, colonists became *more* culturally and economically tied to Britain even as their political institutions grew more independent (a tension that snapped after 1763).
- **Wars for Empire:** Colonial development was repeatedly interrupted by European conflicts that spilled into North America — **King William's War** (1689–1697), **Queen Anne's War** (1702–1713, ended by the Treaty of Utrecht, which gave Britain Acadia, Newfoundland, and the asiento slave-trading contract), and **King George's War** (1744–1748). These conflicts pulled colonial militias into fighting alongside British regulars against France and Spain, previewing the alliance patterns and grievances of the French and Indian War (Period 3).

### Dominion of New England (1686–1688)
- Created by King James II; single governor over several colonies.
- Governor Sir Edmund Andros: taxed without consent, restricted town meetings.
- Dissolved after the **Glorious Revolution (1688)**.

### Salutary Neglect
- British policy of loosely enforcing parliamentary laws → colonies grew accustomed to autonomy.
- When Britain later tightened control, colonial resentment ignited → **American Revolution**.

## 2.5 — Interactions Between American Indians and Europeans

### Patterns of Native-European Diplomacy
- Native nations were not passive victims of colonization — they actively shaped it, playing rival European powers against each other for trade goods, weapons, and military support.
- **Covenant Chain (from the 1670s):** A durable alliance between the **Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee)** and the British colony of New York. The Iroquois used their position between French Canada and English colonies to extract trade concessions and maintain a degree of independence — sometimes called a "play-off system."
- **Praying Towns:** Missionary **John Eliot** established towns of Christian-converted "**Praying Indians**" in Massachusetts Bay who adopted English dress, farming, and worship. Distrusted by *both* English colonists (who doubted their loyalty) and unconverted Native peoples (who saw them as having abandoned tradition) — many Praying Indians were forcibly relocated to internment camps during Metacom's War despite their neutrality or English allegiance.
- Compare to the **Middle Ground** (2.2) in French territory, where accommodation rather than displacement or one-sided conversion was the norm — a key contrast the AP exam frequently tests.

### New England Confederation (1643–1684)
- Formed by Plymouth, Massachusetts Bay, Connecticut, New Haven — mutual protection against Native tribes, Dutch, and French.

### Metacom's War (King Philip's War, 1675–1676)
- Wampanoag chief **Metacom** ("King Philip") resisted English encroachments.
- Devastating on both sides → effectively ended major Native resistance in New England.

### Yamasee War (1715–1717) — South Carolina
- A coalition of the **Yamasee**, **Creek**, and other Native nations — angered by abusive trade practices and colonists' enslavement of Native people — nearly destroyed the South Carolina colony, killing a large share of its white population.
- **Consequences:** The devastating losses to South Carolina's Native slave trade pushed planters to rely almost entirely on *African* chattel slavery going forward, accelerating South Carolina's transformation into a slave-majority colony (see 2.6).

### Walking Purchase (1737) — Pennsylvania
- Pennsylvania officials fraudulently enforced an old (possibly forged) deed to claim far more Lenape (Delaware) land than intended, using relay runners to mark a boundary "as far as a man could walk in a day and a half."
- Shattered William Penn's earlier reputation for fair dealing with Native peoples and pushed the Lenape toward alliance with the French by the 1750s.

### Bacon's Rebellion (1676) — Virginia
- Governor William Berkeley accused of ignoring frontier settlers' safety.
- **Nathaniel Bacon** led rebellion against Native villages and Berkeley.
- **Long-term Impact:**
  - Showed class disparity and resistance to royal authority.
  - Revealed instability of indentured servitude → **planters shifted toward slavery**.

### Pueblo Revolt (1680) — Spanish Southwest
- Unified Pueblo uprising expelled Spanish for over a decade.
- Post-revolt: Spanish adopted less harsh governance.

## 2.6 — Slavery in the British Colonies

### Shift from Indentured Servitude to Slavery
- Initially, Virginia relied on **indentured servants** (headright system: 50 acres for passage).
- Bacon's Rebellion showed instability of indentured servitude.
- By late 17th century, planters favored the permanent, hereditary labor of slavery.

### Institutionalization of Slavery
- 1619: The first documented Africans arrived in Virginia (aboard a Dutch ship) with a status closer to indentured servants than lifelong slaves — their legal status was initially ambiguous under English law.
- **1662 (Virginia):** The colony codified **partus sequitur ventrem** ("that which is born follows the womb") — a child's status (free or enslaved) followed the *mother's*, not the father's. This broke from English common-law tradition (which traced status through the father) and made slavery self-perpetuating and hereditary, regardless of who fathered a child.
- By the **1660s–1680s**: A growing body of Virginia and Maryland statutes defined enslaved Africans and their descendants as **property for life** (chattel), stripped away earlier ambiguities, and closed loopholes.
- Christian baptism no longer granted protective status or a path to freedom (a deliberate legal change, since many colonists had feared conversion would force emancipation).
- **Virginia Slave Code of 1705:** Consolidated decades of piecemeal law into one comprehensive code — defined enslaved people explicitly as **real property**, denied them the right to own property, testify against whites in court, or assemble, and authorized harsh corporal punishment. Became a model other Southern colonies (like South Carolina, 1740) copied and expanded.

### Historiographical Debate: Racism or Economics First?
> [!note] What Caused Racial Slavery?
> Historians have long debated *why* Virginia moved from a labor force of white indentured servants and a small number of Africans (some free) toward a *racially exclusive* system of hereditary slavery. Historian **Edmund Morgan** (*American Slavery, American Freedom*) argued that Virginia's elite, rattled by the class tensions revealed in **Bacon's Rebellion** (2.5) — where poor white and Black laborers had briefly fought side by side — deliberately used race to divide the lower classes: granting poor whites new legal privileges and a shared racial identity with elites, while permanently degrading enslaved Black Virginians. In this view, racism hardened as a *tool of social control* alongside (not before) the economic turn to slave labor.

### Economic Impact
- Triangular trade and cash crops (tobacco, rice, indigo) dependent on enslaved labor.
- **The Middle Passage:** Of an estimated 12.5 million Africans forced onto Atlantic slave ships (1500s–1800s), roughly 15–20% died during the crossing from disease, dehydration, and brutal overcrowding — among the deadliest sustained atrocities in human history.
- **Labor systems differed by crop/region:**
  - **Gang labor** (Chesapeake tobacco): Enslaved workers labored in supervised groups from dawn to dusk under an overseer's direct control.
  - **Task system** (South Carolina/Georgia Lowcountry rice): Enslaved workers were assigned a specific daily task; once completed, remaining time was their own. This gave Lowcountry enslaved communities — which had a Black population *majority* by the 1720s — more autonomy to maintain family life, grow their own food, and preserve African cultural and linguistic traditions (seen in **Gullah/Geechee** culture).

### Slave Resistance
- Work slowdowns, sabotage, escape, outright rebellion.
- **Stono Rebellion (1739, South Carolina):** The largest slave uprising in the British mainland colonies. Roughly 20 enslaved people, some recently arrived and possibly military-trained in Africa, seized weapons and marched south toward Spanish Florida (which had offered freedom to English colonies' escaped slaves at Fort Mose). The uprising killed more than 20 colonists before the militia suppressed it.
  - **Consequence:** South Carolina passed the **Negro Act of 1740**, a harsher slave code restricting enslaved people's movement, assembly, education (literacy), and earning of money — and temporarily slowed the colony's rate of enslaved importation.
- **Cultural retention**: music, dance, storytelling, religious practices — a foundation of African American culture that persisted and evolved under slavery.

## 2.7 — Colonial Society and Culture

### Economy by Region

| Region | Economic Activity |
|---|---|
| **New England** | Subsistence farming, logging, shipbuilding, fishing, rum-distilling |
| **Middle Colonies** | Fertile lands (wheat, corn), iron industry, cities (Philadelphia, NYC) |
| **Southern Colonies** | Plantations (tobacco, timber, rice, indigo); some subsistence farming |

### Religious and Cultural Dynamics
- **Religious Diversity:** Strict Puritan norms in New England; inclusive in Rhode Island & Pennsylvania.
- **Social Structure:** No hereditary aristocracy; more social mobility than Europe; growing middle class.

### The Great Awakening
- Religious revival (roughly 1730s–1740s) emphasizing personal salvation, emotional conversion experiences, and a direct relationship with God over formal ritual and educated clergy.
- **Leaders:**
  - **Jonathan Edwards** — Massachusetts Congregationalist minister; preached the famous fire-and-brimstone sermon **"Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God" (1741)**, vividly warning of eternal damnation.
  - **George Whitefield** — English itinerant (traveling) preacher whose emotional open-air sermons drew enormous crowds up and down the colonies; his multi-colony preaching tours made the Great Awakening the first major shared cultural experience across all thirteen colonies.
- **New Light vs. Old Light:** The revival split Congregationalist and Presbyterian churches into pro-revival "**New Light**"/"New Side" factions and traditionalist "**Old Light**"/"Old Side" factions that rejected the emotional, itinerant style.
- **New Light Presbyterians founded the College of New Jersey (1746)** — later **Princeton** — specifically to train revival-minded ministers, illustrating how the Awakening reshaped colonial higher education.
- **Impact:** Fragmented denominations; rise of religious self-determination and distrust of traditional authority (since ordinary people could judge their own salvation without deferring to elite clergy) → mirrored, and helped normalize, the questioning of political authority that fueled the Revolution a generation later.

### Education

| Region | Approach |
|---|---|
| New England | Tax-supported schools (1647 MA law); Puritan literacy emphasis |
| Middle | Church-sponsored & private schools |
| South | Home tutors; no formal system |

**Higher Education:**
- Harvard (1636) — Puritans; Yale (1701) — Congregationalists; William & Mary (1694) — Anglicans.
- **College of Philadelphia (1765)** — Benjamin Franklin; first major *non-sectarian* college.

### The Press & Zenger Case (1735)
- John Peter Zenger acquitted of libel — established that **truth is a defense against libel**.
- Precedent for freedom of the press; encouraged scrutiny of government.

### The Enlightenment
- **John Locke:** Governance based on inherent human rights (life, liberty, property); sovereignty resides with the people; government exists by a **social contract** and can be overthrown if it violates natural rights.
- **Deism:** An Enlightenment-influenced belief, popular among educated elites, that a rational, non-interventionist God created the universe to run by natural law (like a "clockmaker") rather than through miracles or direct involvement — appealed to figures like **Benjamin Franklin** and, later, Thomas Jefferson.
- **Benjamin Franklin** as the era's model of Enlightenment pragmatism and social mobility: founded the **Junto** (1727, a self-improvement/debate club), published *Poor Richard's Almanack* (1732, practical wisdom and proverbs), and founded the **American Philosophical Society** (1743) to promote scientific inquiry — embodying the Enlightenment ideals of reason, self-improvement, and civic usefulness.
- Fueled revolutionary ideas → U.S. Constitution.
- **Tension worth noting:** The Great Awakening (emotional, faith-driven, egalitarian in spirit) and the Enlightenment (rational, secular, elite-driven) pulled colonial culture in different directions, but *both* independently eroded deference to traditional authority — a shared root of revolutionary thinking.

## 2.8 — Comparison in Period 2

### Comparing the British Colonial Regions

| Region | Economy | Society |
|---|---|---|
| **New England** | Subsistence farming, fishing, shipbuilding | Tight-knit Puritan towns; high literacy; small farms |
| **Middle Colonies** | Grain export ("breadbasket"); iron | Most diverse — ethnically and religiously; growing port cities |
| **South** | Cash-crop plantations (tobacco, rice, indigo) | Hierarchical; heavy reliance on enslaved labor; dispersed settlement |

### Comparing British, Spanish, French, and Dutch Colonization
- **Spanish:** Conquest and extraction (encomienda, mining); rigid *casta* system; Catholic missions and conversion as a central goal.
- **French:** Small population; fur trade; alliances (not conquest) with Native nations; the **Middle Ground** model of mutual accommodation.
- **Dutch:** Commercially driven (Dutch West India Company); patroonship land grants; relatively tolerant, diverse, trade-focused New Netherland — absorbed by England in 1664.
- **British:** Largest settler population; family migration; displaced rather than absorbed Native peoples; greatest degree of self-government (assemblies, town meetings).

> **Key Comparison:** Britain's colonies grew fastest because family-based settlement and self-governing institutions created more durable, self-sustaining societies than Spain's extraction model, France's trade-based outposts, or the Dutch commercial colony. Population figures make the point starkly: by 1700, English colonies had roughly 250,000 settlers versus a much smaller French and Dutch colonial presence — a demographic imbalance rooted directly in the family-migration pattern discussed above.

### Continuity and Change Across Period 2
- **Labor:** Indentured servitude (early 1600s) → hereditary racial slavery (by early 1700s) — driven by Bacon's Rebellion, the growing supply of enslaved Africans via the Atlantic trade, and planters' desire for a permanently controllable labor force (2.5, 2.6).
- **Native relations:** Initial dependence/cooperation (Powhatan aiding Jamestown, Wampanoag aiding Plymouth) → escalating warfare and displacement as English population and land hunger grew (Pequot War → Metacom's War → Yamasee War).
- **Colonial autonomy:** Early "salutary neglect" and locally elected assemblies fostered a habit of self-government that colonists came to see as a right — setting up the conflicts of Period 3 when Britain tried to reassert control after 1763.
