# Period 3 — American Revolution (1754–1800)

---

## 3.1 — Contextualizing Period 3

### From Colonial Growth to Revolution
- By the mid-1700s, colonies had matured into distinct, largely self-governing societies used to managing their own affairs under **salutary neglect**.
- Population boomed (mostly natural increase) and colonial economies grew increasingly tied to — but also increasingly resentful of — British mercantilist restrictions.
- **Enlightenment ideas** (natural rights, social contract, consent of the governed) circulated widely through print culture, priming colonists to question British authority.
- The **Seven Years' War** would end salutary neglect and trigger the taxation disputes that led directly to revolution.

---

## 3.2 — The Seven Years' War (The French and Indian War)

### Wars Leading Up to It

| War | Dates | Key Events |
|---|---|---|
| King William's War | 1689–1697 | British failed to seize Quebec |
| Queen Anne's War | 1702–1713 | Britain gained Nova Scotia, trading rights in Spanish America |
| King George's War | 1744–1748 | British captured Louisbourg (later returned) |

### The Seven Years' War (1754–1763)
- **Cause:** French built forts in the Ohio River Valley (e.g., Fort Duquesne) to link Canada with the Mississippi Valley, threatening British and colonial land claims/speculation (Ohio Company).
- **Opening Shots:** A young **George Washington**, sent to expel the French, was defeated at **Fort Necessity (July 1754)** — widely seen as the war's opening engagement.
- **Initial Setbacks:** General **Edward Braddock's** failed 1755 expedition against Fort Duquesne; Braddock killed, his army routed by French/Native forces.
- **Turn:** Prime Minister **William Pitt** shifted focus to Canada and **reimbursed colonial assemblies** for military expenses (won greater colonial cooperation) → key victories at Louisbourg, Quebec (1759, Gen. Wolfe), Montreal (1760).
- **Native Alignment:** Most Native nations (including the **Iroquois Confederacy**, tied to Britain via the **Covenant Chain**) sided with or leaned toward France, fearing British land hunger; the **Treaty of Easton (1758)** temporarily won some Ohio Valley nations' neutrality by promising to limit settlement.

### Albany Plan of Union (1754)
- Ben Franklin's proposal for unified colonial government and defense.
- **Failed** (colonies unwilling to give up tax control) but **precedent** for future revolutionary congresses.

### Treaty of Paris (1763)
- Britain gained **French Canada** and **Spanish Florida**; France ceded Louisiana to Spain.
- Britain emerged as the dominant European power in North America.

### Aftermath & Tensions

| Issue | Details |
|---|---|
| **End of Salutary Neglect** | Britain shifted to direct, stringent control |
| **Pontiac's Rebellion (1763)** | Multi-tribal coalition led by Ottawa chief **Pontiac**, inspired partly by the Delaware prophet **Neolin's** call to reject European goods/culture; besieged British forts across the Great Lakes/Ohio Valley; Britain sent regular troops → convinced London a firm frontier policy was needed |
| **Proclamation of 1763** | Banned colonial settlement west of the Appalachians → widespread colonial anger; land speculators (including Washington) and settlers already moving west largely ignored it |

## 3.3 — Taxation Without Representation

### Key Acts & Colonial Responses

| Act | Year | Key Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Proclamation of 1763** | 1763 | Banned settlement west of Appalachians |
| **Sugar Act** | 1764 | Duties on foreign sugars; admiralty courts (no juries) |
| **Quartering Act** | 1765 | Colonies must house/supply British troops |
| **Stamp Act** | 1765 | First *direct* tax; stamps on all printed materials → **Stamp Act Congress** |
| **Declaratory Act** | 1766 | Parliament has authority to legislate for colonies in ALL cases |
| **Townshend Acts** | 1767 | Named for Chancellor of the Exchequer **Charles Townshend**; duties on tea, glass, paper, lead, paint; Writs of Assistance (general search warrants) |

### Colonial Unity & Protest
- **Stamp Act Congress (1765):** Delegates from 9 colonies declared only elected representatives could levy taxes.
- **Sons & Daughters of Liberty:** Formed to oppose Stamp Act enforcement; Sons of Liberty used mob action, tarring-and-feathering, and intimidation of stamp distributors, forcing most to resign before the act took effect.
- **"No Taxation Without Representation":** Colonists rejected Parliament's claim of **"virtual representation"** (that Parliament represented all British subjects' interests even without direct election) — colonists argued only **"actual representation"** (representatives elected by and accountable to a specific constituency) was legitimate.
- **John Dickinson, *Letters from a Pennsylvania Farmer* (1767–68):** Widely read essays arguing Parliament could regulate trade but not tax colonies for revenue — helped organize opposition to the Townshend Acts.
- **Massachusetts Circular Letter (1768):** Samuel Adams-authored letter urging colonies to resist Townshend Acts jointly; Britain ordered it rescinded, further inflaming tensions.
- **Boycotts / Non-Importation Agreements** of British goods → economic pressure on Britain; **Daughters of Liberty** promoted "homespun" cloth so colonists could avoid British textiles.

### Crisis Deepens

| Event | Year | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Boston Massacre** | 1770 | British troops fired on crowd; 5 killed (incl. Crispus Attucks); Paul Revere's engraving = propaganda |
| **Committees of Correspondence** | 1772 | Samuel Adams; network of colonial opposition; spread to Virginia |
| **Gaspee Incident** | 1772 | Colonists attacked British customs ship; Britain tried suspects in England → increased grievances |
| **Boston Tea Party** | 1773 | Tea Act retained tax; colonists (disguised as Native Americans) dumped 342 chests of tea |
| **Intolerable Acts** | 1774 | Port Act, MA Government Act, Justice Act, expanded Quartering Act, Quebec Act |

## 3.4 — Philosophical Foundations of the American Revolution

### Enlightenment Influence
- **Deism:** God creates natural laws but does not intervene in human affairs.
- **Rationalism:** Human reason can solve societal problems.
- **John Locke, *Two Treatises of Government* (1689):** Natural rights to **life, liberty, and property**; legitimate government rests on the **consent of the governed**; people retain the right to **revolt** against a government that violates the social contract — the direct philosophical basis for Jefferson's Declaration.
- **Baron de Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748):** Advocated **separation of powers** among branches of government to prevent tyranny — directly shaped the later Constitution's structure.
- **Social Contract Theory (Locke, Rousseau):** Governments formed by compact among the people to ensure liberty; challenged divine right of monarchs.
- **Radical/Real Whig ("Country") ideology:** Inherited from the English Civil War and Glorious Revolution (1688) — deep suspicion of **standing armies**, concentrated power, and government "corruption"; colonists interpreted British taxation and troop deployments through this lens as evidence of a conspiracy against their liberty.

### Thomas Paine's *Common Sense* (January 1776)
- Argued it was irrational for a vast continent ("America") to be ruled by a distant island and a corrupt, hereditary monarch — mocked the very idea of monarchy as illogical.
- Directly attacked **King George III** and monarchy itself; written in plain, accessible language rather than formal political-philosophy prose, aimed at ordinary colonists, not just the elite.
- Sold an estimated **500,000 copies** in its first year — extraordinary reach for the era — and is credited with rapidly shifting public opinion from seeking reconciliation to actively supporting independence.

## 3.5 — The American Revolution

### First Continental Congress (September 1774)
- Convened in Philadelphia to address Intolerable Acts.
- **Actions:** Suffolk Resolves (repeal Intolerable Acts, military prep), Continental Association (enforce boycotts), set to reconvene May 1775 if demands unmet.

### Outbreak of Hostilities

| Event | Date | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Lexington & Concord** | April 18, 1775 | British sought colonial arms; colonial militias forced British retreat |
| **Battle of Bunker Hill** | June 17, 1775 | Technically British victory; showed colonial capacity to inflict heavy casualties |

### Second Continental Congress (May 1775)
- Appointed **George Washington** commander-in-chief.
- Sent **Olive Branch Petition** to King George III → rejected (Prohibitory Act, Aug 1775).

### Declaration of Independence
- Richard Henry Lee's resolution (June 7, 1776) → **Thomas Jefferson** drafted it.
- **Adopted July 4, 1776** — enshrined Enlightenment principles of individual rights.

### Revolutionary War (1775–1783)

**Allegiances:**
- **Patriots (~40%)** — supported independence.
- **Loyalists/Tories (~15–20%)** — supported Britain; strongest among royal officials, Anglican clergy, and some merchants/backcountry farmers dependent on British trade or protection; an estimated **60,000–80,000 Loyalists fled** the new United States during and after the war (many to Canada, the Caribbean, or Britain), and Patriot state governments confiscated Loyalist estates.
- **African Americans (~5,000)** — served in Patriot forces (Rhode Island raised a Black regiment); far more (tens of thousands) sought freedom by fleeing to the British after **Lord Dunmore's Proclamation (1775)** and, later, the **Philipsburg Proclamation (1779)** offered freedom to enslaved people who left rebel masters.
- **American Indians** — mostly sided with Britain (promised restricted colonial expansion); the **Iroquois Confederacy split** — the Oneida and Tuscarora allied with the Patriots while the rest of the Six Nations backed Britain, fracturing the century-old Covenant Chain alliance.
- **Women** — served as camp followers, nurses, and cooks; some (e.g., **Deborah Sampson**, who disguised herself as a man to enlist; the legendary **"Molly Pitcher"**) served in or near combat roles; **Abigail Adams** privately urged husband John to **"remember the ladies"** in the new laws, with little practical effect.
- **Foreign volunteers/aid:** The **Marquis de Lafayette** (France) and **Baron von Steuben** (Prussia) were key to training and leading Continental forces; Britain hired **German ("Hessian") mercenaries** to supplement its army.

**Key Turning Points:**

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Trenton & Princeton** | Dec 1776–Jan 1777 | Washington's surprise crossing of the Delaware and victories revived flagging Patriot morale after a string of losses around New York |
| **Battle of Saratoga** | Oct 1777 | American victory → secured **French alliance (1778)**, then Spain & Holland; widely seen as the war's turning point |
| **Valley Forge** | Winter 1777–78 | Symbol of Patriot suffering and resilience; **Baron von Steuben** drilled the Continental Army into a more disciplined, professional force |
| **Southern Campaign** | 1778–1781 | British shifted strategy south (hoping for Loyalist support); Patriot losses at Charleston/Camden followed by American victories at **Kings Mountain (1780)** and **Cowpens (1781)**; **Nathanael Greene's** war of attrition wore down Cornwallis's army |
| **Battle of Yorktown** | 1781 | Final major battle; French navy (Comte de Grasse) cut off British escape by sea; Cornwallis surrendered |

### Treaty of Paris (1783)
- Britain recognized U.S. independence.
- Mississippi River = western boundary.
- Americans gained fishing rights off Canada.
- Americans agreed to pay pre-war debts and compensate Loyalists.

## 3.6 — The Influence of Revolutionary Ideals

> [!note] Historiographical Debate: "How Revolutionary Was the Revolution?"
> Historians disagree on how much the Revolution actually changed American society. **Gordon Wood** (*The Radicalism of the American Revolution*) argues it radically transformed social relationships and political culture — destroying deference to aristocracy and unleashing genuinely democratic, egalitarian expectations. More skeptical historians emphasize **continuity**: property qualifications for voting mostly persisted, slavery expanded, women gained no formal political rights, and Native Americans lost more land than before. The AP exam frequently tests this tension — be ready to argue **both** that the Revolution was transformative (political ideology, legitimacy of government) **and** limited (social/economic structures, who actually gained rights).

### Women
- **Daughters of Liberty** — boycotts and wartime support.
- **Republican Motherhood** — women educated to instill republican values in children by raising virtuous future (male) citizens; framed civic duty as compatible with the domestic sphere rather than direct political participation — indirect political influence, not public equality.
- Legal status of women (**coverture** — a married woman's legal identity subsumed under her husband's) was **not** altered by the Revolution; most states did not extend suffrage to women (New Jersey briefly allowed some property-owning women to vote 1776–1807, then revoked it).

### Enslaved African Americans
- Slavery directly contradicted revolutionary principles of natural rights and liberty — abolitionists on both sides of the Atlantic invoked Revolutionary rhetoric against it.
- **Northern gradual abolition:** Pennsylvania passed the first **gradual abolition law (1780)**; Massachusetts effectively ended slavery via judicial ruling (**Quock Walker case, 1783**), interpreting its state constitution's "all men are born free and equal" clause; other northern states followed with gradual emancipation laws over subsequent decades.
- The **Pennsylvania Abolition Society (1775)**, the first American abolition society, and growing free Black communities in northern cities reflected the Revolution's ideological opening — but abolition remained regional, gradual, and did not touch the South.
- The **Constitution protected slavery** (Three-Fifths Clause, fugitive slave clause, 1808 slave-trade protection) even as northern states moved away from it — a sharp regional divergence that hardened over time.
- **Cotton gin (1793)**, invented by **Eli Whitney**, made short-staple cotton profitable to process → revitalized and expanded demand for enslaved labor into the Deep South and West → undermined revolutionary ideals rather than fulfilling them.

### Native Americans
- Largely supported the British (who had limited colonial expansion) or remained divided; suffered greatly after American victory — the U.S. treated Native nations allied with Britain as a conquered people rather than as sovereign treaty partners.
- **Treaty of Fort Stanwix (1784)** forced Iroquois nations to cede vast western New York/Pennsylvania lands as a "conquered" people, despite the Oneida and Tuscarora having fought alongside the Patriots — illustrating how little Revolutionary ideals of consent extended to Native nations.
- Continued westward settlement after the war (see **3.12**) accelerated dispossession regardless of wartime allegiance.

### International Repercussions
- Declaration of Independence and republican ideology inspired: the **French Revolution (1789)**, the **Haitian Revolution (1791–1804)** — notably invoking liberty/equality to end slavery, something the U.S. did not do — and later Latin American independence movements (e.g., **Simón Bolívar**).

## 3.7 — The Articles of Confederation

### Structure
- **Unicameral Congress** — one vote per state; key decisions needed supermajority; amendments required unanimity.
- **No executive branch, no federal judiciary.**
- Congress could conduct diplomacy, borrow money, manage war — but **could NOT tax or regulate commerce**.

### Accomplishments
- Oversaw end of Revolutionary War and Treaty of Paris.
- **Newburgh Conspiracy (1783):** Unpaid Continental Army officers threatened to march on Congress or refuse to disband; **Washington personally intervened**, appealing to their loyalty and defusing the crisis — reinforced civilian control over the military, a defining precedent for the new republic.
- **Land Ordinance of 1785** — systematic survey/sale of western lands using a **township-and-section grid** (36 one-square-mile sections per township); one section in each township set aside to fund public education.
- **Northwest Ordinance of 1787** — created a clear process for territories to become states on equal footing with existing ones (rather than remain colonies); prohibited slavery in the Northwest Territory; guaranteed basic civil liberties (jury trial, religious freedom) to territorial residents.

### Weaknesses

| Problem | Details |
|---|---|
| **Foreign Affairs** | Could not enforce treaties; British kept frontier posts |
| **Economic** | Could not tax → no stable currency; national debt unpaid |
| **Internal Conflicts** | States acted as sovereigns; tariff wars; unresolved boundary disputes |

### Shays's Rebellion (1786–1787)
- Massachusetts farmers (led by **Daniel Shays**) closed debtors' courts; attempted to seize a federal armory.
- **Showed the Articles' inability to maintain public order** → catalyzed the Constitutional Convention.

## 3.8 — The Constitutional Convention and Ratification

### The Convention (Philadelphia, 1787)
- 12 states attended (Rhode Island abstained).
- Goal: Revise Articles → resulted in entirely **new Constitution**.

### Key Debates & Compromises

| Issue | Plans / Solution |
|---|---|
| **Representation** | **Virginia Plan** (Madison; bicameral, representation by population, favored large states) vs. **New Jersey Plan** (William Paterson; unicameral, equal representation, favored small states) → **Great Compromise** (also called the **Connecticut Compromise**, proposed by Roger Sherman): bicameral Congress — Senate (equal, 2 per state) + House (proportional) |
| **Slavery** | **Three-Fifths Compromise** — every 5 enslaved people counted as 3 for both taxation and representation purposes, inflating southern political power in the House and Electoral College; international **slave trade protected from federal ban until 1808**; **Fugitive Slave Clause** required escaped enslaved people be returned to their enslavers even if they reached a free state |
| **Commerce** | Congress regulates interstate/foreign commerce; no export taxes (a concession to southern export economies) |
| **Presidency** | 4-year term; **Electoral College** (a compromise between direct popular election and congressional selection); presidential veto, subject to congressional override |

### Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists

| Group | Key Views | Notable Figures |
|---|---|---|
| **Federalists** | Strong federal government; loose interpretation | Madison, Hamilton, John Jay |
| **Anti-Federalists** | Feared tyranny; wanted states' rights; demanded Bill of Rights | George Mason, Patrick Henry |

### The Federalist Papers
- 85 essays by **Madison, Hamilton, and Jay** (published under the pseudonym **"Publius"**) arguing for ratification in New York newspapers.
- **Federalist No. 10** (Madison) — argued a large republic actually controls the "mischiefs of faction" better than a small one, since it dilutes any single faction's power.
- **Federalist No. 51** (Madison) — explained checks and balances: **"ambition must be made to counteract ambition."**
- Anti-Federalists countered in essays like **"Brutus No. 1"** — warned a strong central government and a federal judiciary with broad power would inevitably swallow the states and threaten liberty, especially over such a large territory.

### Ratification
- Key objection: **No Bill of Rights** to explicitly protect individual liberties from the new, stronger national government.
- Federalists promised to add one → secured ratification in closely divided states (Massachusetts, Virginia, New York); **Bill of Rights ratified 1791**.

## 3.9 — The Constitution

### Federalism
- **Federal:** National defense, foreign affairs, interstate commerce, postal services.
- **States:** Education, local elections, etc.
- Federal power has grown over time via amendments (e.g., 19th Amendment) and economic interconnection.
- **Necessary and Proper Clause ("Elastic Clause"):** Grants Congress power to make laws needed to carry out its enumerated powers — the constitutional basis for later debates over **implied powers** (e.g., Hamilton's national bank argument in 3.10).
- **Supremacy Clause (Article VI):** Federal law and the Constitution take precedence over conflicting state laws — directly rebutted by Jefferson/Madison's Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions' nullification theory (see 3.10).

### Separation of Powers & Checks and Balances

| Branch | Role |
|---|---|
| **Legislative (Congress)** | Makes laws, taxes, spending |
| **Executive (President)** | Recommends/enforces laws; veto |
| **Judicial (Supreme Court)** | Interprets laws and Constitution |

### Bill of Rights (Ratified 1791)

| Amendment | Protection |
|---|---|
| 1st | Religion, speech, press, assembly, petition |
| 2nd | Right to bear arms |
| 3rd | No quartering of soldiers |
| 4th | No unreasonable searches/seizures |
| 5th | Due process; self-incrimination; double jeopardy |
| 6th | Speedy/public trial; counsel |
| 7th | Jury trial in civil cases |
| 8th | No cruel/unusual punishment or excessive bail |
| 9th | Unlisted rights still protected |
| 10th | Powers not delegated → reserved to states/people |

## 3.10 — Shaping a New Republic

### Washington's Presidency
- First cabinet: Jefferson (State), Hamilton (Treasury), Knox (War), Randolph (AG).
- **Judiciary Act of 1789**: Supreme Court — 1 Chief Justice + 5 Associates; district/circuit courts.

### Hamilton's Financial Plan
1. **Pay off debt** at face value (federal assumes state war debts, i.e., "assumption") — rewarded speculators who had bought up depreciated war bonds and established U.S. credit internationally.
2. **Protective tariffs** to promote domestic industry.
3. **National Bank (chartered 1791)** for stable currency and to manage government finances.
- Opposition from Jefferson and Madison (favored yeoman farmers, feared concentrated financial/moneyed power): compromise on assumption/debt → southern states (with less debt) agreed in exchange for the national **capital moved to Washington, D.C.** (the "Dinner Table Bargain," 1790).
- **Constitutional debate over the Bank:** Jefferson argued for **strict construction** — the Constitution nowhere explicitly authorizes a national bank, so Congress lacked the power. Hamilton argued for **loose construction**, citing the **Necessary and Proper Clause** — a bank was a reasonable means to carry out Congress's enumerated fiscal powers. Washington sided with Hamilton; this loose-vs-strict divide became a defining and recurring theme of early American politics.

### Democratic-Republican Societies
- Local political clubs (inspired partly by the French Revolution) that organized in the early 1790s to criticize Federalist policies (the Bank, the excise tax, the Jay Treaty) — Federalists viewed them with suspicion, especially after the Whiskey Rebellion; an early sign of organized political opposition/what became the Democratic-Republican Party.

### Foreign Affairs Under Washington
- **French Revolution** divided Americans; Washington declared **Proclamation of Neutrality (1793)**.
- **Jay Treaty (1794):** Britain evacuated frontier posts but didn't address impressment.
- **Pinckney Treaty (1795):** Spain opened Mississippi River and New Orleans to American trade; Florida boundary set at 31st parallel.

### Domestic Issues

| Event | Details |
|---|---|
| **Northwest Confederacy / Battle of Fallen Timbers (1794)** | General Anthony Wayne defeated Native tribes; Treaty of Greenville → Ohio Territory opened |
| **Whiskey Rebellion (1794)** | Western PA farmers refused whiskey tax; Washington sent 15,000 militia → rebellion collapsed; showed federal authority |

### First Political Parties

| Party | Leader | Views |
|---|---|---|
| **Federalists** | Hamilton | Strong central government; loose Constitution; northern business |
| **Democratic-Republicans** | Jefferson | States' rights; strict Constitution; small farmers |

### Washington's Farewell Address
- Warned against: **permanent alliances, political parties, sectionalism, European entanglements**.
- Set **two-term precedent** (until FDR; codified by **22nd Amendment, 1951**).

### John Adams' Presidency
- **XYZ Affair (1797–98):** French agents (referred to as "X, Y, and Z" in published dispatches) demanded bribes and a loan from U.S. diplomats before negotiations over French seizures of American shipping could even begin → American outrage ("Millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute"), but **Adams resisted Federalist war fever and avoided a formal war** with France — a decision that cost him support within his own party but that he considered his greatest achievement.
- **Quasi-War (1798–1800):** Undeclared naval conflict with France — American and French ships skirmished at sea; ended by the **Convention of 1800**, which also released the U.S. from its 1778 alliance obligations to France.
- **Alien and Sedition Acts (1798)** — four laws passed by the Federalist Congress amid war fever with France, aimed largely at suppressing Democratic-Republican (often immigrant) opposition:
  - **Naturalization Act:** raised residency requirement for citizenship from 5 to 14 years.
  - **Alien Friends Act:** allowed the president to deport any non-citizen deemed dangerous.
  - **Alien Enemies Act:** allowed detention/deportation of male citizens of a hostile nation during wartime.
  - **Sedition Act:** criminalized "false, scandalous, and malicious" writing against the government or officials — used to prosecute Democratic-Republican newspaper editors.
- **Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (Jefferson & Madison, 1798):** Argued states could judge the constitutionality of federal laws and **nullify** ones they deemed unconstitutional (specifically targeting the Alien and Sedition Acts) — an early articulation of **compact theory** (the Constitution as a compact among sovereign states) and a direct precursor to 19th-century states' rights and nullification disputes.

### Election of 1800 — "The Revolution of 1800"
- **Jefferson (Democratic-Republican)** defeated **Adams (Federalist)**, marking the **first peaceful transfer of power between rival political parties** in U.S. history — a critical proof that the constitutional system could survive partisan conflict.
- A flaw in the original Electoral College system produced a **tie between Jefferson and his own running mate, Aaron Burr** (each with 73 electoral votes), throwing the election to the **House of Representatives**; after 35 deadlocked ballots, Federalist **Alexander Hamilton** (who distrusted Burr more than Jefferson) helped tip the House toward Jefferson.
- The crisis directly led to the **12th Amendment (1804)**, which required electors to cast separate ballots for president and vice president.

## 3.11 — Developing an American Identity

### A Distinct National Culture
- **Republicanism** as a cultural, not just political, force: emphasized civic virtue, simplicity, and the citizen's duty to the public good over aristocratic privilege — Americans consciously tried to distinguish their culture from Britain's.
- **Noah Webster:** Called the "Father of American Scholarship and Education"; published widely used school textbooks ("blue-backed speller") and later *An American Dictionary of the English Language* — standardized American spelling (distinct from British English) and promoted a shared linguistic/educational nationalism.
- **Print culture:** Newspapers, almanacs (e.g., Franklin's earlier *Poor Richard's Almanack*), and pamphlets spread shared political ideas across a geographically dispersed population; the *New-England Primer* remained the most widely used colonial-era textbook.

### Literature
- **Phillis Wheatley** — an enslaved (later freed) woman, the first African American to publish a book of poetry (*Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral*, 1773); her success was invoked in Enlightenment-era debates over Black intellectual capacity.
- **William Hill Brown's *The Power of Sympathy* (1789)** — considered the first American novel.
- Growth of a distinctly American print market signaled cultural independence even as most "high culture" still looked to Europe for standards.

### Art & Architecture
- **Republican Motherhood:** Women framed as guardians of civic virtue, raising the next generation of citizens — **Judith Sargent Murray's** *On the Equality of the Sexes* (1790) went further, arguing women's minds were naturally equal to men's and deserved equal education; the **Young Ladies' Academy of Philadelphia (1787)** was among the first chartered institutions for female education.
- **Painters:** **Charles Willson Peale** (founded one of the first American museums, displaying natural history alongside portraits of Revolutionary leaders), **Gilbert Stuart** (iconic portraits of Washington), and **John Trumbull** (grand history paintings of Revolutionary battles and the Declaration's signing) built a shared visual mythology around the Founding and its leaders.
- **Neoclassical/Federal architecture:** Public buildings and homes (e.g., Jefferson's Monticello) borrowed Greek and Roman forms — domes, columns — to visually associate the new republic with the civic virtue of classical republics.

### Religion
- **Thomas Jefferson's Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (1786)** — disestablished the Anglican church in Virginia and became a model for the First Amendment's separation of church and state; reflected Enlightenment-influenced religious pluralism among the Founders.
- The Second Great Awakening's early stirrings emphasized individual choice and personal conversion over hierarchy — echoing republican political values of individual agency.

## 3.12 — Movement in the Early Republic

### Westward Migration
- Land hunger, rapid population growth, and the promise of cheap land pulled settlers — many **Scots-Irish** — into the trans-Appalachian West even before the Revolution ended.
- **Daniel Boone** blazed the **Wilderness Road** through the Cumberland Gap and helped found **Boonesborough, Kentucky (1775)**, opening Kentucky to settlement; frontier communities like the **Watauga Association (1772, Tennessee)** organized informal self-government far ahead of any formal territorial authority.
- **Northwest Ordinance (1787)** provided the legal framework for organizing and admitting new states from the territory on equal footing with the original 13 — a deliberate rejection of a permanent-colony model; **Marietta, Ohio (1788)** became the first permanent U.S. settlement in the Northwest Territory.
- **Pinckney's Treaty (1795)** opened the Mississippi River and the port of New Orleans (via "right of deposit") to American trade, easing western farmers' access to eastern and international markets — critical since overland transport was slow and costly.

### Conflict with Native Nations
- Settler expansion directly violated existing treaty boundaries and provoked sustained resistance from Native confederacies.
- The **Northwest Indian Confederacy**, led by Miami chief **Little Turtle**, inflicted major defeats on U.S. forces, including **St. Clair's Defeat at the Battle of the Wabash (1791)** — the worst battlefield defeat the U.S. Army ever suffered against Native American forces.
- General **Anthony Wayne** reversed this at the **Battle of Fallen Timbers (1794)**, leading to the **Treaty of Greenville (1795)**, in which Native nations ceded most of present-day Ohio — a short-term resolution that nonetheless set the pattern of treaty-then-violation that continued for decades.
- Federal Indian policy under Washington rhetorically recognized tribal sovereignty (treaties implied nation-to-nation dealing) even as it consistently facilitated further land cessions.

### Frontier Unrest & Federal Authority
- Sparse governance and economic hardship on the frontier fueled unrest: the **Whiskey Rebellion (1794)** in western Pennsylvania and the smaller **Fries's Rebellion (1798–99)** both tested — and ultimately affirmed — the new federal government's authority to enforce its laws and taxes.
- Migration strained transportation infrastructure (rough overland roads, dependence on river transport), spurring early interest in internal improvements (roads, river navigation) that would expand greatly in Period 4.
- **Cotton gin (1793)** made short-staple cotton cultivation profitable across the Deep South, pulling settlement and slavery westward into Georgia and the future Alabama/Mississippi territories — linking westward movement directly to slavery's expansion.

## 3.13 — Continuity and Change in Period 3

### What Changed
- Thirteen dependent colonies → independent republic with a **written Constitution**, a **Bill of Rights**, and a working federal government able to tax, regulate commerce, and enforce its laws (unlike under the Articles of Confederation).
- Political legitimacy shifted from hereditary monarchy and divine right to **popular sovereignty** and the **consent of the governed**; titles of nobility and **primogeniture** (inheritance by the eldest son alone) were abolished.
- A **first party system** emerged (Federalists vs. Democratic-Republicans) despite the Founders' hope that parties ("factions") could be avoided — and in 1800, that system proved it could survive a **peaceful transfer of power** between rivals, arguably the Revolution's most important practical achievement.
- Loyalist elites lost land and status; Loyalist-owned estates were confiscated and redistributed, reshuffling colonial-era social hierarchies in some regions.
- A distinct **American cultural identity** began forming in language, art, architecture, and print culture (3.11), even while much of "elite" culture still looked to Europe.

### What Continued
- **Slavery not only persisted but expanded** — protected by the Constitution itself (Three-Fifths Clause, fugitive slave clause, 1808 slave-trade protection) and, after the cotton gin (1793), pulled westward with settlement — a direct contradiction of Revolutionary natural-rights rhetoric that later generations would have to confront.
- **Women remained excluded from formal political power** — no voting rights (beyond New Jersey's brief, later-revoked exception) and no office-holding; "Republican Motherhood" offered a culturally elevated but still fundamentally domestic role.
- **Native American dispossession continued and arguably accelerated** — regardless of wartime allegiance (even Patriot allies like the Oneida lost land), westward settlement and successive treaties (Fort Stanwix 1784, Greenville 1795) steadily eroded Native sovereignty and territory.
- **Economic and regional divisions** (North vs. South, agrarian vs. commercial, elite vs. common farmer/debtor) carried over largely unresolved into the new republic — visible in disputes from Shays's Rebellion through the Whiskey Rebellion to the Hamilton–Jefferson financial/constitutional fights.

### Historiographical Takeaway
The central AP exam framing for this period: the Revolution was **genuinely transformative in politics and ideology** (legitimacy of government, rejection of hereditary rule, written constitutionalism) but **far more limited in its social and economic effects** (slavery, gender, Native sovereignty) — a gap between revolutionary promise and revolutionary practice that defines much of the conflict in the periods that follow.
