# Period 4 — New Republic (1800–1848)

---

## 4.1 — Contextualizing Period 4

### A Young Republic Comes of Age
- Period 3 ended with the first peaceful transfer of power between rival parties (Federalists → Democratic-Republicans, 1800) — proof the new constitutional system could survive political conflict.
- The nation was still small and agrarian but growing rapidly in territory and population, setting up the **Market Revolution** and westward expansion covered in this period.
- Unresolved tensions carried over: how much power the federal government should have, and the future of slavery in new territories.
- Growing democratization (expanded White male suffrage) and religious revivalism (Second Great Awakening) would reshape politics and society throughout the period.

---

## 4.2 — The Rise of Political Parties and the Era of Jefferson

### The Election of 1800 ("Revolution of 1800")
- **Federalists (Adams):** Strong national government; pro-Britain; protective tariffs.
- **Democratic-Republicans (Jefferson):** States' rights; pro-France; lower tariffs.
- **Result:** Jefferson won — first peaceful transfer of power between parties.

### Jefferson's Presidency
- Maintained national bank and debt-repayment plan (pragmatic compromise) despite his Democratic-Republican philosophy of limited government — showed Federalist economic policy had staying power.
- Reduced military, repealed excise taxes, lowered national debt.
- **Twelfth Amendment (1804):** Required separate electoral ballots for president and vice president, fixing the flaw exposed by the 1800 Jefferson–Burr electoral tie.
- **First Barbary War (1801–1805):** Jefferson refused to keep paying tribute to North African Barbary States for safe Mediterranean shipping; sent the Navy and Marines ("shores of Tripoli") — first U.S. military action abroad, boosted national pride.
- **Judiciary Act of 1801 / "Midnight Judges":** Outgoing Federalists packed the courts with last-minute appointments before Jefferson took office; incoming Democratic-Republicans refused to deliver some commissions (including William Marbury's) → direct setup for *Marbury v. Madison*.

### The Louisiana Purchase (1803)
- Napoleon sold the Louisiana Territory (needed funds for European wars + lost Santo Domingo to **Toussaint Louverture's rebellion**).
- Jefferson (strict constructionist) wrestled with constitutionality → justified via **treaty-making powers**.
- **$15 million** → doubled U.S. size; removed European presence from borders.
- **Lewis and Clark Expedition** — mapped the territory; documented species; strengthened claims to Oregon.

### John Marshall's Supreme Court ⭐
*Chief Justice 1801–1835; favored strong central government and property rights.*

| Case | Year | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Marbury v. Madison** ⭐ | 1803 | Established **judicial review** — Supreme Court can declare acts unconstitutional |
| **Fletcher v. Peck** | 1810 | First time a *state* law declared unconstitutional; sanctity of contracts |
| **Martin v. Hunter's Lease** | 1816 | Supreme Court jurisdiction over state courts on constitutional matters |
| **Dartmouth College v. Woodward** | 1819 | State cannot alter private corporation charter — protected private contracts |
| **McCulloch v. Maryland** ⭐ | 1819 | States cannot tax federal institution; affirmed **implied powers** and national bank |
| **Cohens v. Virginia** | 1821 | Supreme Court can review state court decisions involving federal authority |
| **Gibbons v. Ogden** ⭐ | 1824 | Federal government has broad control over **interstate commerce** |

## 4.3 — Politics and Regional Interests

### Era of Good Feelings (Monroe, 1816–1824)
- Federalist Party faded; apparent political unity.
- Underlying tensions: tariffs, national bank, internal improvements, **rising sectionalism over slavery**.

### Henry Clay's American System
1. **Protective Tariffs** — promote manufacturing, raise revenue.
2. **National Bank** — stable currency and credit.
3. **Internal Improvements** — federally funded infrastructure.

### Tariff of 1816
- First protective tariff; protected U.S. manufacturers from British competition.

### The Panic of 1819
- Triggered by Second Bank tightening credit.
- Bank closures, unemployment, foreclosures → weakened faith in the national bank.

### The Missouri Compromise (1820)

**Background:** Missouri's application for statehood (as a slave state) threatened the 11 free / 11 slave Senate balance.

**Henry Clay's Solution:**
1. Admit **Missouri** as a slave state.
2. Admit **Maine** as a free state.
3. Prohibit slavery north of **36° 30'** latitude in the rest of the Louisiana Territory.

- **Tallmadge Amendment (1819):** Rep. James Tallmadge's proposal to ban further slave importation into Missouri and gradually free enslaved children born there — passed the House, failed in the Senate, but the debate revealed how explosive the balance-of-power question had become. Jefferson called it "a fire bell in the night."

> **Preserved the balance** between free and slave states for 30+ years — until the Compromise of 1850 and the Kansas-Nebraska Act.

### The Election of 1824 — "Corrupt Bargain"
- Four Democratic-Republican candidates split the vote along regional lines (the "Era of Good Feelings" one-party system had no way to settle internal disputes): **Andrew Jackson** (West, war hero) — 99 electoral votes/153,000 popular votes; **John Quincy Adams** (New England) — 84 electoral votes; **William Crawford** (South) — 41; **Henry Clay** (West, Speaker of the House) — 37.
- No candidate won an Electoral College majority → **Twelfth Amendment** sent the decision to the House of Representatives, where each state delegation cast one vote.
- Clay, eliminated from contention but Speaker of the House, threw his support to Adams, who won the House vote. Adams then appointed Clay **Secretary of State** (the traditional stepping-stone to the presidency).
- Jacksonians denounced the deal as a **"corrupt bargain"** — Jackson had won the most popular and electoral votes but lost the presidency. The charge (never proven) poisoned Adams's presidency and fueled Jackson's 1828 campaign.

### John Quincy Adams's Presidency (1825–1829)
- Pursued an ambitious, nationalist agenda — federally funded roads/canals, a national university, astronomical observatories — that alienated strict-constructionist Southerners and Westerners as federal overreach.
- Weak, unpopular president; lacked a party base after the "corrupt bargain"; accomplished little legislatively.
- His administration's support for high tariffs culminated in the **Tariff of 1828 ("Tariff of Abominations")**, passed by a Jacksonian Congress that (some argue) deliberately designed it to be so unpopular in the South it would embarrass Adams — it backfired and passed anyway, devastating Southern export markets.

## 4.4 — America on the World Stage

### Jefferson's Foreign Policy
- Aimed to avoid war; maintained neutrality in Napoleonic Wars.
- **Chesapeake-Leopard Affair (1807):** British attacked U.S. warship → anti-British sentiment.
- **Embargo Act (1807):** Prohibited American ships from foreign ports → hurt U.S. economy more than Britain; repealed 1809.

### War of 1812
**Causes:**
- British impressment of American sailors.
- **War Hawks** (Clay, Calhoun) — wanted to defend American honor, gain Canada, destroy Native resistance.
- Britain suspended naval blockade just as Madison declared war (news arrived too late).

**Key Events:**

| Event | Details |
|---|---|
| Invasion of Canada | Failed |
| USS Constitution | Notable naval victory |
| Chesapeake Campaign | British burned Washington, D.C.; failed to take Baltimore → **"The Star-Spangled Banner"** |
| **Battle of New Orleans** | Jackson's victory after the peace treaty was signed |
| **Treaty of Ghent (1814)** | Status quo ante bellum; prewar boundaries restored; maritime issues unresolved |
| Hartford Convention | New England Federalists (opposed war) discussed secession → labeled unpatriotic |

**Legacy:**
- U.S. gained international respect.
- Federalist Party collapsed.
- Native Americans lost British support → forced to surrender land.
- **Andrew Jackson** and **William Henry Harrison** emerged as political figures.
- Nationalism grew; Americans looked westward.

### Monroe Doctrine (1823)
- Declared Western Hemisphere **closed to further European colonization**.
- U.S. would oppose any European interference in Western Hemisphere republics.
- Backed by British navy, not U.S. military (initially).
- **Long-term significance:** Foundation of U.S. foreign policy; extended by Roosevelt Corollary (1904).

### Key Treaties Under Monroe

| Treaty | Year | Terms |
|---|---|---|
| Rush-Bagot Agreement | 1817 | Limited naval armaments on Great Lakes; unfortified US-Canada border |
| Treaty of 1818 | 1818 | Joint Oregon occupation; fishing rights; 49th parallel northern boundary |
| Adams-Onís Treaty | 1819 | Spain ceded Florida; U.S. gave up Texas claims; $5 million |

### Texas: Revolution and the Annexation Debate
- American settlers (led by **Stephen F. Austin**) colonized Mexican Texas through the 1820s, invited by Mexico but retaining slavery and English language/customs — cultural friction with the Mexican government grew.
- **Texas Revolution (1835–1836):** Sparked by Mexican President **Santa Anna**'s centralizing policies (abolishing state legislatures, threatening to end slavery). **Battle of the Alamo (Feb–Mar 1836)** — Santa Anna's forces killed all ~200 defenders (Travis, Bowie, Crockett) after a 13-day siege; became a rallying cry ("Remember the Alamo!"). **Battle of San Jacinto (April 1836)** — Sam Houston's forces defeated and captured Santa Anna, securing Texan independence.
- The **Republic of Texas** (1836–1845) requested annexation almost immediately, but Presidents Jackson and Van Buren stalled — annexing Texas (a huge new slave state) would inflame sectional tensions and risked war with Mexico.
- Annexation remained a live political question through the 1840s; it was not resolved until **1845**, when it directly set up the **Mexican-American War (1846)** — the bridge from Period 4 into Period 5's sectional crisis over slavery in the territories (Wilmot Proviso, Compromise of 1850).

## 4.6 — Market Revolution: Society and Culture

### Women's Changing Roles
- **Cult of Domesticity ("Separate Spheres"):** As paid work moved out of the household and into shops/factories, the home was redefined as a private, feminine "sphere" — women as moral guardians and child-rearers ("Republican Motherhood" evolved into this ideal) while men worked in the public, competitive "sphere" of business.
- Limited employment options (domestic service, teaching, factory — Lowell System).
- Legal restrictions: **couldn't vote**; under **coverture**, a married woman's property and wages legally belonged to her husband; limited divorce and custody rights.
- **Declining birth rate:** average White woman had ~7 children in 1800 vs. ~5 by 1850 — early evidence of deliberate family limitation, especially among the growing urban middle class.
- **Historiographical debate:** Historians disagree on whether "separate spheres" ideology *empowered* women (giving them a distinct moral authority and organizational base that fed directly into reform movements — temperance, abolition, Seneca Falls) or *restricted* them (excluding women from politics, wage-earning, and public life). Both readings are defensible on the AP exam if supported with evidence.

### Population and Immigration
- U.S. population doubled every 25 years (1800–1850).
- Post-1832 immigration surge (peaked at 428,000 in 1854): causes — cheap ocean transport, European famines/revolutions, economic opportunity.
- Mostly settled in northern cities and the Old Northwest.

### Urbanization
- Urban population grew from 5% (1800) to 15% (1850).
- New cities at transportation hubs: Buffalo, Cleveland, Detroit, Chicago, Cincinnati, St. Louis.

### Organized Labor
- *Commonwealth v. Hunt* (1842) — affirmed right to organize and negotiate contracts.
- Challenges: depressions, employer hostility, immigrant labor supply limiting union effectiveness.

## 4.7 — Expanding Democracy

### Politics of the Common Man (1824–1840)
- New suffrage laws → removed property/religious qualifications; all White males could vote (driven by Western states with no entrenched elite, then copied East).
- Voter turnout: 350,000 (1824) → 2.4 million (1840) — over 80% of eligible voters cast ballots by 1840, a level rarely matched since.
- Political parties used **nominating conventions** instead of the old, elite-controlled congressional caucus system — seen as more democratic since delegates came from state/local party organizations.
- **Anti-Masonic Party (1828–1830s):** Opposed the secretive, "elitist" Masonic fraternal order; the nation's first significant third party. Held the **first-ever national nominating convention (1831)** — a format both major parties quickly adopted.
- The two-party system that emerged (Democrats vs. National Republicans/Whigs) was organized to be genuinely national, with strong support in both North and South — a structural check on sectionalism that would erode by the 1850s.

### Spoils System (Jackson)
- Appointed loyal Democrats to federal jobs.
- **Rotation in Office** — limited terms to allow more citizens to participate.

## 4.8 — Jackson and Federal Power

### Election of 1828
- Jackson won decisively, appealing as a war hero and man of the frontier.
- **Tariff of Abominations (1828)** — hurt Southern planters; worsened sectional tensions.

### Jackson's Presidency
- "Representative of the Common Man"; exercised strong executive power.
- Vetoed more bills than all previous presidents combined.

### Indian Removal Act (1830)
- Forced relocation of the "Five Civilized Tribes" (Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole) from the Southeast to "Indian Territory" (present-day Oklahoma) west of the Mississippi.
- The **Cherokee** had adapted many Anglo-American institutions — a written language (Sequoyah's syllabary), a constitution, a newspaper (*Cherokee Phoenix*), farms — and still faced removal once Georgia wanted their land (gold was discovered there in 1828).
- **Trail of Tears (1838–1839):** The Cherokee removal, carried out under Van Buren after Jackson left office. At least **4,000 Cherokees died** of cold, disease, and starvation on the forced march; some Cherokee accounts and historians place the toll considerably higher (6,000+).
- **Seminole Wars** in Florida — the Seminoles (aided by escaped enslaved people who had taken refuge among them) resisted removal militarily into the 1840s, the only tribe never fully defeated or removed.
- **Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831):** Marshall Court ruled the Cherokee Nation was a "domestic dependent nation," not a foreign state, and so couldn't sue in federal court.
- **Worcester v. Georgia (1832):** Georgia laws had no force within Cherokee territory — a win for Cherokee sovereignty on paper. Jackson **refused to enforce** this ruling (allegedly: "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it") — a stark example of executive defiance of the judiciary.

### Nullification Crisis
- **South Carolina Exposition and Protest (1828):** Written anonymously by Vice President **John C. Calhoun** in response to the "Tariff of Abominations" — laid out the theory that a state convention could **nullify** a federal law it deemed unconstitutional within its borders.
- **Webster-Hayne Debate (1830):** Senate showdown between Daniel Webster (MA) and Robert Hayne (SC) — Hayne defended states' rights and nullification; Webster argued the Union was formed by "the people," not sovereign states, and could not be legally dissolved by a minority ("Liberty and Union, now and forever, one and inseparable!").
- **1832:** South Carolina held a state convention that formally declared the Tariffs of 1828 and 1832 null and void within the state and threatened secession if the federal government tried to enforce them.
- Jackson: "The Union must be preserved"; requested and received the **Force Bill (1833)**, authorizing him to use the military to collect tariff duties in South Carolina.
- **Compromise Tariff of 1833** (engineered by Henry Clay) gradually lowered rates over several years — ended the immediate crisis, but neither side backed down ideologically: South Carolina "nullified" the Force Bill on its way out, and Calhoun's states'-rights/nullification theory would resurface in secession arguments in 1860–61.

### Bank War
- Jackson saw the Second Bank (headed by **Nicholas Biddle**) as an unconstitutional monopoly that concentrated economic power in the hands of wealthy Eastern elites and foreign investors, unaccountable to ordinary voters — despite *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) upholding its constitutionality.
- **Vetoed recharter (1832)** — Clay pushed an early recharter bill through Congress specifically to make it a campaign issue against Jackson in the 1832 election; Jackson's veto message was itself a piece of populist political theater, and he won reelection decisively, treating it as a mandate to kill the bank.
- Withdrew federal deposits and redistributed them to state "**pet banks**" (chosen partly for political loyalty) — the Bank's national regulatory role vanished, and state banks over-issued paper banknotes to fund speculation, especially in western land.
- **Specie Circular (1836)** — required that public land purchases be paid in gold/silver ("specie") rather than paper banknotes, intended to curb speculation; instead it triggered a scramble for hard currency that helped collapse the credit bubble.

### Van Buren and the Panic of 1837
- **Martin Van Buren** (Jackson's chosen successor) won the **Election of 1836**, but inherited the consequences of the Bank War almost immediately.
- **Panic of 1837:** Triggered by the Specie Circular, the end of British loans to the U.S. amid a British financial crisis, and a collapse in cotton prices — combined with overextended state banks, it produced runs on banks, hundreds of bank and business failures, and roughly **five years of depression** (one of the worst of the 19th century) with widespread unemployment.
- Van Buren refused to have the federal government intervene directly (consistent with Democratic small-government philosophy) and instead pushed the **Independent Treasury Act (1840)** — had the federal government hold its own funds in "subtreasuries" separate from any bank, public or private. It passed in 1840 but was repealed and restored repeatedly over subsequent decades.
- The Panic devastated Van Buren's popularity and the Democratic Party heading into 1840.

### Election of 1840 — "Log Cabin and Hard Cider"
- Whigs nominated **William Henry Harrison** (a war hero from the Battle of Tippecanoe) over Henry Clay, calculating a military hero with no political paper trail would be more electable.
- When a Democratic newspaper mocked Harrison as a simple frontiersman who would be happy in a log cabin with a barrel of hard cider, the Whigs seized the insult and ran the first full-blown modern media campaign — log cabin imagery, cider, songs, rallies — portraying the actually wealthy, Virginia-born Harrison as a humble man of the people (and Van Buren, of comparatively humble origins, as an aristocrat).
- Harrison won in a landslide; died of pneumonia only **31 days into his term**, succeeded by Vice President **John Tyler**, whose states'-rights views clashed with Whig congressional leadership ("His Accidency").
- **Significance:** Confirmed mass political campaigning (slogans, symbols, spectacle over substance) as the new normal in an era of near-universal White male suffrage.

### Democrats vs. Whigs

| Issue | Democrats (Jackson, Van Buren) | Whigs (Clay, Webster, Harrison) |
|---|---|---|
| National Bank | Opposed — concentrated, unaccountable power | Supported — stable currency and credit |
| Federal internal improvements | Opposed/limited — states' rights, strict construction | Supported — Clay's American System |
| Tariffs | Generally lower | Generally higher, protective |
| Government's economic role | Minimal; laissez-faire; suspicious of monopolies/corporations | Active; government should promote economic development |
| Base of support | Southern planters, Western farmers, urban workers, immigrants | Northern merchants/industrialists, commercial farmers, reformers |
| View of Native peoples/social reform | Removal favored; less receptive to reform movements | More often sympathetic to reform (temperance, abolition-leaning wing), anti-immigrant nativist streak |

> **Historiographical debate:** Was Jackson a genuine "champion of the common man" who broke the grip of Eastern elites and expanded democracy for ordinary White men? Or was he an authoritarian who defied the Supreme Court (*Worcester v. Georgia*), abused the veto and spoils system, and forcibly removed tens of thousands of Native Americans? Historian **Arthur Schlesinger Jr.** (1945) framed Jackson as a proto-populist ally of the working class against monied elites; later historians emphasize that Jacksonian "democracy" expanded political power for White men while excluding and actively harming women, African Americans, and Native peoples. Both readings draw on real evidence — useful for DBQ/LEQ argumentation.

## 4.10 — The Second Great Awakening

### Causes of Religious Reform
- Democratic/individualistic emphasis; reaction against Enlightenment rationalism; fears of materialism; market revolution disruptions.

### Key Figures
- **Rev. Timothy Dwight** — Yale revivals; motivated evangelical preachers.
- **Charles Grandison Finney (1823)** — Led revivals across upstate New York; broke with strict Calvinist **predestination**, preaching that individuals could choose salvation through faith and moral effort (**Arminianism**) — a theological shift that dovetailed with the era's faith in individual self-improvement and reform.
- **"Burned-Over District"** — nickname for upstate/western New York (along the Erie Canal), so repeatedly swept by revivals that it was said to have no "fuel" (unconverted souls) left to burn; also birthplace of Mormonism, the Oneida Community, and much of the abolition/women's rights movements.
- **Baptists and Methodists** — used itinerant **circuit preachers** to reach the frontier and rural South; their emotional, egalitarian camp-meeting style (open to women and, in the South, enslaved people) made them the largest Protestant denominations by 1850, eclipsing the older, more formal Congregationalists and Presbyterians.

### New Denominations
- **Seventh-Day Adventists** — from William Miller's failed 1844 prophecy.
- **Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (1830)** — Joseph Smith; moved to Utah under Brigham Young.

### Social Reforms Sparked
- Temperance Movement
- Abolitionist Movement
- Mental health reform

## 4.11 — An Age of Reform

### Temperance
- Average American consumed **5 gallons of hard liquor annually** by 1820.
- **American Temperance Society (1826)** — total abstinence.
- Maine prohibited liquor manufacture/sale in 1851.
- Led to **18th Amendment (1919)** — Prohibition.

### Public Asylums
- **Dorothea Dix** — crusade for humane treatment of the mentally ill → state mental hospitals.
- **Thomas Gallaudet** — school for the deaf.
- **Dr. Samuel Gridley Howe** — school for the blind.
- Prison reform: Auburn system (NY) — strict discipline, moral instruction.

### Public Education
- **Horace Mann** — led the common school movement; compulsory attendance; longer school year; trained teachers.
- **McGuffey Readers** — taught reading and morality (hard work, punctuality, sobriety).

### Women's Rights
- **Seneca Falls Convention (1848)** — "Declaration of Sentiments": "all men and women are created equal."
- **Elizabeth Cady Stanton** and **Susan B. Anthony** led the campaign for voting, legal, and property rights.

### Anti-Slavery Movement

| Organization / Person | Role |
|---|---|
| American Colonization Society (1817) | Transported freed slaves to Liberia (Monrovia, 1822); impractical; most free Black leaders rejected it — they wanted equality in America, not "return" to a continent they'd never lived in |
| **David Walker's *Appeal* (1829)** | Free Black Boston pamphleteer; called for enslaved people to rise up violently against their enslavers if necessary; smuggled south in sailors' clothing; terrified Southern legislatures into passing stricter laws against Black literacy and assembly |
| William Lloyd Garrison | *The Liberator* (1831) — demanded **immediate**, uncompensated abolition (radical break from gradualism); helped found the American Antislavery Society (1833) |
| Liberty Party (1840) | First antislavery political party; moderate abolitionists pursuing political rather than moral-suasion means |
| **Frederick Douglass** | Escaped slave; brilliant orator and writer (*Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass*, 1845); published *The North Star*; broke with Garrison over using politics/direct action, not just moral suasion |
| **Harriet Tubman** | Escaped slave; made repeated trips south via the Underground Railroad, personally guiding dozens of enslaved people to freedom |
| **Sojourner Truth** | Escaped slave; powerful abolitionist and women's rights speaker, linking the two causes |
| **Nat Turner's Revolt (1831)** | Virginia; ~55 White deaths; brutal retaliation (execution of Turner and many uninvolved enslaved people); Virginia legislature narrowly debated and rejected gradual emancipation afterward — effectively ended serious antislavery discussion in the South and hardened proslavery ideology |
| **Denmark Vesey Conspiracy (1822)** | Charleston, SC; a free Black carpenter and former enslaved person allegedly planned a large-scale slave uprising; betrayed before it began; ~35 executed, harsh crackdown on Charleston's free Black community and independent Black churches — part of the pattern of revolt scares tightening slave codes across the South |
| **Gag Rule (1836–1844)** | House of Representatives rule automatically tabling all antislavery petitions without discussion; abolitionists flooded Congress with petitions (over 130,000 in one year) specifically to provoke this censorship; **John Quincy Adams**, now a congressman, fought it for years as a free-speech/right-of-petition issue and finally got it repealed in 1844 — turned a slavery debate into a civil-liberties debate that won Northern sympathy even from non-abolitionists |

## 4.12 — African Americans in the Early Republic

### Free African Americans
- Free Black population grew to roughly **250,000 in the North and additional free communities in the Upper South** by the 1830s, mostly through gradual emancipation laws passed by Northern states after the Revolution, self-purchase, or manumission.

**In the North:**
- Economic/political equality limited; most Northern states barred Black men from voting (even as property qualifications fell for White men — democratization was explicitly racialized), serving on juries, or testifying against Whites; competed with immigrants for the lowest-paid jobs.
- Formed independent institutions for mutual aid and community: **Richard Allen** founded the **African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church** in Philadelphia (1816) after being forced into segregated seating at a White Methodist church — became a hub for both worship and antislavery organizing.

**In the South:**
- Could own property (and, rarely, even enslaved relatives to protect them) but faced constant threat of kidnapping into slavery, restrictions on movement and assembly, and required "free papers" to prove their status.
- Proximity to enslaved family members kept many from migrating north even when they had the means.

### Slave Resistance
- Everyday resistance: work slowdowns, feigned illness, sabotage of tools/crops, breaking equipment — lower-risk than open rebellion but widespread.
- **Underground Railroad** — informal network of safe houses and guides (free Black and White abolitionists, including Quakers) helping enslaved people escape north or to Canada; increased Southern demands for stricter fugitive slave laws (a running sectional grievance leading into the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850).
- **Slave Codes** — laws stripping enslaved people of legal rights (banning literacy, restricting assembly and movement, denying legal marriage/family recognition); became significantly stricter across the South after **Denmark Vesey's conspiracy (1822)** and **Nat Turner's Revolt (1831)**.
- **Open rebellion was rare and almost always crushed** — the combination of White numerical majorities, armed slave patrols, and the practical difficulty of coordinating a rebellion across scattered plantations made revolt a desperate, high-casualty gamble; resistance more often took quieter, sustained forms.

## 4.13 — The Society of the South in the Early Republic

### King Cotton
- By 1850s, cotton = **two-thirds of U.S. exports**; the **cotton gin (1793)** made short-staple cotton (which grows well across the Deep South) profitable, driving the crop's explosive spread.
- Westward expansion of planters into Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas — the "**Cotton Kingdom**."
- Enslaved population: 1 million (1800) → nearly 4 million (1860).
- **Domestic ("internal") slave trade:** After the international slave trade was banned in **1808**, the enslaved population of the Upper South (Virginia, Maryland) was increasingly sold to the expanding Cotton Kingdom of the Deep South — sometimes called the **"Second Middle Passage."** Roughly one million enslaved people were forcibly relocated this way between 1800 and 1860, routinely separating families.

### Slavery
- Justification shifted over time: the Revolutionary generation often called slavery a **"necessary evil"** (regrettable but economically indispensable); by the 1830s–1850s, defenders like **John C. Calhoun** (1837 Senate speech) argued it was a **"positive good"** — economically essential, biblically sanctioned, and (they claimed) more humane than Northern wage labor ("wage slavery").
- High investment in enslaved labor (rather than machinery, factories, or infrastructure) left the South chronically short of capital and far less industrialized/urbanized than the North — a structural difference that would badly disadvantage the Confederacy in the Civil War.
- Strict slave codes; fear of revolts intensified after Vesey (1822) and Turner (1831).

### Southern Class Structure
- **Planter elite:** A small fraction of White Southerners (roughly the top 5%, owning 20+ enslaved people) controlled most of the region's wealth and political power.
- **Yeoman farmers:** The majority of White Southerners owned few or no enslaved people, farmed small plots for subsistence, and lived apart from the plantation economy — yet largely supported slavery and White supremacy out of racial solidarity, kinship ties to planter families, and hope of upward mobility.
- **Poor Whites** and a very small **free Black** population rounded out Southern society.

### Southern Culture
- Code of chivalry; personal honor (dueling persisted longer than in the North); **paternalism** — planters' self-justifying belief that they cared for enslaved people as a father figure would, ignoring the coercion and violence the system actually rested on.
- Upper class valued classical education; lower classes had limited access to schooling (the South lagged far behind the North in public education); teaching enslaved people to read was illegal in most states by the 1830s.
- Methodists and Baptists grew rapidly in the South in part because they accommodated a pro-slavery stance (after splitting from their Northern branches over the issue in the 1840s).

### Social Reform in the South
- Limited impact; tradition over modernization.
- Anti-slavery seen as a northern threat.

## 4.14 — Causation in Period 4

Causation questions ask *why* something happened and *what it led to* — not just what happened. Period 4 is the AP exam's favorite hunting ground for causation essays because nearly every development here has a traceable chain running both backward (to the Revolution's unresolved debates) and forward (into the Civil War).

### Chain 1: The Market Revolution → Regional Divergence
- **Causes:** Transportation innovation (turnpikes, the Erie Canal, railroads) + technology (interchangeable parts, textile mills, the cotton gin) + a fast-growing population and immigration made large-scale, specialized, market-oriented production possible for the first time.
- **Effects in the North:** Wage labor, factory work, and urbanization grew; the Lowell System pulled young women out of purely domestic roles; the "cult of domesticity" emerged as a *response* to work leaving the home, not a timeless tradition; organized labor (unions, *Commonwealth v. Hunt*) appeared for the first time.
- **Effects in the South:** The cotton gin made short-staple cotton wildly profitable, so instead of diversifying or industrializing, the South *doubled down* on plantation slavery — cotton exports and the enslaved population both roughly quadrupled between 1800 and 1850.
- **Result:** By 1850 the North and South had two fundamentally different, increasingly incompatible economic systems and value systems (free wage labor vs. enslaved labor), each region convinced its own model was more legitimate — a structural cause of the Civil War, not just a moral disagreement over slavery in the abstract.

### Chain 2: Expanding Democracy → Contested Definition of "the People"
- **Causes:** Removal of property/religious voting qualifications (Western states leading the way), the rise of mass political parties and nominating conventions, and Jackson's popular image as a war hero and frontiersman.
- **Effects:** Voter turnout among White men exploded (350,000 in 1824 → over 2.4 million in 1840); the **spoils system** and **rotation in office** tied political jobs to party loyalty and mass mobilization rather than elite patronage.
- **Limits/backlash:** This democratization was explicitly racialized and gendered — most Northern states restricted or barred Black men from voting *at the same time* they removed property qualifications for White men, and no state extended suffrage to women. The **Seneca Falls Convention (1848)** and the abolitionist movement directly challenged this narrower definition of political membership, previewing post–Civil War fights over the 14th and 15th Amendments.
- **Jackson historiography connects here:** whether you frame Jacksonian Democracy as a genuine expansion of democracy or a *redefinition of democracy along racial lines* changes how you'd argue a causation essay about this period.

### Chain 3: Territorial Expansion → Recurring Sectional Crises
- **Louisiana Purchase (1803)** roughly doubled U.S. territory without settling whether new states would be slave or free.
- **Missouri's statehood bid (1819)** forced the issue for the first time → **Missouri Compromise (1820)** drew the 36°30' line, but only *postponed* the conflict — Jefferson himself called it "a fire bell in the night," predicting it wouldn't hold.
- **Indian Removal (1830, Trail of Tears)** opened Southeastern land specifically for cotton-slavery expansion, tying territorial and economic causation together.
- **Texas independence (1836)** reopened the free/slave balance question on an even larger scale; annexation was deliberately delayed for nearly a decade (1836–1845) because both Jackson and Van Buren recognized it as too explosive.
- **Result:** By the end of Period 4, the "temporary fix" logic of the Missouri Compromise was running out of room — Texas annexation (1845) and the Mexican-American War (1846–1848) it triggered would reopen the exact question the Missouri Compromise tried to settle, this time with no line able to satisfy either side (leading directly to the Wilmot Proviso, the Compromise of 1850, and Kansas-Nebraska in Period 5).

### Chain 4: Religious Revival → Reform → Radicalized Sectional Conflict
- **Second Great Awakening** revivalism (Finney's Arminian theology of individual moral agency) taught that individuals — and by extension society — could be perfected through effort. This directly *caused* the reform movements of the 1820s–1840s: temperance, public education, asylum/prison reform, and abolition all shared the same underlying logic that sin and social problems could be actively reformed away.
- **Effect on abolition specifically:** Garrison's shift to demanding *immediate* abolition (1831) reflected this same "perfectionist" religious logic — slavery wasn't just economically flawed, it was a *sin* requiring immediate repentance, not gradual, compensated correction.
- **Southern backlash:** David Walker's *Appeal* (1829) and Nat Turner's Revolt (1831) convinced White Southerners that abolitionist rhetoric could incite violent rebellion, which *caused* Southern states to tighten slave codes, restrict Black literacy, and shift from defending slavery as a "necessary evil" to a "positive good" (Calhoun, 1837) — a much harder position to compromise away.
- **The Gag Rule (1836–1844)** shows how the slavery debate spilled into unrelated constitutional territory (free speech, right of petition), pulling moderate Northerners who didn't care about abolition directly into sectional conflict over principle.
- **Result:** By 1848, reform-movement abolitionism and Southern "positive good" ideology had hardened into mutually exclusive worldviews — the moral confrontation Period 5 would inherit could no longer be managed through economic compromise alone.

> **Big picture:** Period 4 didn't cause the Civil War through any single event — it built four separate, reinforcing engines (economic divergence, contested democracy, repeated territorial crises, and moral/religious radicalization) that all converged on the same fault line: slavery's future in an expanding nation. Any Period 4 causation essay should show at least two of these chains working together, not just list events.
