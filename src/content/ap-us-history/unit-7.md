# Period 7 — Progressive Era and World Wars (1890–1945)

---

## 7.1 — Contextualizing Period 7

### Where Period 7 Picks Up
- Picks up from the Gilded Age (Period 6): massive industrialization, unregulated trusts (Standard Oil, U.S. Steel, railroads), extreme wealth inequality, and a weak federal government that mostly deferred to laissez-faire economics.
- **Frederick Jackson Turner's "Frontier Thesis" (1893):** argued the now-"closed" frontier (per the 1890 Census) had shaped American character (individualism, democracy); its closing raised anxiety about where the next outlet for economic and population growth would come from — a key psychological driver of overseas expansion.
- Continued **New Immigration** from southern and eastern Europe (Italians, Poles, Russian/Eastern European Jews) — over 20 million immigrants arrived 1880–1920, fueling urban growth, ethnic enclaves, and nativist backlash.

### A Global Power Emerges
- By 1890, the frontier was declared "closed" (Census Bureau) — many Americans looked overseas for the next frontier of markets and influence.
- Industrialization created overproduction at home, pushing business and political leaders to seek foreign markets and raw materials.
- Progressive reformers were simultaneously tackling the domestic costs of industrialization — corruption, inequality, unsafe workplaces — while the nation debated its new role abroad.
- This period covers the U.S. rise as a world power, the Progressive reform era, and two world wars that cemented its global leadership.

### The Big Throughline: Growth of Federal Power
- Period 7's central story (per College Board Key Concept 7.1) is **economic instability driving reform of U.S. society and government**: Progressives regulate corporations → WWI centralizes the economy under federal war boards → the Depression forces the New Deal's permanent welfare/regulatory state → WWII cements federal power and a global military-economic role.
- Each crisis (industrial abuses, WWI, Depression, WWII) expanded what Americans expected government to do — a pattern tested repeatedly on the AP exam as **continuity and change over time (CCOT)**.

### Chronological Anchors
| Era | Approx. Dates | Core Theme |
|---|---|---|
| Imperialism & Progressivism | 1890–1917 | U.S. becomes a world power abroad; reforms industrial capitalism at home |
| World War I | 1914/1917–1919 | Mobilization, civil liberties curtailed, failed peace |
| The Twenties | 1920–1929 | Consumer prosperity, cultural conflict, "return to normalcy" |
| Great Depression & New Deal | 1929–1939 | Economic collapse; federal government redefines its role |
| World War II | 1939/1941–1945 | Total mobilization; U.S. emerges as a global superpower |

## 7.2 — Imperialism: Debates

### Context
- **Alaska (1867):** Secretary of State Seward purchased from Russia for $7.2M ("Seward's Folly").
- **Hawaii (1893):** American settlers overthrew the Hawaiian monarchy; Cleveland opposed annexation; McKinley later annexed it.

### Arguments FOR Imperialism
- **Economic:** Expand markets; secure raw materials; industrial overproduction needed foreign outlets.
- **Military:** **Alfred Thayer Mahan**, *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890) — argued a strong navy and coaling stations/overseas bases were essential for world power; directly shaped naval buildup and the push for Hawaii and Pacific bases.
- **Social/Racial:** Anglo-Saxon superiority; moral duty to spread Christianity and Western civilization ("civilizing mission"); popularized by Rudyard Kipling's poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899), written about the Philippines.
- **Social Darwinism:** U.S. expansion as natural selection on a global scale — stronger nations/races naturally dominate weaker ones (Josiah Strong's *Our Country*).
- **Political/strategic:** Senator **Henry Cabot Lodge** and others argued great powers needed colonies and naval stations to compete with European empires already carving up Africa and Asia.

### Arguments AGAINST
- Contradicted self-determination and democracy — a nation born of anti-colonial revolution should not rule colonies without consent of the governed.
- Rejected racial superiority theories.
- Isolationists invoked Washington's Farewell Address advice against permanent foreign entanglements.
- Financial burden of navy and overseas territories; feared costly colonial wars.
- **Anti-Imperialist League (1899):** organized opposition including **Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, William Jennings Bryan, Jane Addams,** and Samuel Gompers (AFL — feared cheap colonial labor competing with American workers). Carnegie offered to personally pay Spain's $20 million price for Philippine independence.
- **Insular Cases (1901, e.g. *Downes v. Bidwell*):** Supreme Court ruled the Constitution does not automatically extend full rights to residents of new territories ("the Constitution does not follow the flag") — territories like Puerto Rico and the Philippines were unincorporated, with Congress deciding what rights applied.

## 7.3 — Imperialism: The Progressive Era (The Spanish-American War and Foreign Policy)

### Causes of the Spanish-American War

| Cause | Details |
|---|---|
| Cuban Revolt | Spain's harsh response (Gen. Weyler — "The Butcher") created concentration camps; American sympathy |
| Yellow Journalism | Pulitzer (New York World) and Hearst (New York Journal) sensationalized Spanish atrocities |
| De Lôme Letter (1898) | Spanish diplomat criticized McKinley → outrage |
| **Sinking of the USS Maine (Feb 15, 1898)** | 260 Americans killed; yellow press blamed Spain (likely an accident) |

### The War Itself ("A Splendid Little War" — John Hay)
- Lasted about 10 weeks (April–August 1898); low U.S. casualties in combat (far more died of disease, e.g. yellow fever/typhoid, than in battle).
- **Commodore George Dewey** destroyed the Spanish fleet at the **Battle of Manila Bay** (Philippines) — first major action, showed the war's global reach beyond Cuba.
- **Theodore Roosevelt's Rough Riders** — volunteer cavalry regiment; charge up **San Juan Hill** (Cuba) made TR a national hero and launched his political career.

### Outcomes (Treaty of Paris, Dec 10, 1898)
- U.S. gained: Cuba's independence, **Puerto Rico, Guam**, control of **Philippines** ($20M to Spain).
- **Foraker Act (1900):** established civil government in Puerto Rico under U.S. control; Puerto Ricans gained U.S. citizenship later (Jones Act, 1917) but not full statehood or voting representation in Congress.

### Philippine-American War (1899–1902)
- **Emilio Aguinaldo** led Filipino resistance, expecting independence after helping defeat Spain; instead the U.S. annexed the islands, triggering a brutal guerrilla war.
- Bloodier and longer than the Spanish-American War itself — over 4,200 American and an estimated 200,000+ Filipino deaths (including from disease/famine).
- U.S. forces used tactics (reconcentration camps, torture such as the "water cure") that drew comparisons to Spain's conduct in Cuba — fueled Anti-Imperialist League criticism.
- Heated debate: annexation violated self-governance principles; **William Howard Taft** became the first civilian governor of the Philippines (1901), pursuing infrastructure and education reforms; full Philippine independence not granted until 1946.

### Cuba and the Platt Amendment (1901)
- Made Cuba a U.S. protectorate; allowed U.S. intervention; Guantanamo Bay naval base leased to the U.S. — remained in force until 1934.

### Open Door Policy (1899)
- Secretary of State John Hay — equal trading rights for all nations in China.

### Roosevelt's "Big Stick" Policy
- "Speak softly and carry a big stick" — military strength to achieve diplomatic goals.
- **Panama Canal:**
  - Roosevelt supported Panamanian independence from Colombia (1903).
  - Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty granted U.S. control of Canal Zone.
  - Canal completed **1914** — linked Atlantic and Pacific.

### Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine (1904)
- Prompted by European creditor nations threatening to intervene militarily in Venezuela and the Dominican Republic over unpaid debts.
- U.S. would intervene in European-Latin American conflicts to prevent European enforcement — reinterpreted the Monroe Doctrine from "keep Europe out" to "the U.S. may intervene first."
- Positioned U.S. as **police power** ("international police power") in Latin America; justified interventions in the **Dominican Republic (1905), Nicaragua, Haiti**, and elsewhere over the following decades.

### Other Foreign Policies

| Policy | President | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Gentlemen's Agreement (1907)** | Roosevelt | Restricted Japanese immigration; avoided official exclusion act |
| **Great White Fleet (1907–09)** | Roosevelt | Global naval tour; demonstrated U.S. naval power |
| **Dollar Diplomacy** | Taft | U.S. financial investment abroad replaces military action; encouraged bankers to invest in Latin America/East Asia (e.g., Nicaragua) so the U.S. gained influence through economic leverage rather than force — though troops were still sent when investments were threatened |
| **Moral Diplomacy** | Wilson | Promoted democracy; opposed imperialism; still intervened militarily in Mexico, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic when Wilson judged regimes illegitimate |
| **Jones Act (1916)** | Wilson | Philippines — full territorial status; male suffrage; promised eventual independence |

> **Compare:** Big Stick (military threat) → Dollar Diplomacy (economic leverage) → Moral Diplomacy (democracy promotion) all aimed at the same goal — U.S. dominance in Latin America and Asia — through different tools. All three still resulted in U.S. military interventions when other means failed.

## 7.4 — Progressivism

### Origins
- Middle-class Americans disturbed by big business power, economic disparities, political corruption.
- Embraced **pragmatism** (William James, John Dewey) — truth tested by practical outcomes.

### Muckrakers
- Journalists who exposed societal issues (coined by TR).
- Key works: Upton Sinclair (*The Jungle* — meatpacking), Ida Tarbell (Standard Oil).

### Political Reforms

| Reform | Details |
|---|---|
| Secret Ballot (Australian Ballot) | Voter privacy; reduced manipulation and bribery at the polls |
| Direct Primaries | Party members choose candidates directly, bypassing party bosses |
| **Initiative, Referendum, Recall** | Direct democracy tools championed by **Robert La Follette's "Wisconsin Idea"** — citizens propose laws (initiative), vote directly on laws (referendum), and remove officials from office (recall) |
| City Commission / City Manager Plans | Replaced corrupt political machines with nonpartisan, expert-run city government |
| **17th Amendment (1913)** | Direct election of U.S. Senators (previously chosen by state legislatures) |
| **18th Amendment (1920)** | Prohibition of alcohol |
| **19th Amendment (1920)** | Women's suffrage |

### Theodore Roosevelt's Presidency

**Square Deal ("Three C's"):**
1. **Control of Corporations** — trust-busting.
2. **Consumer Protection** — food safety.
3. **Conservation** of natural resources.

**Key Actions:**

| Action | Details |
|---|---|
| **1902 Coal Strike** | Mediated between miners and owners; 10% wage increase, 9-hour workday; first time president intervened in labor dispute |
| **Trust Busting** | Standard Oil lawsuit → breakup 1911 (Sherman Antitrust Act) |
| Elkins Act (1903) | Prohibited railroad rebates to favored customers |
| Hepburn Act (1906) | ICC set maximum railroad rates |
| Pure Food and Drug Act (1906) | Banned adulterated/mislabeled food and drugs → FDA |
| Meat Inspection Act (1906) | Federal inspection of meat processing plants |
| Newlands Reclamation Act | Funded irrigation in arid western lands |
| National parks/forests | Established numerous protected areas |

### William Taft
- Continued progressive policies; more cautious; actually broke up more trusts than TR (e.g. Standard Oil, American Tobacco, both 1911) but lacked TR's political skill.
- **Mann-Elkins Act (1910):** Extended ICC authority to telephone/telegraph.
- **16th Amendment (1913):** Federal graduated income tax.
- **Payne-Aldrich Tariff (1909):** Raised tariffs despite campaign promises to lower them — angered progressive Republicans.
- **Ballinger-Pinchot Affair (1909–10):** Taft's Interior Secretary Ballinger opened protected federal lands to development; conservationist Gifford Pinchot (a TR ally) accused him of favoring corporate interests and was fired — split the Republican Party between Taft's conservative wing and TR's progressive wing.

### Election of 1912
- TR vs. Taft (Republican split) → **Woodrow Wilson** (Democrat) won with only 42% of the popular vote but a landslide in the Electoral College (435 votes), because the Republican vote was split.
- **TR's "Bull Moose" Progressive Party:** formed after TR lost the Republican nomination to Taft; ran on the **New Nationalism** platform; TR won 27% of the popular vote — best-ever showing for a third party.
- **Eugene V. Debs (Socialist Party)** won about 6% of the popular vote — high-water mark for American socialism.

### New Nationalism vs. New Freedom

| | **New Nationalism (TR)** | **New Freedom (Wilson)** |
|---|---|---|
| View of big business | Bigness is inevitable and can be efficient — government should **regulate** trusts, not break them all up | Monopoly itself is the enemy — government should **break up** trusts and restore small-business competition |
| Government's role | Strong, activist federal government; expanded regulatory agencies | Limited federal government; remove barriers (tariffs, monopolies) to let a free market work |
| Social welfare | Included minimum wage, child labor laws, women's suffrage, social insurance | Focused mainly on economic/antitrust reform, less on social welfare initially |
| In practice | — | Once elected, Wilson governed more like a New Nationalist than he campaigned — the FTC (1914) *regulated* rather than dismantled big business, echoing TR's approach |

### Wilson's "New Freedom"

**Goal:** Dismantle the "triple wall of privilege" (tariffs, banks, trusts).

| Reform | Details |
|---|---|
| **Underwood Tariff (1913)** | First significant tariff reduction in 50+ years; graduated income tax to compensate for lost revenue |
| **Federal Reserve Act (1914)** | Created the Federal Reserve System — 12 district banks; central banking structure; adjusts the money supply and interest rates — still the basic structure of U.S. monetary policy today |
| **Federal Trade Commission (FTC, 1914)** | Investigate and prevent unfair trade practices |
| **Clayton Antitrust Act (1914)** | Strengthened Sherman Act by outlawing specific practices (price discrimination, interlocking directorates); exempted labor unions and strikes from antitrust prosecution — Samuel Gompers called it labor's "Magna Carta" |
| Federal Farm Loan Act | Low-interest loans for farmers |
| Keating-Owen Child Labor Act (1916) | Prohibited interstate sale of goods made by children under 14 (struck down in *Hammer v. Dagenhart*, 1918) |
| Adamson Act (1916) | Established 8-hour workday for interstate railroad workers, averting a national rail strike |

### Social Workers
- **Jane Addams (Hull House, Chicago, 1889)** — settlement house model; civic reform; child-labor laws; housing reform; women's rights.
- **Frances Perkins** and **Harry Hopkins** → later New Deal leaders (Perkins became the first female Cabinet member, Secretary of Labor).
- **Margaret Sanger** — opened the first U.S. birth control clinic (1916); founded what became Planned Parenthood; controversial even among reformers.

### NAACP and National Urban League
- **NAACP (1909):** Founded by W.E.B. Du Bois and white progressives in response to race riots; legal challenges against segregation and discrimination.
- **National Urban League (1911):** Helped African Americans transition to urban life amid the early Great Migration.
- **Ida B. Wells:** Investigative journalist and anti-lynching crusader (*Southern Horrors*, 1892); co-founder of the NAACP; also active in the women's suffrage movement, where she pushed back against segregation within the movement itself.

### African Americans in the Progressive Era
- **Booker T. Washington:** Economic self-help, vocational/industrial education (Tuskegee Institute); the "Atlanta Compromise" (1895) accepted social segregation in exchange for economic opportunity.
- **W.E.B. Du Bois:** Immediate civil rights, political participation, higher education for the "Talented Tenth"; co-founded the Niagara Movement (1905), precursor to the NAACP; publicly criticized Washington's gradualism.
- Progressive Era largely ignored racial inequalities — most Progressive reforms (e.g., women's suffrage campaigns in the South, labor protections) excluded or actively harmed Black Americans; Jim Crow and disenfranchisement (poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses) deepened during this period.

### Women's Suffrage
- **NAWSA** and more radical **National Woman's Party (Alice Paul)** pushed for constitutional amendment.
- **19th Amendment (1920):** Women's right to vote — aided by women's contributions in WWI.

## 7.5 — World War I: Military and Diplomacy

### Wilson's Neutrality
- Declared U.S. neutral at the outbreak of WWI.
- "Freedom of the seas" — neutral nations should trade without interference.
- British naval blockade and German submarine warfare complicated neutrality.

### Road to War

| Event | Date | Details |
|---|---|---|
| Lusitania Sinking | May 1915 | 1,198 passengers (128 Americans) killed by German U-boat |
| **Zimmermann Telegram** | March 1917 | Germany proposed military alliance with Mexico against the U.S. |
| Renewed Submarine Attacks | 1917 | Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare |
| Declaration of War | April 6, 1917 | Wilson: "make the world safe for democracy" |

### The American Expeditionary Force (AEF)
- **General John J. Pershing** commanded U.S. forces; insisted American troops fight as a distinct, unified army rather than being folded into depleted British/French units.
- **Selective Service Act (1917):** ~24 million men registered for the draft; about 2.8 million were drafted; roughly 4.7 million Americans served in total across all branches.
- Key U.S. engagements: **Château-Thierry** and **Belleau Wood** (June 1918) — helped stop the German spring offensive; **Second Battle of the Marne** (July 1918) — turning point that began the Allied counteroffensive; **Meuse-Argonne Offensive** (Sept–Nov 1918) — largest U.S. military operation to that point, helped force Germany to seek an armistice.
- Fresh American troops and industrial capacity tipped the stalemate of trench warfare in the Allies' favor; roughly 116,000 American deaths (many from the 1918 influenza pandemic as well as combat).
- **Armistice: November 11, 1918.**

### Wilson's Fourteen Points (1918)
- Transparent agreements ("open covenants, openly arrived at"); freedom of the seas; free trade; arms reduction; self-determination for European nationalities; adjustment of colonial claims; **League of Nations** (Point 14) — an international body to preserve peace collectively.
- Wilson wanted a "peace without victory," but the actual Treaty of Versailles disappointed him — the Allies (especially France, seeking security and revenge) demanded harsher terms.

### Treaty of Versailles (1919)
- Imposed harsh penalties on Germany: reparations, territorial losses, military restrictions.
- Included League of Nations and self-determination principles.

### Rejection of the Treaty
- **Irreconcilables:** Opposed League under any circumstances.
- **Reservationists (Lodge):** Demanded significant changes.
- Senate rejected the treaty; U.S. never joined the League of Nations.

## 7.6 — World War I: Home Front

### Mobilization
- **War Industries Board (Bernard Baruch):** Coordinated war production — set prices, allocated raw materials, standardized manufacturing; the most sweeping federal intervention in the economy to date, a model later echoed by New Deal and WWII agencies.
- **Herbert Hoover (Food Administration):** Voluntary conservation ("Meatless Mondays," "Wheatless Wednesdays"); increased food shipments to Allies without formal rationing.
- **Railroad Administration (McAdoo):** Government took control of railroads; standardized equipment.
- **National War Labor Board (Taft):** Resolved labor disputes; higher wages, shorter workdays; in exchange, unions largely agreed not to strike during the war.
- **Committee on Public Information (George Creel):** Propaganda agency; posters, films, and "Four-Minute Men" speakers maintained public support and sold the war as a moral crusade — also fueled suspicion of German-Americans and dissenters.

### Financing the War
- Raised **$33 billion**; **Liberty Bonds** encouraged citizen investment.

### Civil Liberties Curtailed

| Law / Case | Details |
|---|---|
| **Espionage Act (1917)** | Prohibited interference with military operations; harsh penalties |
| **Sedition Act (1918)** | Expanded to include disloyal speech about the government |
| **Schenck v. United States (1919)** | Holmes' "clear and present danger" test — speech can be restricted during wartime (Schenck distributed anti-draft flyers) |
| **Debs v. United States (1919)** | Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs convicted for an anti-war speech; sentenced to 10 years — later ran for president from prison in 1920, winning nearly 1 million votes |

### African Americans
- ~400,000 served in segregated units, including the celebrated **369th Infantry Regiment ("Harlem Hellfighters")**, decorated by France.
- **Great Migration:** ~1 million (eventually 6 million by mid-century) moved from the rural South to northern industrial cities (Chicago, Detroit, New York) — **pushed** by Jim Crow segregation, disenfranchisement, boll weevil damage to cotton, and lynching; **pulled** by wartime factory labor shortages (immigration from Europe dropped sharply during the war) and the hope of better wages/less overt racism.
- Migration reshaped Northern cities, fueled the later Harlem Renaissance, and contributed to postwar racial tension (competition for jobs/housing → riots like Chicago's Red Summer of 1919).

### Postwar Problems
- **Red Scare (1919):** Fear of communism → xenophobia, restrictive immigration.
- **Palmer Raids:** Mass arrests of anarchists, socialists; 6,000 arrests, 500 deported including Emma Goldman.

## 7.7 — 1920s: Innovations in Communication and Technology

### Roaring Twenties
- 64% rise in manufacturing output (1919–1929).
- Assembly line (Ford) → mass production across industries.
- Oil and electricity fueled economic growth; electrical generation grew 300%+.

### Key Economic Features

| Topic | Details |
|---|---|
| **Consumer Economy** | Electric appliances; buying on credit/installment plans expanded purchasing power — but left households and the broader economy dangerously overleveraged (a contributing cause of the 1929 crash) |
| **Automobiles** | Ford's Model T and moving assembly line slashed prices; transformed society; boosted steel, rubber, gasoline, suburbs; introduced commuting |
| **Advertising** | Grew into a major industry — created demand for new consumer goods, promoted a culture of consumption over thrift |
| **Farm Problems** | Crop prices fell after wartime demand collapsed; overproduction; rural America left behind by urban prosperity — a preview of nationwide economic trouble |
| **Labor Unions** | Membership declined roughly 20% due to "open shop" (anti-union) policies, welfare capitalism (employers offering benefits to discourage unionizing), and a hostile political/judicial climate |

### Technology and Culture
- **Radio** — mass medium alongside newspapers; created shared national culture (news, sports, entertainment) for the first time.
- **Movies/Hollywood** — silent films gave way to "talkies" by the end of the decade (*The Jazz Singer*, 1927); movie stars became national celebrities.
- **Charles Lindbergh (1927)** — first solo nonstop transatlantic flight (New York to Paris) in the *Spirit of St. Louis*; became an instant international celebrity, symbolizing the era's fascination with technology and individual achievement.
- **Jazz** — African American musicians (originating in New Orleans, spreading via the Great Migration); symbolized modern urban culture; gave the decade its nickname, the "Jazz Age."
- **Art Deco** — new architectural styles (e.g. the Chrysler Building) reflecting modernist, machine-age aesthetics.

## 7.8 — 1920s: Cultural and Political Controversies

### Modernism vs. Fundamentalism

| Group | Beliefs |
|---|---|
| **Modernists** | Urban Protestants; reconciled religion with science; less literal Bible interpretation |
| **Fundamentalists** | Rural conservatives; literal Bible; opposed evolution as threat to morality |

### Scopes Trial (1925)
- John Scopes charged for teaching evolution in Tennessee.
- Clarence Darrow (defense) vs. William Jennings Bryan (prosecution).
- Scopes convicted (overturned on technicality); illustrated cultural battle over science and religion.

### Prohibition
- **18th Amendment + Volstead Act** — outlawed manufacture, sale, transport of alcohol.
- Led to speakeasies, bootlegging, organized crime (Al Capone).
- Repealed by **21st Amendment (1933)**.

### Immigration Restrictions
- **Emergency Quota Act (1921):** first hard numerical cap on immigration — capped each nationality at 3% of that group's U.S. population per the **1910 census**.
- **Immigration Act of 1924 (Johnson-Reed/National Origins Act):** tightened the quota to **2% of the 1890 census** — deliberately rolled the baseline back before the mass arrival of southern/eastern Europeans, sharply favoring northern/western Europe; capped total European immigration and **excluded Asian immigration entirely**.
- Quota laws targeted "undesirable" immigrants from southern/eastern Europe (Italians, Poles, Jews) and Asia — reflected nativism, eugenics-influenced racial theory, and postwar Red Scare fears.

### Sacco and Vanzetti (1920–1927)
- Italian immigrant anarchists **Nicola Sacco** and **Bartolomeo Vanzetti** convicted of armed robbery/murder on thin evidence; many believed they were convicted primarily for their ethnicity, immigrant status, and radical politics amid Red Scare nativism.
- Executed 1927 despite international protests — became a symbol (like the KKK's revival and immigration quotas) of the decade's anti-immigrant, anti-radical backlash.

### Revival of the KKK
- Expanded to target Jews, Catholics, immigrants (not just African Americans).
- Millions of members at peak; criminal activities led to decline by late 1920s.

### Harlem Renaissance
- Flourishing of African American culture — poetry, music, art — centered in Harlem, NYC; fueled by the Great Migration's concentration of Black artists, writers, and intellectuals in Northern cities.
- **Langston Hughes, Duke Ellington, Louis Armstrong, Zora Neale Hurston** — gained national recognition.
- Jazz became defining feature of the period.
- **Marcus Garvey and the UNIA (Universal Negro Improvement Association):** promoted **Black nationalism, racial pride, and "Back-to-Africa"** separatism as an alternative to both Washington's accommodationism and Du Bois's integrationism; built a mass following among urban working-class Black Americans before Garvey's fraud conviction and deportation (1927).

### The "New Woman" and Changing Culture
- **Flappers** — young women with bobbed hair, shorter skirts, and more public independence (smoking, drinking, dancing) symbolized changing gender norms after the 19th Amendment, though the image applied mostly to urban, middle-class women.
- **The "Lost Generation"** — writers disillusioned by WWI and consumer culture, including **F. Scott Fitzgerald** (*The Great Gatsby*, 1925) and **Ernest Hemingway**, often wrote from expatriate communities in Europe.

### Republican Dominance

| President | Key Notes |
|---|---|
| **Warren Harding (1921–23)** | "Return to normalcy"; Teapot Dome Scandal — Interior Secretary Albert Fall illegally leased federal oil reserves (Teapot Dome, WY) to private companies for bribes; first Presidential Cabinet member convicted of a crime |
| **Calvin Coolidge** | "The business of America is business" — laissez-faire; tax cuts; vetoed farm/veteran bills; extended 1920s prosperity |
| **Herbert Hoover** | Capable administrator (former Food Administration head); elected 1928 over Democrat **Al Smith** (first Catholic major-party nominee — his religion and opposition to Prohibition cost him votes in the South); soon faced Great Depression |

## 7.9 — The Great Depression

### Stock Market Crash (1929)
- **Black Thursday (Oct 24)** — massive sell-off.
- **Black Tuesday (Oct 29)** — complete collapse.

### Underlying Causes

| Cause | Details |
|---|---|
| Uneven income distribution | Richest 5% earned 33%+ of all income |
| Stock market speculation / buying on margin | Prices fell → financial ruin |
| Excessive use of credit | Over-borrowing → loan defaults, bank failures |
| Overproduction of consumer goods | Surplus → financial strain |
| Weak farm economy | Ongoing issues since 1920s |
| Government policies | Lax regulation; high tariffs; Federal Reserve gold standard |
| Global economic problems | Europe still recovering from WWI |

### Effects
- U.S. GNP: $104B (1929) → $56B (1932).
- **25% unemployment by 1933** (13 million workers).
- "Hoovervilles" — shantytowns of homeless, named mockingly after the president.
- 20% of banks failed → 10 million savings accounts erased; no deposit insurance existed yet, so failures wiped out families' savings entirely.

### Historiographical Debate: What Really Caused It?
- **Monetarist view (Milton Friedman & Anna Schwartz):** the Federal Reserve's failure to act — letting the money supply contract by about a third — turned an ordinary recession into a catastrophic depression; banking panics were the key trigger.
- **Keynesian view:** a collapse in aggregate demand (falling investment and consumer spending after the crash) was the root cause; only large-scale government spending (ultimately achieved via WWII) could restore full employment.
- Both views agree the Depression was a *global* crisis, not purely a U.S. failure — European economies were still fragile from WWI debts and reparations, so the crisis fed on itself internationally.

### Hoover's Policies and the Bonus Army
- Initially: voluntary action, private charity, "rugged individualism"; opposed direct federal aid as damaging to self-reliance and the federal budget.
- **Hawley-Smoot Tariff (1930):** Highest tariffs in U.S. history → retaliatory tariffs from trading partners → reduced global trade, deepening the worldwide downturn.
- **Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC, 1932):** Emergency loans to banks, railroads, and industries ("trickle-down" — money would supposedly flow down to help ordinary people, but this was slow and limited).
- **Bonus Army (Summer 1932):** ~15,000–20,000 WWI veterans and families marched on Washington, D.C., demanding early payment of a bonus promised for 1945; Hoover refused, and after a clash, ordered the army — led by **General Douglas MacArthur** — to forcibly clear their camps with tanks and tear gas; the violent eviction of veterans devastated Hoover's public image heading into the 1932 election.

## 7.10 — The New Deal

### FDR's Election (1932)
- Promised "new deal"; repealed Prohibition; aid for unemployed; reduced government spending.
- Won decisively: ~60% popular vote; all but 6 states.

### New Deal Philosophy
- **Three R's:** Relief (unemployed), Recovery (economy), Reform (economic institutions).
- The New Deal abandoned laissez-faire in favor of active, sustained federal intervention in the economy — a permanent shift in the size and role of the federal government (the central theme of Period 7).

### First vs. Second New Deal

| | **First New Deal (1933–34)** | **Second New Deal (1935–38)** |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Immediate **relief** and short-term **recovery** — stop the bleeding (bank runs, unemployment, farm/industrial collapse) | **Reform** and permanent **relief** — build lasting institutions, especially a social safety net and labor rights |
| Tone | Cooperated with business (NRA codes written with industry input) | More confrontational toward business and the wealthy; embraced labor unions and "soak the rich" taxation |
| Key programs | FERA, PWA, CCC, TVA, NRA, AAA, Glass-Steagall, FDIC, SEC | WPA, Wagner Act (NLRA), Social Security Act, Revenue Act of 1935 ("Wealth Tax Act") |
| Legacy | Many programs struck down by the Supreme Court as unconstitutional overreach | Social Security and the Wagner Act became lasting pillars of the modern American welfare/labor state |

### First New Deal Programs

| Program | Director | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **FERA** | Harry Hopkins | Federal funds to local governments for direct relief |
| **PWA** | Harold Ickes | Infrastructure projects (roads, bridges) |
| **CCC** | — | Young men on conservation projects |
| **TVA** | — | Regional development; dams and power plants for Tennessee Valley |
| **Glass-Steagall Act** | — | Banking regulations; restricted use of customer deposits |
| **FDIC** | — | Government insurance for bank deposits |
| **SEC** | — | Regulated stock market; corporate transparency |
| **AAA** | — | Paid farmers to reduce crop production → raise prices (struck down 1936, *United States v. Butler* — unconstitutional use of taxing power) |
| **NRA** | — | Set fair-competition codes, minimum wages, and labor standards for industries (struck down 1935, *Schechter Poultry v. United States* — unconstitutional delegation of legislative power to the executive) |
| Bank Holiday | Roosevelt | Temporarily closed all banks (March 1933); Emergency Banking Act → inspected banks reopened with restored public confidence |
| Indian Reorganization Act (1934) | — | Ended the Dawes Act's allotment policy; restored some tribal self-government and communal landholding |

### Fireside Chats
- Radio broadcasts by FDR; first on March 12, 1933 (explaining the bank holiday); restored public confidence in banking and became a defining tool for building direct public trust in the president.

### Critics of the New Deal
- **From the left/populist:** **Huey Long** ("Share Our Wealth" — guaranteed income funded by taxing the rich) and **Father Charles Coughlin** (radio priest, initially pro-New Deal, later attacked it as too favorable to bankers, drifted toward antisemitic rhetoric) argued the New Deal didn't go far enough to redistribute wealth; **Dr. Francis Townsend** pushed a generous old-age pension plan, pressuring Congress toward Social Security.
- **From the right:** the **American Liberty League** (conservative Democrats and business leaders) argued the New Deal was unconstitutional government overreach that threatened free enterprise and individual liberty.

### Second New Deal Programs

| Program | Details |
|---|---|
| **WPA (1935–40)** | Millions of jobs; large-scale public works; supported unemployed artists and writers |
| **Wagner Act (NLRA, 1935)** | Guaranteed union rights and collective bargaining; created the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to enforce it — union membership surged afterward |
| **Social Security Act (1935)** | Insurance for retirees 65+; unemployment and disability benefits; funded by a regressive payroll tax (excluded many agricultural and domestic workers — disproportionately Black and female — at Southern Democrats' insistence) |
| **Revenue Act (1935) ("Wealth Tax Act")** | Increased taxes on high incomes, large gifts, capital gains — a response to Huey Long-style pressure to "soak the rich" |

### Rise of the CIO
- Wagner Act protections fueled a wave of unionization; the **Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO)**, led by **John L. Lewis**, broke from the more conservative craft-based AFL to organize industrial workers (steel, auto, rubber) by entire workplace regardless of skill.
- Sit-down strikes (e.g., the 1936–37 Flint, Michigan GM strike) won recognition for the United Auto Workers — a major labor victory of the era.

### Election of 1936 and Coalition
- FDR reelected; New Deal coalition: Solid South, urban ethnic groups, Midwestern farmers, labor unions, liberals, northern Black voters (shifted from Republican to Democrat).

### Supreme Court Challenges
- Court struck down NRA (*Schechter*, 1935) and AAA (*Butler*, 1936) as unconstitutional.
- FDR's Judicial Procedures Reform Bill (1937), the so-called **"court-packing" plan**, proposed adding up to 6 new justices (one for every sitting justice over 70) — widely criticized as a power grab even by New Deal allies, and defeated in Congress; badly damaged FDR's political capital despite his landslide 1936 reelection.
- Court later upheld the Wagner Act and Social Security Act anyway (the "switch in time that saved nine") — some justices began ruling more favorably toward New Deal legislation around this time, though historians debate how much the court-packing threat actually influenced them versus a coincidental shift in the Court's makeup and legal reasoning.
- **The "Roosevelt Recession" (1937–38):** FDR cut relief spending and raised taxes to balance the budget, prematurely believing recovery was underway; the economy relapsed sharply — evidence Keynesians cite that sustained deficit spending, not budget balancing, was needed to fully end the Depression.

### Fair Labor Standards Act (1938)
- Minimum wage; standard workweek with overtime; restrictions on child labor.

### Social Conditions

| Issue | Details |
|---|---|
| Dust Bowl | Severe droughts in Great Plains → migration (depicted in Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath*) |
| Women | Lower wages; Eleanor Roosevelt advocated for them |
| African Americans | Higher unemployment; often excluded from relief programs |

> **New Deal criticized** for not being an equal opportunity for all.

### Limitations
- Did not fully end the Depression; economy remained unstable — unemployment was still around 15% in 1940; full recovery came only with WWII mobilization.
- Political resistance by 1937 (a conservative coalition of Republicans + conservative Southern Democrats blocked further New Deal legislation).
- Focus shifted to threats from Nazi Germany by late 1930s.

### Historiographical Debate: Did the New Deal "Work"?
- **Mainstream view:** the New Deal eased suffering, prevented total economic collapse, and built lasting reforms (Social Security, FDIC, SEC, labor rights) — but did not, by itself, end the Depression; only WWII spending achieved full employment.
- **Conservative/critical view:** New Deal spending was inefficient, prolonged uncertainty for business (discouraging investment), and the Depression persisted longer in the U.S. than in some other industrialized nations.
- **Consensus point tested on the AP exam:** whatever its economic effectiveness, the New Deal permanently redefined the relationship between the federal government and the economy/citizens — a "third New Deal" legacy of regulation and social insurance that outlasted the 1930s.

## 7.11 — Interwar Foreign Policy

### Disarmament Efforts (1920s)
- **Washington Conference (1921):** Five-Power, Four-Power, Nine-Power Treaties; Pacific stability.
- **Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928):** Renounce war as national policy; no enforcement mechanism.
- **Dawes Plan (1924):** Germany paid reparations → Allies paid U.S. war debts; collapsed after 1929 crash.

### FDR's Foreign Policy
- **Good Neighbor Policy (1933):** Ended U.S. intervention in Latin American affairs.

### Rise of Fascism and Militarism

| Country | Leader | Key Events |
|---|---|---|
| **Italy** | Mussolini | Fascist regime (1922); aggressive nationalism |
| **Germany** | Hitler | Nazi Party gained power (1933); anti-Semitic policies; militaristic expansion |
| **Japan** | Military leaders | Invaded Manchuria (1931); invaded China; Second Sino-Japanese War (1937); Nanjing Massacre |

### Early U.S. Responses to Aggression
- **Stimson Doctrine (1932):** the U.S. would not recognize territorial changes achieved by force — a response to Japan's invasion of Manchuria; largely symbolic, since it carried no enforcement mechanism.
- **Munich Agreement (1938):** Britain and France appeased Hitler by allowing Germany to annex the Sudetenland (Czechoslovakia) in exchange for a promise of no further expansion; the promise was broken within months — became the classic cautionary example against appeasement, later invoked to justify firmer U.S. resistance to aggression.

### American Isolationism
- Nye Committee (1934): concluded WWI had been driven largely by profit-seeking bankers and arms manufacturers ("merchants of death") — deepened public disillusionment with WWI intervention.
- **Neutrality Acts (1935–37):** No arms shipments, no loans, no travel on belligerent ships, no financial transactions with warring nations — intended to avoid the mistakes (loans, trade ties) that had drawn the U.S. into WWI.
- **America First Committee (founded 1940):** prominent isolationist organization; aviator **Charles Lindbergh** was its most famous spokesman, arguing the U.S. should stay out of Europe's war — collapsed after Pearl Harbor.

### Roosevelt's Adjustments
- **Quarantine Speech (1937):** Collective quarantine of aggressors → public opposition.
- **Cash and Carry (1939):** Belligerents could buy U.S. arms with their own transport/funds (favored Britain).
- **Selective Service Act (1940):** First peacetime draft.
- **Lend-Lease Act (1941):** Britain and Allies could purchase military supplies on credit.
- **Atlantic Charter (1941):** FDR and Churchill outlined post-war vision: self-determination, free trade.

### Pearl Harbor
- **December 7, 1941:** Japan attacked U.S. naval base → Roosevelt's "Infamy Speech" → Congress declared war on Japan.
- Germany and Italy declared war on the U.S. → full U.S. entry into WWII.

### Formation of the United Nations
- Discussions at Dumbarton Oaks (1944) → 50 nations drafted UN Charter in San Francisco (April 1945).
- U.S. Senate approved membership — contrast with League of Nations rejection after WWI.
- UN officially formed **October 24, 1945**.

## 7.12 — World War II: Mobilization

### U.S. Government Agencies
- **WPB (War Production Board, 1942):** Converted peacetime industries to war production (car factories built tanks and planes).
- **OPA (Office of Price Administration):** Froze prices, wages, rents; rationed meat, sugar, gasoline, tires → black market for scarce goods.
- Financed partly through **war bonds** (patriotic mass-marketing campaigns) and partly through the **Revenue Act of 1942**, which for the first time made the income tax apply broadly to the middle class (introducing payroll withholding) rather than just the wealthy.

### Economic Impact
- By 1944: unemployment virtually **eradicated** — **WWII ended the Great Depression**, finally validating (for many economists) the Keynesian argument that only massive government spending could restore full employment.
- U.S. war-related industrial output in 1944 **doubled that of all Axis powers combined** — the "Arsenal of Democracy."
- Manhattan Project: top-secret program that developed the first atomic weapons (Gen. Leslie Groves, physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer).

### Society During WWII

| Group | Experience |
|---|---|
| Women | Filled industrial jobs; "Rosie the Riveter"; ~350,000 served in uniform in newly created non-combat branches (**WACs** — Women's Army Corps, **WAVES** — Navy); boosted the postwar case for equality, though most women were pushed back out of factory jobs after the war |
| African Americans | 1.5 million migrated north/west for industrial jobs (continuing the Great Migration); **A. Philip Randolph** threatened a mass march on Washington to protest discrimination in defense industries, pressuring FDR to issue **Executive Order 8802 (1941)**, banning discrimination in defense-industry hiring and creating the Fair Employment Practice Committee (military remained segregated); the **Tuskegee Airmen** became the first African American combat aviators; "Double V" campaign (victory overseas + equality at home); race riots in NYC and Detroit (1943) |
| Mexican Americans | **Bracero Program (1942):** brought Mexican agricultural workers to the U.S. to fill wartime labor shortages; **Zoot Suit Riots (1943, Los Angeles):** white servicemen attacked Mexican American youths, revealing wartime racial tensions on the home front |
| Native Americans | **Navajo Code Talkers** used their language to create an unbreakable U.S. military code in the Pacific theater |

### Japanese Internment
- **Executive Order 9066 (Feb 1942):** Forced relocation/internment of 100,000+ Japanese Americans (2/3 U.S. citizens).
- **Korematsu v. United States (1944):** Supreme Court upheld internment 6-3 — national security outweighed individual rights (later widely criticized).

## 7.13 — World War II: Military

### European Theater
- **German advance halted (1942–43):** Soviet victory at the **Battle of Stalingrad** — turning point on the Eastern Front; U.S./British forces campaigned in **North Africa (1942–43)** against Rommel's Afrika Korps, then invaded **Italy (1943)**, forcing Mussolini's fall.
- Allied strategic bombing campaigns reduced Germany's industrial war capacity.
- **D-Day (June 6, 1944):** Amphibious invasion of Normandy under Gen. **Dwight D. Eisenhower** — opened the long-awaited "second front" in Western Europe.
- **Battle of the Bulge (Dec 1944–Jan 1945):** Germany's last major offensive in the West; failed, hastening the collapse of the Third Reich.
- **Hitler committed suicide (April 1945)** → unconditional German surrender **May 7, 1945 (V-E Day)**.
- **Holocaust:** U.S. and Allied troops liberated concentration camps and uncovered the systematic genocide of 6 million Jews and millions of others (Roma, disabled people, political prisoners) — knowledge of the full scale shocked the world and later shaped U.S. support for the state of Israel and human-rights frameworks.

### Pacific Theater
- **Battle of Midway (June 1942):** Decisive U.S. naval victory (helped by broken Japanese codes) that destroyed four Japanese carriers and turned the tide of the Pacific War.
- **Guadalcanal (1942–43):** First major U.S. offensive in the Pacific, beginning the push back against Japanese expansion.
- **Island Hopping Strategy:** Attacked strategic islands (e.g. **Iwo Jima, Okinawa** — 1945, extremely costly battles) that could support further advances; bypassed heavily fortified positions to save time and lives.
- **Kamikaze attacks** — Japanese suicide pilots; notably at Leyte Gulf and Okinawa; American planners cited the ferocity of these late battles as evidence an invasion of the Japanese home islands would cost enormous casualties — part of the rationale for using the atomic bomb.

### Atomic Bombs
- **Trinity Test:** July 16, 1945, New Mexico — first successful atomic bomb test.
- **Hiroshima:** August 6, 1945.
- **Nagasaki:** August 9, 1945.
- ~250,000 Japanese casualties; Japan surrendered **August 15, 1945**.
- **Formal surrender:** September 2, 1945, aboard USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay.

## 7.14 — Postwar Diplomacy

### Wartime Conferences

| Conference | Date | Key Outcomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Tehran** | Nov 1943 | Planned D-Day; Soviet entry against Japan |
| **Yalta** | Feb 1945 | Division of Germany; free elections in Eastern Europe (Soviets later violated); Soviet entry vs. Japan; United Nations groundwork |
| **Potsdam** | July 1945 | Finalized Japan surrender demands; emerging U.S.-Soviet tensions |

### Post-War Economic Landscape
- U.S. infrastructure intact (only major combatant not bombed at home) → dominant economic power, producing roughly half the world's industrial output by 1945.
- **Bretton Woods Conference (1944):** Established the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** and **World Bank**; pegged international currencies to the U.S. dollar (itself pegged to gold) — placed the U.S. dollar at the center of the postwar global economy.
- **GI Bill (Servicemen's Readjustment Act, 1944):** Funded college education, low-interest home and business loans, and unemployment benefits for returning veterans — fueled the postwar suburban boom and expansion of the middle class (though benefits were often unequally administered, excluding many Black veterans in practice).
- Europe devastated → Marshall Plan (later, 1948) to rebuild Western Europe.

## 7.15 — Causation in Period 7

### How the Eras of Period 7 Connect
- **Imperialism → WWI entry:** overseas expansion (Spanish-American War, naval buildup under Mahan's influence) built the military and diplomatic habits — and the sense of a "world power" identity — that made Wilson's 1917 intervention in a European war thinkable, even after decades of stated neutrality.
- **Progressivism → New Deal roots:** Progressive-era precedents (federal regulation of business, income tax via the 16th Amendment, social welfare experiments like workers' compensation) provided both the ideological justification and some of the personnel (Frances Perkins, Harry Hopkins came out of settlement-house Progressivism) for the New Deal's later, much larger expansion of federal power.
- **WWI home front → WWII home front:** War Industries Board-style federal economic coordination, propaganda (CPI), and civil liberties restrictions (Espionage/Sedition Acts) previewed — and were consciously copied or avoided — during WWII mobilization (e.g., WPB echoed the WIB; Japanese internment echoed WWI-era suspicion of "enemy" ethnic groups, but on a far larger, more coercive scale).
- **WWI** left unresolved global tensions (harsh Treaty of Versailles, German resentment that fed Nazi propaganda) and fueled 1920s American isolationism and disillusionment (the "Lost Generation," Senate rejection of the League of Nations) — directly shaping the reluctant, delayed U.S. response to fascism in the 1930s.
- **1920s economic imbalances** — stock speculation on margin, a weak farm economy, uneven income distribution, easy consumer credit, and a Federal Reserve slow to respond — directly caused the **Great Depression**; Hoover's limited-government response proved inadequate to a crisis of this scale.
- The **Depression's severity** forced an unprecedented expansion of federal power under the **New Deal** (First New Deal relief/recovery → Second New Deal's lasting reforms like Social Security and the Wagner Act), permanently changing the relationship between government, the economy, and citizens — even though the New Deal alone did not fully end the Depression.
- **WWII mobilization** finally ended the Depression (validating Keynesian arguments about deficit spending) and left the U.S. as the world's dominant economic and military power (Bretton Woods, atomic monopoly, intact industrial base), setting up its Cold War rivalry with the Soviet Union in Period 8.

### A Recurring Pattern
- Notice the repeated cycle across Period 7: **crisis → expanded federal power → backlash/limits → next crisis expands it further.** Progressive regulation → WWI centralization → 1920s partial retreat (laissez-faire Republicans) → Depression discredits limited government → New Deal expansion → WWII cements it. This pattern of ratcheting government growth (rather than smooth, linear change) is a common AP essay (LEQ) framework for Period 7.
