# Period 9 — Global Role (1980–Present)

---

## 9.1 — Contextualizing Period 9

### The Conservative Turn
- The 1970s brought stagflation, the Vietnam hangover, and Watergate-driven distrust of government — discrediting New Deal/Great Society liberalism for many voters.
- Reagan's 1980 landslide reflected a broader **conservative resurgence**: lower taxes, deregulation, a stronger military, and traditional social values.
- The Cold War entered its final decade, ending in Soviet collapse (1991) and leaving the U.S. as the world's sole superpower.
- This period covers the conservative era, globalization, and the challenges (terrorism, economic crises, political polarization) of the 21st century.

### Roots of the Conservative Resurgence (Pre-1980)
- **Tax revolt:** California's **Proposition 13 (1978)** slashed property taxes and capped future increases — model for anti-tax politics nationwide.
- **Sagebrush Rebellion:** Western resistance to federal land-use regulation fed into calls for deregulation and states' rights.
- **New Right coalition** fused three strands that Reagan would unite in 1980:
  - Economic conservatives (business groups, supply-siders) wanting lower taxes and less regulation.
  - Foreign-policy hawks alarmed by détente's collapse and Soviet assertiveness.
  - Social/religious conservatives reacting against the sexual revolution, *Roe v. Wade* (1973), and the stalled **Equal Rights Amendment**.
- **Religious Right:** Evangelical organizations like the **Moral Majority (Jerry Falwell, 1979)** mobilized conservative Christian voters around abortion, school prayer, and "family values."
- Think tanks (**Heritage Foundation**, American Enterprise Institute) supplied the policy ideas — deregulation, supply-side tax theory — that the Reagan administration would implement.

### International Context Entering the 1980s
- **Iran Hostage Crisis (1979–1981):** 52 Americans held for 444 days after the Iranian Revolution; Carter's failed rescue attempt and inability to free them damaged his presidency and became a symbol of American weakness abroad.
- **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (December 1979)** ended the détente of the 1970s, prompted a U.S. grain embargo and Olympic boycott (1980 Moscow Games), and revived Cold War tensions that Reagan would escalate further.
- **OPEC oil shocks** (1973, 1979) fueled stagflation and underscored American dependence on foreign energy, feeding public frustration with the status quo.
- Together, these crises made 1980 a "referendum on American decline" — the backdrop against which Reagan's optimistic, assertive nationalism appealed to voters.

## 9.2 — Reagan and Conservatism

### Reagan's Rise
- Former actor and California governor; prominent conservative figure.
- Gained national attention in **Goldwater's 1964 campaign**; nearly secured 1976 Republican nomination.
- **Election of 1980:** Defeated incumbent Jimmy Carter (weakened by stagflation and the Iran hostage crisis) and third-party candidate John Anderson; won 44 states and 489 electoral votes.
- Won presidency in **1980**; Iranian hostages released on inauguration day (minutes after Reagan was sworn in).
- Survived assassination attempt early in presidency (March 1981) → boosted popularity.
- Coalition brought together economic conservatives, Cold War hawks, and the religious right (Moral Majority) — the "**three-legged stool**" of modern conservatism.

### The Reagan Revolution — Goals
1. Reduce size of federal government.
2. Lower taxes.
3. Cut welfare spending.
4. Strengthen the military.
5. Create a more conservative federal judiciary.

### Reaganomics (Supply-Side Economics)
- Tax cuts and reduced government spending → stimulate private investment and economic growth.
- Critics called it **"trickle-down economics"** — benefited the wealthy with hope prosperity would filter down.
- Also known as the **Laffer Curve** theory (economist Arthur Laffer): argued that lower tax rates could eventually increase total tax revenue by spurring growth.

> [!note] Historiographical Debate — Did Reaganomics Work?
> Supporters point to the strong growth of 1983–1989 and falling inflation as proof supply-side cuts revived the economy. Critics counter that growth relied more on the Fed's inflation fight and heavy deficit spending (especially on the military) than on tax cuts, and that Reaganomics tripled the national debt while sharply widening income inequality. Both readings are testable — know the evidence for each side rather than a single verdict.

### Federal Tax Reduction
- **Economic Recovery Tax Act (1981):** 25% personal income tax cut over 3 years; reduced corporate and capital gains taxes; top marginal rate fell from 70% to 50%.
- **Tax Reform Act (1986):** Simplified the tax code and cut the top marginal rate further, to 28% — the lowest since before the Great Depression — while closing some loopholes.

### Spending
- Domestic program cuts >$40 billion (food stamps, student loans, mass transportation).
- **Military spending increased dramatically.**
- Bipartisan bill strengthened Social Security (raised retirement age, taxed some benefits).

### Deregulation
- Eased restrictions on savings and loans, corporate mergers, environmental protections, auto emissions.
- Interior Secretary James Watt — opened federal lands for coal, timber, offshore oil.

### Labor
- Fired striking air traffic controllers (PATCO) → decertified union.
- Union membership: 30%+ (1962) → ~12% (late 1990s).

### Recession and Recovery
- **1982 Recession:** Bank failures; 11% unemployment.
- **1983 Recovery:** Inflation below 4%; economy rebounded; income gap widened.

### Budget and Trade Deficits
- National debt tripled: $900B → $2.7 trillion.
- Trade deficit grew to $150B annually.
- **Gramm-Rudman-Hollings Act** — mandated spending cuts and tax increases.

### Election of 1984
- Reagan (R) vs. **Walter Mondale (D)**, who chose **Geraldine Ferraro** as the first female VP nominee of a major party.
- Reagan won in a landslide — 49 of 50 states, 525 electoral votes — riding the economic recovery and "**Morning in America**" campaign optimism.

### The Reagan Judiciary
- Appointed **Sandra Day O'Connor (1981)** — first woman on the Supreme Court.
- Appointed **Antonin Scalia** and **Anthony Kennedy**, shifting the federal judiciary rightward for a generation; fulfilled goal #5 of the Reagan Revolution (see above).

### Iran-Contra Affair (1986–1987)
- **Boland Amendment (1982–84):** Congress banned U.S. military aid to the Nicaraguan **Contras**, right-wing rebels fighting the leftist Sandinista government.
- Reagan officials (notably NSC aide **Lt. Col. Oliver North**) secretly sold arms to Iran — partly to secure release of American hostages held in Lebanon — and illegally diverted the profits to fund the Contras, evading the Boland Amendment.
- Scandal raised serious questions about **executive power and accountability**; congressional hearings (1987) implicated senior officials, though Reagan denied direct knowledge.
- Damaged Reagan's credibility in his final years but did not derail his presidency.

### AIDS Crisis
- The AIDS epidemic emerged in the early 1980s; the Reagan administration was widely criticized for a slow, underfunded federal response and for Reagan's public silence on the issue for years, as the disease disproportionately struck the gay community.
- Activist groups (e.g., **ACT UP**, founded 1987) pushed for research funding and faster drug approval.

### 1988 Election
- **George H.W. Bush (R):** Promised "Read my lips — no new taxes."
- **Michael Dukakis (D):** Portrayed as soft on crime and defense.
- Bush won 426 electoral votes — largely a continuation of Reagan-era conservatism.
- Bush later broke his tax pledge by signing a 1990 budget deal raising taxes to address the deficit — angered the conservative base and hurt his 1992 reelection bid (see 9.4).

### Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA, 1990)
- Prohibited discrimination against citizens with physical and mental disabilities in hiring, transportation, and public accommodation.

### Civil Rights Developments

| Group | Key Events |
|---|---|
| **Women** | Increased workforce participation; pay gap narrowed; #MeToo movement (2017) exposed sexual harassment |
| **African Americans** | Rodney King beating (1991); acquittal sparked national conversation; Black Lives Matter (2013); George Floyd's death (2020) |
| **LGBT** | Clinton's "don't ask, don't tell" (military); hate crimes law (2009); **Obergefell v. Hodges (2015)** — legalized same-sex marriage nationwide (14th Amendment) |

## 9.3 — The End of the Cold War

### Reagan's Military Buildup
- Increased defense spending: $171B (1981) → $300B+ (1985).
- New weapons: B-1 bomber, MX missile.
- **Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI / "Star Wars"):** Missile defense via lasers and particle beams; critics worried it would escalate arms race.
- **"Evil Empire" speech (1983):** Reagan branded the USSR morally illegitimate, sharpening Cold War rhetoric before later softening his tone as talks with Gorbachev progressed.

### The Reagan Doctrine
- Beyond containment, Reagan committed the U.S. to actively **aiding anti-communist insurgencies** worldwide to roll back Soviet influence:
  - The **Contras** in Nicaragua against the Sandinista government (see Iran-Contra, 9.2).
  - The **mujahideen** in Afghanistan against Soviet occupation (covert CIA funding/arms).
  - Anti-communist forces in Angola and elsewhere.
- Cause and effect: this proxy-war strategy raised the cost of Soviet global commitments at the same time the USSR's economy was stagnating — a major contributor to Gorbachev's push for reform.

### Improved U.S.-Soviet Relations
- **Mikhail Gorbachev (Soviet leader, 1985):**
  - **Glasnost:** Political openness.
  - **Perestroika:** Economic restructuring (free-market practices).
  - Sought to end costly arms race.
- **Reagan at Berlin Wall (1987):** "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall."

### Summit Meetings

| Summit | Year | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Geneva | 1985 | Cultural/scientific/environmental agreements |
| Reykjavik | 1986 | Less successful; disagreements on missile defense |
| Washington, D.C. | 1987 | **INF Agreement** — destroyed intermediate-range missiles |

### Collapse of Soviet Communism
- **Fall of the Berlin Wall (November 9, 1989):** East German government opened crossings amid mass protests; Germans tore down the wall — the single most iconic image of communism's collapse in Eastern Europe. Germany formally reunified in October 1990.
- A wave of largely peaceful revolutions swept Soviet satellite states (Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Romania) out of communist rule in 1989.
- Nationalist movements overwhelmed Gorbachev.
- Baltic states declared independence (1990).
- **Failed coup (1991)** → Soviet Union dissolved **December 1991**.
- **Boris Yeltsin** formed **Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS)** with 9 former Soviet republics.
- Yeltsin disbanded Communist Party; began democracy and free-market economy.

> [!note] Historiographical Debate — Who "Won" the Cold War?
> One view credits Reagan's military buildup and hardline rhetoric with forcing the USSR into an arms race it could not afford, precipitating collapse. Another emphasizes internal Soviet causes — a stagnant command economy, the costs of Afghanistan, and Gorbachev's own reforms (glasnost/perestroika) unleashing forces he couldn't control. Most historians now treat it as multi-causal rather than a single "victory."

### End of the Cold War (Officially)
- **START I (1991):** Bush and Gorbachev — nuclear warheads reduced to under 10,000 each.
- **START II (1992):** Bush and Yeltsin — further reduced to ~3,000 each.

### The Gulf War (1990–1991)
- **August 2, 1990:** Iraq under **Saddam Hussein** invaded and annexed **Kuwait**, seeking its oil reserves and to erase war debts.
- Bush organized a UN-sanctioned, 35-nation coalition; framed the response as building a "**New World Order**" of collective security in the post-Cold War world.
- **Operation Desert Shield** (troop buildup) → **Operation Desert Storm** (Jan 17–Feb 28, 1991): five weeks of air bombardment followed by a swift 100-hour ground campaign that liberated Kuwait.
- Coalition casualties were light (~300); Hussein remained in power in Iraq — an unfinished outcome that set up the later Iraq War of 2003 (9.6).

### Post-Cold War Conflicts and Europe
- **Breakup of Yugoslavia (1990s):** Ethnic/nationalist violence in Bosnia and later Kosovo; U.S./NATO intervened with airstrikes (1995, 1999) after the end of superpower rivalry removed the old restraint on regional conflicts.
- **Somalia (1992–93):** U.S. humanitarian/peacekeeping mission ended after the "Black Hawk Down" incident (18 U.S. soldiers killed, Oct. 1993) — soured American appetite for open-ended interventions.
- **EU (2002):** Unified market of 15 nations; 12 adopted the euro.
- By 2007: EU expanded to 27 nations, including 10 former Soviet satellites.

## 9.4 — A Changing Economy

### Election of 1992
- **Bill Clinton (D):** Moderate "New Democrat"; focused on economy, jobs, education, health care.
- Defeated Bush (R) despite third-party challenge from Ross Perot; 370 electoral votes, 43% popular vote.

### Clinton's Economic Record
- Achieved **federal surplus** by 1998 — first since 1969.
- Balanced budget preserved Medicare and Social Security; reformed welfare (5-year limit); raised minimum wage.
- Reelected 1996 — first Democrat since FDR to win a second term.

### The Republican Revolution (1994)
- **Newt Gingrich** led House Republicans to victory in the 1994 midterms on the **"Contract with America"** — a unified platform of tax cuts, balanced-budget requirements, term limits, and welfare reform.
- Republicans won control of **both houses of Congress for the first time since 1954**, forcing Clinton to govern from the political center ("triangulation") and setting up the welfare reform and balanced-budget deals of his second term.
- Divided government led to a **budget standoff and federal government shutdowns (1995–96)** when Clinton vetoed Republican spending cuts.

### Clinton's Impeachment
- Lied under oath about sexual relations (Monica Lewinsky) → **impeached by Republican House (1999)** on charges of perjury and obstruction of justice.
- Not convicted by Senate; Clinton finished his term with high approval ratings despite the scandal.

### Economic Expansion
- Longest peacetime economic expansion; growth rates >4% annually.
- Technology drove productivity: Internet, personal computers, wireless communications.
- High-tech companies: Apple, Intel, Microsoft, Amazon, AOL, Yahoo, Google.
- **Dot-com bubble (late 1990s) and bust (2000–2001):** Speculative investment poured into unprofitable internet startups; the NASDAQ crashed in 2000, wiping out trillions in market value and ending the decade's boom.
- **Enron scandal (2001):** Massive corporate accounting fraud collapsed a major energy company, exposing weak oversight of corporate accounting and prompting the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002)** to tighten financial reporting rules.

### Globalization
- **WTO, IMF, World Bank** — global economic organizations.
- **NAFTA (North American Free Trade Agreement):** Signed by Bush, ratified and implemented by Clinton, took effect **January 1, 1994**; created a free-trade zone eliminating most tariffs between the U.S., Canada, and Mexico.
  - Supporters: lower consumer prices, expanded markets for U.S. exporters.
  - Critics: accelerated **deindustrialization** as manufacturers relocated factories to lower-wage Mexico, hollowing out unionized manufacturing jobs in the Midwest ("**Rust Belt**").
- G8 controlled 2/3 of global wealth; China, India, Brazil began outpacing older industrial powers.

### Deindustrialization and the Rust Belt
- Manufacturing's share of U.S. jobs fell steadily from the 1970s on as steel, auto, and textile production shifted overseas or automated.
- Former industrial centers (Ohio, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Illinois) lost population and tax base — the "**Rust Belt**," a mirror image of Sunbelt growth (see 9.5).
- Cause and effect: declining manufacturing employment weakened private-sector unions (see 9.2 Labor), which scholars link directly to widening wage inequality, since unions had historically compressed wage gaps.

### Wealth Inequality
- Top 1% owned 35% of nation's wealth by 2007.
- Top 1% held 43% of financial wealth by late 2010s.
- Scholars linked decline of unions to rising inequality.
- **Comparison — Reaganomics vs. Clintonomics:** Reagan cut taxes and increased defense spending, tripling the debt; Clinton raised top tax rates modestly (1993), cut some spending, and — helped by the tech boom — balanced the budget and ran a **surplus by 1998**. Both eras nonetheless saw income inequality widen, suggesting structural forces (globalization, deindustrialization, declining unions) mattered as much as either party's tax policy.

## 9.5 — Migration and Immigration in the 1990s and 2000s

### Internal Migration: The Sunbelt Shift
- Millions of Americans relocated from the older industrial Northeast/Midwest (the Rust Belt, 9.4) to the **Sunbelt** — the South and Southwest — drawn by warmer climate, lower costs of living/taxes, defense/tech industry jobs, and the spread of air conditioning.
- **Cause and effect:** population growth in Sunbelt states (Texas, Florida, California, Arizona) shifted congressional reapportionment and electoral votes southward and westward after each census, reshaping national political power.

### Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Legacy)
- Ended racially based quota system; prioritized U.S. relatives and skilled workers.
- Opened immigration to non-European countries.

### Push and Pull Factors
- **Push factors:** political instability and civil wars in Central America (El Salvador, Guatemala, Nicaragua) in the 1980s; economic hardship; in Mexico, agricultural dislocation partly linked to NAFTA's effect on small farmers competing with subsidized U.S. crops.
- **Pull factors:** U.S. labor demand in agriculture, construction, and services; family reunification provisions of the 1965 Act; perceived economic opportunity.

### Immigration Shifts (1970–2015)

| Group | Change |
|---|---|
| European/Canadian | 68% → 14% |
| South/East Asian | 7% → 27% |
| Mexican | 8% → 27% |
| Latin American (non-Mexico) | 11% → 24% |

### Undocumented Immigration
- **Immigration Reform and Control Act (1986):** Penalized employers; granted amnesty to 3M+.
- **Proposition 187 (California, 1994):** Voter-approved measure to deny public education, health care, and social services to undocumented immigrants; largely struck down in federal court but signaled growing state-level immigration backlash and reshaped California politics.
- **Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act (1996):** Strengthened enforcement; limited success.
- **Secure Fence Act (2006):** Authorized hundreds of miles of fencing along the U.S.-Mexico border — reflected post-9/11 concern for border security alongside immigration control.
- **DREAM Act:** Repeatedly proposed in Congress (first in 2001, failed again in 2010) to create a path to legal status for undocumented immigrants brought to the U.S. as children; never passed.
- **DACA (2012, Obama):** Since the DREAM Act failed in Congress, Obama used executive action to defer deportation for "Dreamers" via DACA; faced legal challenges over presidential authority to bypass Congress.

### Trump's Immigration Policies
- Proposed border wall; ending DACA; restricting legal immigration.
- Reduced migrants by 2019; sparked major debate.
- **Comparison:** where 1986's IRCA paired enforcement with amnesty (a compromise), post-1996 policy trended toward enforcement-only approaches — reflecting how immigration shifted from a bipartisan issue to one of the sharpest fault lines of political polarization (9.7).

## 9.6 — Challenges of the 21st Century

### Disputed Election of 2000
- Al Gore won popular vote by 500,000+; election hinged on Florida's 25 electoral votes.
- **Bush v. Gore:** Supreme Court halted recount 5–4 → **George W. Bush** won with 271 electoral votes.

### War on Terrorism (Post-9/11)

| Event | Details |
|---|---|
| **September 11, 2001** | Al-Qaeda attacks killed ~3,000; united country against terrorism |
| **Afghanistan** | Bush ordered overthrow of Taliban; succeeded in toppling regime but Afghanistan remained unstable |
| **USA PATRIOT Act** | Enhanced government surveillance and investigative powers |
| **Dept. of Homeland Security** | Largest government reorganization since WWII |

### Iraq War
- Bush labeled Iraq, N. Korea, Iran the "**axis of evil**" (2002).
- Claimed Iraq was developing WMDs (no evidence linking to 9/11).
- **Operation Iraqi Freedom (March 19, 2003):** Air attacks without UN approval; U.S. quickly overran Iraq.
- **No WMDs found**; Bush declared victory → massive insurgent attacks; prolonged conflict.

> [!note] Comparison — Pre- vs. Post-9/11 Foreign Policy
> Before 9/11, post-Cold War U.S. foreign policy (1990s) emphasized selective humanitarian/peacekeeping interventions (Somalia, Bosnia, Kosovo) and general reluctance toward large ground commitments after Somalia's failure. After 9/11, policy shifted toward **preemptive war** (the "Bush Doctrine" — striking threats before they materialize, used to justify invading Iraq without an attack having occurred), expanded executive surveillance powers (PATRIOT Act), and a sustained, decades-long military presence in the Middle East/Central Asia that previous post-Cold War policy had avoided.

### Domestic Policy: George W. Bush (2001–2009)
- **No Child Left Behind Act** — increased educational accountability and testing.
- **Medicare reform** — prescription drug coverage.
- **Hurricane Katrina (2005):** FEMA failure → loss of public confidence → Democrats gained Congress in 2006.

### The 2008 Financial Crisis
- **Causes:** Years of loose lending standards let banks issue **subprime mortgages** to borrowers with poor credit; these loans were bundled into complex mortgage-backed securities and sold to investors worldwide. A housing bubble (prices far outpacing incomes) burst starting in 2007, causing mass mortgage defaults.
- **September 2008:** Investment bank **Lehman Brothers** collapsed — the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history — triggering a global credit freeze and panic ("**Great Recession**").
- **TARP (Troubled Asset Relief Program, Oct. 2008):** Bush signed the $700 billion bank bailout to prevent total financial collapse — a striking example of a conservative administration embracing large-scale government intervention when the alternative (economic collapse) seemed worse.
- Unemployment peaked near 10% in 2009; millions lost homes to foreclosure — the worst downturn since the Great Depression.
- Cause and effect: the crisis handed Obama a historic economic emergency on taking office and shaped his entire first-term domestic agenda (stimulus, Dodd-Frank, below).

### Election of 2008
- Long Democratic primary: Hillary Clinton vs. **Barack Obama**.
- Obama chose Joe Biden as VP; Republicans: John McCain / Sarah Palin.
- Obama won: 364 electoral votes; **first African American president**.
- Faced economic crisis and ongoing wars.

### Obama's Foreign Policy
- Increased troops in Afghanistan; used drone attacks in Pakistan.
- **Osama bin Laden killed (May 2011):** CIA and Navy SEAL operation in Pakistan.
- **ISIS** — extremist group in Iraq/Syria seeking global caliphate; Obama provided air support and military training.

### Domestic Policy: Obama (2009–2017)
- **Economic Stimulus (Keynesian):** Response to Great Recession.
- **Dodd-Frank Act (2010):** Financial regulation reform; prevented future crises.
- **Affordable Care Act (2010, "Obamacare"):** Expanded health insurance; individual mandate; Medicaid expansion; subsidies.
- Obama won reelection 2012 — 332 to 206 electoral votes; strong Hispanic support.

### Political Backlash: Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street
- **Tea Party movement (2009–2010):** Grassroots conservative reaction against TARP, the stimulus, and the ACA — argued government spending and debt were out of control; fueled the Republican takeover of the House in the **2010 midterms** and pulled the GOP further right.
- **Occupy Wall Street (2011):** Left-leaning protest movement camped near Wall Street, decrying the bailouts and rising wealth inequality under the slogan "**We are the 99%**."
- Cause and effect: both movements were direct responses to the 2008 financial crisis and its unequal recovery, and both fed the intensifying political polarization traced through 9.7.

### Paris Climate Agreement
- **2015 (Obama):** U.S. joined the international Paris Agreement, committing to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.
- **2017 (Trump):** U.S. withdrew, citing economic costs.
- **2021 (Biden):** U.S. rejoined on his first day in office.
- Illustrates how climate policy became a recurring point of partisan reversal between administrations.

### Election of 2016
- **Donald Trump (R):** Real estate developer/reality TV star; "Make America Great Again"; attacked immigration and trade deals; won key swing states; **306 electoral votes despite losing popular vote by 3 million**.
- **Hillary Clinton (D):** First woman nominated for president by a major party.
- Republicans maintained control of House and Senate.

### Trump's First Term (2017–2021)
- **Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2017):** Major cut to corporate and individual tax rates, echoing Reagan-era supply-side logic; supporters cited growth, critics cited a widened deficit and disproportionate benefits to high earners.
- **Trade policy:** Imposed tariffs on China and other trading partners, breaking with decades of bipartisan free-trade orthodoxy (NAFTA, WTO) in favor of economic nationalism; renegotiated NAFTA into the **USMCA (2020)**.
- **Supreme Court:** Appointed three justices — **Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh, Amy Coney Barrett** — cementing a conservative majority, continuing the judicial strategy Reagan began in the 1980s (9.2).
- **First impeachment (Dec. 2019):** House impeached Trump over a phone call pressuring Ukraine's president to investigate a political rival; acquitted by the Senate (Feb. 2020).

### 2020 Pandemic (COVID-19)
- Identified in China (Dec 2019); confirmed in U.S. (Jan 2020).
- Trump administration's inconsistent response; 500,000+ American deaths by early 2021.
- **Operation Warp Speed** — accelerated vaccine development; first vaccinations December 2020.

### Election of 2020
- Increased mail-in voting due to pandemic.
- **Joe Biden (D)** — Obama's VP; chose **Kamala Harris** (first person of color nominated for VP).
- Biden won by 7 million votes; **306 electoral votes**.
- Trump falsely claimed victory; ongoing disputes.

### Attack on the Capitol (January 6, 2021)
- Trump rally led to supporters storming the U.S. Capitol.
- First such attack since 1814; 5 deaths.
- Senate confirmed Biden's victory.
- Trump became **first president impeached twice**; not convicted.

### Biden's Administration
- Called for unity in inaugural address.
- **American Rescue Plan (2021):** $1.9 trillion pandemic relief package.
- Plan to vaccinate all Americans by fall 2021.
- **Withdrawal from Afghanistan (August 2021):** Ended America's longest war (begun 2001, 9.6); chaotic final evacuation as the Taliban swiftly retook Kabul — renewed debate over the costs and limits of post-9/11 nation-building.
- **Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (2021)** and **Inflation Reduction Act (2022):** Investments in infrastructure and clean energy alongside efforts to curb inflation, which rose sharply in 2021–2022 amid pandemic supply-chain disruption.
- **Russia's invasion of Ukraine (February 2022):** U.S. led Western sanctions on Russia and supplied military aid to Ukraine — a major test of the post-Cold War European security order established in the 1990s (9.3).

### Election of 2024
- **Donald Trump (R)** defeated **Kamala Harris (D)** — Biden's VP, who became the Democratic nominee after Biden withdrew from the race — winning both the popular vote and 312 electoral votes to Harris's 226.
- Trump flipped several swing states that Biden had carried in 2020 (Georgia, Michigan, Wisconsin, Arizona, Nevada, Pennsylvania), returning to office for a second, non-consecutive term.

## 9.7 — Causation in Period 9

### How Period 9's Threads Connect
- The **end of the Cold War (1991)** removed the organizing principle of U.S. foreign policy, leaving the country to define a new role as sole superpower.
- **Globalization** (NAFTA, WTO, the tech boom) tied the U.S. economy closer to the world but also widened domestic wealth inequality and hollowed out manufacturing regions.
- **9/11** redirected U.S. foreign policy toward counterterrorism (Afghanistan, Iraq, PATRIOT Act), a shift with consequences still shaping policy decades later.
- Rising **economic inequality**, demographic change, and media fragmentation combined to deepen political polarization — visible in the contested elections and social unrest of the 2010s–2020s.

### Causal Chains Worth Knowing for the Exam
1. **1970s stagflation/Watergate → conservative resurgence:** Economic crisis and distrust of government discredited liberalism, enabling the New Right coalition (economic, foreign-policy, and religious conservatives) to elect Reagan in 1980 (9.1 → 9.2).
2. **Reagan's military buildup + Soviet economic stagnation → Cold War's end:** Higher defense spending and the Reagan Doctrine's proxy wars raised the cost of Soviet global commitments just as its command economy was failing, pressuring Gorbachev into reforms that spiraled beyond his control (9.2 → 9.3).
3. **End of Cold War → globalization accelerates:** With no rival superpower blocking global markets, trade agreements (NAFTA, WTO) and technology spread capital and jobs worldwide — raising aggregate U.S. wealth while concentrating pain in manufacturing regions (9.3 → 9.4).
4. **Deindustrialization + declining unions → widening inequality → political backlash:** Factory closures weakened organized labor's power to compress wages; three decades later, that same economic anxiety fed both the Tea Party (right) and Occupy Wall Street (left), and later the populist politics of the 2010s–2020s (9.4 → 9.6).
5. **9/11 → sustained war footing → domestic and foreign-policy consequences:** The attacks justified new surveillance powers (PATRIOT Act), preemptive war doctrine (Iraq), and two-decade military commitments (Afghanistan) whose costs and outcomes (chaotic 2021 withdrawal) still shape debates about the proper limits of American power (9.6).
6. **2008 financial crisis → institutional distrust → polarization:** The crisis and uneven recovery discredited both establishment-Republican deregulation and, for some, establishment-Democratic bailout politics — fueling anti-establishment movements on both ends of the spectrum that reshaped both major parties by the 2016 and 2024 elections (9.6).

### Historiographical Framing — Causes of Rising Polarization
- Political scientists debate the primary driver of late-20th/21st-century polarization; know the main candidate explanations rather than a single cause:
  - **Economic:** globalization and deindustrialization created "winners and losers" whose geographic concentration hardened into partisan geography (Rust Belt vs. Sunbelt/coastal metros).
  - **Media/technological:** the end of the "Fairness Doctrine" (1987) and the rise of cable news, talk radio, and later social media let Americans self-sort into ideologically separate information ecosystems.
  - **Institutional:** the 1994 "Contract with America" and the rise of partisan redistricting ("gerrymandering") made primary elections — where the most ideological voters turn out — more decisive than general elections in many districts.
  - **Demographic/cultural:** ongoing debates over immigration, race (Rodney King, BLM), and social issues (same-sex marriage, abortion) mapped onto existing party divides, especially after the religious right's rise in the New Right coalition.
- On the exam, a strong causation essay pairs a specific event with **both** an immediate trigger and a deeper structural cause — e.g., the 2016 and 2024 elections' populist appeal is best explained by decades of economic dislocation and media fragmentation, not any single campaign.
