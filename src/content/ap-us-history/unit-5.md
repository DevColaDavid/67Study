# Period 5 — Civil War and Reconstruction (1844–1877)

---

## 5.1 — Contextualizing Period 5

### From Growth to Crisis
- Period 4's democratization and market revolution left the North and South on increasingly divergent economic and social paths — industrial/free-labor vs. agrarian/slave-labor.
- The **Missouri Compromise (1820)** had kept an uneasy balance, but it was only a temporary fix, not a resolution.
- Renewed territorial expansion (Texas, Oregon, and soon Mexican Cession lands) reopened the question of slavery's extension with each new acquisition.
- This period covers the collapse of political compromise, the Civil War, and the failed effort at Reconstruction that followed.
- **College Board's three Key Concepts for Period 5** frame the whole unit:
  1. **5.A — Expansion and regional division:** the U.S. expanded across the continent, sparking conflicts over slavery's extension and over Native American land.
  2. **5.B — Conflict and civil war:** intensifying regional divisions over slavery and other issues led to the Civil War.
  3. **5.C — Reconstruction:** Union victory ended slavery and secession but left unresolved conflicts over federal power and the rights of citizens.

### The Nation's Diverging Economies (by 1860)
- **North:** ~110,000 factories; roughly 90% of the nation's manufacturing output; dense rail and canal networks; free-labor wage system; population ~22 million.
- **South:** cotton was ~60% of all U.S. exports and supplied ~75% of the world's raw cotton; wealth concentrated in enslaved labor (~4 million enslaved people, worth more than all the North's factories and railroads combined); population ~9 million (5.5 million free, 3.5+ million enslaved).
- This growing economic gap — not just moral disagreement — meant the two regions increasingly needed different national policies (tariffs, internal improvements, banking, land policy), turning slavery's expansion into a fight over which economic system would control the West and the federal government.

### Native Americans and Continued Removal
- Manifest Destiny's push into Texas, Oregon, and the Mexican Cession put renewed pressure on Native peoples already displaced by the Indian Removal Act (Period 4).
- Overland trails, the Gold Rush, and (after 1848) government-backed settlement disrupted Plains bison herds and tribal land claims.
- **Fort Laramie Treaty (1851):** U.S. negotiated with Plains tribes (Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, others) to define tribal territories and guarantee safe passage for settlers along the Oregon Trail — an early step toward the reservation system that would expand after the Civil War.
- This set up the sustained federal-Native conflict of Period 6, as westward expansion in Period 5 kept treaties fragile and short-lived.

## 5.2 — Manifest Destiny

- Coined by journalist **John L. O'Sullivan** in 1845 — divine mission to spread American liberty and democracy from Atlantic to Pacific.
- **Opposition:** Many Northerners feared it was a scheme to expand slavery into new territories.

### Texas Annexation
- Mexico invited American settlers into Texas (1821); by 1830, Americans outnumbered Mexicans 3:1.
- Mexico outlawed slavery → tensions rose.
- **1836:** American settlers (Sam Houston) declared independence → **Republic of Texas**.

### Oregon Territory
- Claimed by Spain, Russia, Britain, and U.S.; settled by thousands of Americans in the mid-1840s.
- **Election of 1844:** James K. Polk won on Manifest Destiny platform → settled boundary at **49th parallel**.

### Gold Rush
- 1848 discovery of gold in California → rapid population growth, boomtowns.
- California did **not** adopt slavery → unique social landscape hinting at national tensions.

### Alaska Purchase (1867)
- Secretary of State **William Seward** purchased Alaska from Russia for $7.2 million.

### Gadsden Purchase (1853)
- U.S. minister **James Gadsden** bought a strip of southern Arizona/New Mexico from Mexico for $10 million.
- Purpose: secure a flat southern route for a future transcontinental railroad — tied Manifest Destiny directly to sectional competition over where the railroad (and its economic benefits) would run.

### Filibustering and the Ostend Manifesto
- **Filibusters** — private American adventurers who launched unauthorized invasions of Latin American territory hoping to add new slave states.
- **William Walker** briefly seized power in Nicaragua (1856) and legalized slavery there before being overthrown.
- **Ostend Manifesto (1854):** a leaked, secret memo from U.S. diplomats proposing the U.S. buy or seize **Cuba** from Spain to add another slave state; public outrage over the scheme forced the Pierce administration to disavow it.
- Both episodes show Southern expansionists looking for new slave territory once continental expansion into free-leaning California/Oregon had stalled slavery's growth.

## 5.3 — The Mexican-American War (1846–1848)

### Causes
- U.S. annexation of Texas → border dispute (Mexico: Nueces River; U.S.: Rio Grande).
- Polk wanted California and New Mexico.
- Mexican army attacked U.S. patrol near Rio Grande → pretext for war.

### Wilmot Proviso
- Proposed to prohibit slavery in any territory acquired from Mexico.
- Passed the House; **blocked in the Senate** → revealed deep sectional divisions.

### Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848)
- Mexico ceded the vast **Mexican Cession** — California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, parts of Colorado and Wyoming (~525,000 square miles) — for $15 million; the U.S. also assumed $3.25 million in American citizens' claims against Mexico.
- **Reignited the slavery debate** — where would slavery be allowed in new territories? This single treaty is why nearly every module from 5.4–5.7 exists: it made the "who gets the West" question unavoidable.

### Domestic Opposition to the War
- Many Whigs and abolitionists (e.g., **Whig Congressman Abraham Lincoln**, via his "**Spot Resolutions**") argued Polk provoked the war to expand slave territory and demanded he identify the exact "spot" where American blood was shed on American soil.
- **Henry David Thoreau** refused to pay taxes to protest the war and slavery, was briefly jailed, and wrote *Civil Disobedience* (1849) — introduced the tactic of nonviolent resistance to unjust government policy.
- The war cost roughly 13,000 American deaths, the large majority from disease rather than combat — a preview of Civil War-era medical realities.

## 5.4 — The Compromise of 1850

### Background
- Southerners resented Missouri Compromise restrictions.
- California (Gold Rush) sought admission as a **free state** — south of the 36°30' line.
- Southern "fire-eaters" threatened secession.

### Three Main Positions on Slavery

| Position | Description | Supporters |
|---|---|---|
| **Free-Soil** | No slavery in new territories | Northern Democrats & Whigs; Free-Soil Party (1848) |
| **Southern** | Slavery should extend everywhere; willing to extend Missouri Compromise line to Pacific | Plantation owners |
| **Popular Sovereignty** | Let territory residents decide by vote | Senator Lewis Cass (moderate) |

### Henry Clay's Compromise of 1850
1. Admit **California** as a free state.
2. **Utah and New Mexico** territories organized with popular sovereignty.
3. Resolve Texas-New Mexico boundary; federal debt assumption.
4. Ban **slave trade** (not slavery) in D.C.
5. New, stringent **Fugitive Slave Law**.

> Passed piece by piece by **Stephen A. Douglas**; signed by **Millard Fillmore** after Taylor's death.
> **Short-term relief** only — Fugitive Slave Law and popular sovereignty increased sectional strife.

### The Georgia Platform (1850)
- Southern moderates' formal statement accepting the Compromise **on the condition** that the North fully enforce the Fugitive Slave Law and not interfere further with slavery.
- Explicitly warned that any future Northern violation of the Compromise (e.g., weak enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Law) would justify secession — meaning the Compromise's "peace" was conditional from the start, not a genuine resolution.

## 5.5 — Sectional Conflict: Regional Differences

### Immigration
- **Irish (~2 million):** Fled famine in 1840s; settled in Northern cities; faced anti-Catholic discrimination.
- **Germans (>1 million):** Fled 1848 revolutions; prospered in Midwest; supported public education and anti-slavery.
- **Nativism:** Know-Nothing Party (American Party) — proposed 21-year naturalization period.

### Fugitive Slave Law (1850)
- Federal involvement in retrieval of runaway slaves; denied jury trials; compelled local officials to participate.
- **Financial bias built into the law:** special federal commissioners were paid **$10** if they ruled a captured person was indeed a fugitive (returning them to slavery) but only **$5** if they ruled the person free — a structural incentive Northerners saw as a mockery of due process.
- **Anthony Burns case (Boston, 1854):** federal troops and marshals forcibly returned an escaped enslaved man to Virginia despite mass protests, radicalizing many previously moderate Bostonians against the law.
- **Personal liberty laws:** several Northern states passed laws obstructing enforcement (barring state officials/jails from helping catch fugitives) — a Northern echo of Southern "states' rights" arguments, showing both sections were willing to defy federal law when it suited them.
- Deepened North-South divide; galvanized the abolitionist cause.

### Underground Railroad
- Network of Black and White abolitionists; **Harriet Tubman** led hundreds to freedom.

### Free Labor vs. Slave Labor Ideology
- The emerging Republican Party built its identity around **"free soil, free labor, free men"** — the idea that free wage labor, social mobility, and small-farm/entrepreneurial opportunity in the North and West were morally and economically superior to the closed, aristocratic slave-labor system of the South.
- Many Northerners opposed slavery's *expansion* less out of concern for enslaved people and more because they didn't want to compete with unpaid labor for land and jobs in the territories — an important nuance: most Republicans in 1860 were **not** abolitionists demanding immediate emancipation, but "free-soilers" opposing slavery's spread.

### Influential Literature

| Work | Author | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) | Harriet Beecher Stowe | Shaped Northern attitudes; bolstered abolition; international sympathy |
| *Impending Crisis of the South* (1857) | Hinton R. Helper | Critiqued slavery's economic harm; banned in South |

## 5.6 — Failure of Compromise

### Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854)
- Senator **Stephen A. Douglas** divided Nebraska Territory into Kansas and Nebraska.
- Slavery decided by **popular sovereignty** → effectively nullified the Missouri Compromise.

### "Bleeding Kansas"
- Violent clashes between pro- and anti-slavery settlers, many from outside Kansas ("border ruffians" from Missouri crossed over to vote illegally in territorial elections).
- Incidents: Sack of Lawrence, **Pottawatomie Creek Massacre** (John Brown).

### Lecompton Constitution (1857)
- Kansas's pro-slavery territorial legislature drafted a state constitution **without a fair popular vote** and sent it to Congress for admission as a slave state.
- President **James Buchanan** endorsed it despite the fraud, but Senator **Stephen Douglas** broke with his own party to oppose it — violated his own popular-sovereignty principle and **split the Democratic Party** along sectional lines.
- Kansas voters ultimately rejected the constitution in an 1858 referendum; Kansas entered the Union as a **free state in 1861**.
- **Effect:** discredited popular sovereignty as a fair process and showed Democrats could not hold together — a direct cause of the party's 1860 split.

### Caning of Senator Sumner (1856)
- Abolitionist Senator Charles Sumner beaten by Rep. Preston Brooks → North was horrified; South celebrated.

### Birth of the Republican Party (1854)
- Anti-slavery ex-Whigs, Democrats, and Free-Soilers; formed in Wisconsin.
- Goal: stop the *spread* of slavery.

### Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)
- Supreme Court: African Americans were **not U.S. citizens**; Congress had no authority to prohibit slavery in territories → Missouri Compromise unconstitutional.
- Outraged the North; celebrated in the South.

### Lincoln-Douglas Debates (1858)
- Abraham Lincoln (Republican) vs. Stephen Douglas (Democrat) for Illinois Senate seat.
- Lincoln argued against moral expansion of slavery; Douglas supported popular sovereignty.
- **Freeport Doctrine (Freeport, IL debate):** Douglas argued that even after *Dred Scott* technically legalized slavery in all territories, a territory could still exclude it in practice by simply refusing to pass local "slave codes" protecting slaveholders' property — slavery, he said, could not survive without friendly local law.
- Douglas won the Senate seat, but the Freeport Doctrine **alienated Southern Democrats**, who wanted federal protection for slavery in the territories regardless of local sentiment — this split helped fracture the Democratic Party in 1860.
- Boosted Lincoln's national profile → 1860 presidential candidacy.

### The Panic of 1857
- A financial crash hit Northern banks, railroads, and manufacturing hard, while Southern cotton exports were largely insulated (Europe's demand for cotton stayed strong).
- Reinforced the Southern belief that **"Cotton is King"** — that the slave-labor cotton economy was more resilient than the North's "wage-slave" industrial system — boosting Southern confidence that secession was economically survivable.

## 5.7 — Election of 1860 and Secession

### John Brown's Raid at Harpers Ferry (1859)
- Brown attempted to seize federal arsenal to start a slave uprising.
- Captured by **Robert E. Lee**; tried, convicted, hanged.
- South: proof of Northern aggression. North: Brown as martyr.

### Election of 1860
- Democratic Party **split** — Northern Democrats (Douglas) vs. Southern Democrats (Breckinridge, running on federal protection of slavery in the territories).
- Constitutional Union Party nominated John Bell (avoided the slavery issue; "the Union, the Constitution, the enforcement of the laws").
- **Abraham Lincoln** won without a single Southern electoral vote — about 40% of the national popular vote but a solid majority (180 of 303) of the Electoral College, since the four-way split let him sweep the North.
- **Why the split mattered:** Lincoln's win with almost no Southern support proved to Southerners that the North's larger population could now control the presidency without any Southern votes at all — the political balance of power that had preserved compromise for decades was gone.

### Secession
- South Carolina seceded first (Dec 1860); six more Deep South states followed by Feb 1861 → **Confederate States of America**, with **Jefferson Davis** as president.
- **South Carolina's "Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession"** explicitly named the election of a president "hostile to slavery" and Northern refusal to enforce the Fugitive Slave Law as the causes — direct primary-source evidence that slavery, not just "states' rights" in the abstract, drove secession.
- **Confederate Constitution:** closely modeled on the U.S. Constitution but explicitly protected slavery in all Confederate territory, barred protective tariffs, and limited the president to a single six-year term; still centralized more power at the state level, which would hamper the Confederate war effort.
- **Crittenden Compromise** (constitutionally protect slavery at 36°30′, guarantee it in existing slave states) — rejected by Republicans, including Lincoln, as it would have permitted future slave-territory expansion.
- **Fort Sumter (April 1861):** Confederate attack → four more Upper South states seceded (Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, Arkansas).
- **Border States** (Maryland, Missouri, Kentucky, Delaware) kept in Union through political maneuvering and martial law — losing them would have given the Confederacy access to Washington, D.C.'s doorstep and the Ohio River.

### The Cornerstone Speech (March 1861)
- Confederate Vice President **Alexander Stephens**, in Savannah, GA, declared the Confederacy's "cornerstone rests upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery ... is his natural and normal condition."
- Directly contradicts later "states' rights, not slavery" arguments about Confederate motives — used by APUSH questions as a primary source showing the Confederacy's own leadership framed slavery and white supremacy as the war's central cause.

## 5.8 — Military Conflict in the Civil War

### Union vs. Confederate Advantages

| Category | Union | Confederacy |
|---|---|---|
| Population | 22 million | 5.5 million |
| Economy | Banks, factories, railroads, farmland | Cotton exports; hoped for European support |
| Military | Large navy; larger manpower | Defensive war; experienced leaders; shorter supply lines |
| Political | Established central government | States' rights weakened Confederate central authority |

### Union Strategy (Anaconda Plan)
1. **Naval blockade** of Southern ports.
2. Control the **Mississippi River** — split the Confederacy.
3. Capture **Richmond** (Confederate capital).

### Key Battles

| Battle | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| First Bull Run | July 1861 | Union unprepared; no short war |
| **Shiloh** | April 1862 | Bloodiest battle in American history to that point (~23,000 casualties); showed both sides the war would be long and costly |
| Monitor vs. Merrimac | March 1862 | First ironclad battle; wooden warships obsolete |
| **Antietam** | Sept 1862 | Bloodiest single day in U.S. history (~23,000 casualties); Union halted Lee's advance into MD; gave Lincoln the "victory" he needed to issue the Emancipation Proclamation |
| **Battle of Fredericksburg** | Dec 1862 | Devastating Union defeat under Burnside |
| **Vicksburg** | July 4, 1863 | Grant gained control of Mississippi River; split Confederacy in two |
| **Gettysburg** | July 1–3, 1863 | Largest battle of the war; Pickett's Charge failed; last major Confederate offensive in the North; ~50,000 total casualties |
| **Fall of Atlanta** | Sept 1864 | Sherman captured the key rail/manufacturing hub; revived Northern morale and secured Lincoln's re-election |
| **Sherman's March to the Sea** | Late 1864 | Total war tactics; scorched earth from Atlanta to Savannah; destroyed Southern infrastructure and civilian will to fight |
| **Appomattox Court House** | April 9, 1865 | Lee surrendered to Grant; generous terms (paroled soldiers kept horses, no treason trials) aimed at reconciliation |

### Grant's Overland Campaign and Total War (1864–1865)
- Lincoln made **Ulysses S. Grant** general-in-chief in 1864; Grant adopted a strategy of simultaneous pressure on all fronts and was willing to accept heavy Union casualties (Wilderness, Spotsylvania, Cold Harbor) to grind down Confederate manpower it couldn't replace.
- **Total war**: Union armies (especially Sherman's) targeted the Confederacy's economic capacity to fight — railroads, crops, livestock, civilian infrastructure — not just its armies, to break the will to continue.

### The Home Front and Women's Roles
- Thousands of women on both sides served as **nurses** (Clara Barton, who later founded the American Red Cross; Dorothea Dix, superintendent of Union army nurses) and ran the **U.S. Sanitary Commission**, which raised funds and supplies for soldiers.
- Women took over farms, plantations, and factory jobs in men's absence; some (like **Loreta Janeta Velázquez** and other documented cases) disguised themselves to fight.

### Diplomatic Dimensions
- **Trent Affair (1861):** Capture of Confederate diplomats from British ship nearly brought Britain into the war.
- **Cotton Diplomacy Failed** — Britain sourced cotton from Egypt and India; Emancipation Proclamation prevented European support for Confederacy since intervening would mean defending slavery.

## 5.9 — Government Policies During the Civil War

### Emancipation Proclamation (Jan 1, 1863)
- Declared freedom for enslaved people in **Confederate-held territories** (exempt: border states and Union-controlled areas).
- **Shifted war aim** from preserving Union to abolishing slavery.
- **Prevented foreign intervention** — supporting Confederacy would mean endorsing slavery.
- Nearly **200,000 African Americans** served in the Union Army and Navy by war's end. The **54th Massachusetts Regiment** — led by Colonel **Robert Gould Shaw** — became famous for its courageous assault on **Fort Wagner, SC (1863)**, proving to skeptical Northern whites that Black soldiers would fight; Black soldiers were paid less than white soldiers until Congress equalized pay in 1864.

### Civil Liberties
- **Suspension of Habeas Corpus** — Lincoln arrested suspected Confederate sympathizers and dissenters without trial, citing wartime necessity under his commander-in-chief power.
- **Copperheads (Peace Democrats):** Northern Democrats who opposed the war and favored a negotiated peace with the Confederacy; Congressman **Clement Vallandigham** was arrested and exiled to the Confederacy for anti-war speeches — a controversial test of wartime civil liberties.
- **Ex Parte Milligan** (1866, post-war): Supreme Court ruled civilians cannot be subjected to military tribunals when civilian courts are open and functioning — implicitly rebuked Lincoln's wartime military-trial practices, though only after the war ended.

### The Draft (1863 Conscription Act)
- Men could pay a $300 fee or hire a substitute to avoid service — seen as unfair to the poor ("rich man's war, poor man's fight") → **New York Draft Riots (July 1863)**, the deadliest riot in American history to that point (~120 killed), largely Irish immigrant mobs attacking Black New Yorkers and draft offices.

### Election of 1864
- Lincoln ran on the **National Union ticket** (Republicans + War Democrats) with **Andrew Johnson**, a pro-Union Southern Democrat, as running mate to broaden appeal.
- Democratic nominee **George McClellan** ran partly on a peace platform, though he personally rejected ending the war without reunion.
- **Sherman's capture of Atlanta (Sept 1864)** revived Northern morale just before the election and secured Lincoln's decisive victory (212–21 electoral votes) — without it, war-weariness might have produced a negotiated peace preserving slavery and/or Southern independence.

### Key Legislation

| Law | Year | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Morrill Tariff Act | 1861 | Protect domestic industries; generate revenue |
| Legal Tender Act | 1862 | Issued "greenbacks" — first paper currency not directly backed by gold/silver, to help finance the war |
| Homestead Act | 1862 | 160 free acres to settlers who farmed for 5 years |
| Morrill Land Grant Act | 1862 | Federal land for agricultural/technical colleges |
| Pacific Railway Act | 1862 | Authorized transcontinental railroad |
| National Banking Act | 1863 | Created a system of national banks and a uniform national currency; taxed state bank notes to push them out of circulation |

> **Why it matters:** With Southern Democrats out of Congress, Republicans passed a sweeping economic agenda (tariffs, banking, land grants, railroads) that had been blocked for decades — the Civil War reshaped the federal government's economic role as much as it settled slavery.

### Assassination of Lincoln
- John Wilkes Booth (Confederate sympathizer) shot Lincoln on **April 14, 1865**, just five days after Lee's surrender — removed the president most inclined toward a lenient, unifying Reconstruction and left Andrew Johnson, a far less skillful politician, in charge.

## 5.10 — Reconstruction

### Competing Plans

| Plan | Who | Key Terms |
|---|---|---|
| **Lincoln's 10% Plan** | Lincoln | 10% of voters swear loyalty + accept emancipation → form new state government |
| **Wade-Davis Bill (1864)** | Radical Republicans in Congress | 50% of voters + only non-Confederates vote for new constitutions → Lincoln vetoed |

### Freedmen's Bureau (1865)
- Provided aid to Black and White Southerners; founded thousands of schools; negotiated labor contracts and provided food/medical care.
- **Special Field Order No. 15 (Sherman, Jan 1865):** set aside coastal land in SC/GA/FL for freed families in 40-acre plots ("40 acres and a mule" — the Army also lent surplus mules) — briefly raised hopes for Black economic independence through land ownership.
- Land redistribution largely undone: **Johnson's pardons** restored land to former Confederates, and the Bureau (which expired in 1872) never had the funding or troop support to make freedom economically meaningful for most Black Southerners — a key reason Reconstruction's promise went unfulfilled.

### Andrew Johnson
- Succeeded Lincoln; originally welcomed by Radical Republicans, who mistakenly assumed his working-class Southern-Unionist background meant he'd be harsh toward the planter elite.
- Issued personal pardons restoring former Confederates to power, as long as they took a loyalty oath (high-ranking officials/wealthy planters needed individual presidential pardons, which Johnson granted liberally).
- **Vetoed** the Freedmen's Bureau expansion (1866) and the **Civil Rights Act of 1866** (which granted citizenship and equal legal rights to all persons born in the U.S. except Native Americans — Congress **overrode** the veto, the first major legislation overridden in U.S. history, and used it as the template for the 14th Amendment to make those rights unamendable by a future Congress).
- Vetoed legislation **29 times**; Congress overrode many — this executive-legislative standoff defined "Presidential Reconstruction" (1865–66) before Congress took control.

### Congressional (Radical) Reconstruction (1866–)
- Radical Republicans took control after sweeping the 1866 midterms (partly a backlash to Southern **Black Codes** and race riots); passed **Reconstruction Acts of 1867** — South divided into 5 military districts under Army control; states required to ratify the 14th Amendment and guarantee adult Black male voting rights before re-admission to Congress.
- **State governments during Radical Reconstruction** were coalitions of freedmen, **"carpetbaggers"** (Northerners who moved South, often to invest or teach — the term was a pejorative implying they came only to profit), and **"scalawags"** (Southern whites, often small farmers or ex-Whigs, who supported Republican Reconstruction) — Southern Democrats used both terms to delegitimize these governments regardless of their actual records.

### Constitutional Amendments

| Amendment | Year | Key Provision |
|---|---|---|
| **13th** | 1865 | Abolished slavery throughout the U.S. |
| **14th** | 1868 | Citizenship to all born in U.S.; equal protection; due process; foundation of later civil rights law |
| **15th** | 1870 | Cannot deny vote based on race, color, or previous servitude |

### Impeachment of Andrew Johnson (1867–1868)
- Violated **Tenure of Office Act** by dismissing Secretary of War Edwin Stanton.
- House impeached; **acquitted by one vote** in the Senate.

### Civil Rights Act of 1875
- Equal accommodations in public places; no exclusion from juries.
- Poorly enforced; Congress abandoned Reconstruction by 1877.

### African Americans in Reconstruction
- Reunited families separated by slavery, established independent Black churches (often the center of Black community and political life) and schools.
- Black colleges founded: Howard, Atlanta, Fisk, Morehouse.
- **Black political participation:** roughly 2,000 African Americans held public office during Reconstruction. **Hiram Revels** (Mississippi, 1870) became the first Black U.S. Senator; **Blanche K. Bruce** (Mississippi, 1875) was the second; **P.B.S. Pinchback** briefly served as acting governor of Louisiana (1872–73), the first Black state governor. Most Black officeholders served at state and local levels (legislatures, sheriffs, school boards).

## 5.11 — Failure of Reconstruction

### White Supremacy & the KKK
- **Ku Klux Klan** (founded 1867 by Nathan Bedford Forrest) — violence against Black voters and White reformers.
- **Force Acts (1870–1871)** — Congress tried to stop Klan violence.

### Black Codes
- Laws restricting African Americans' rights and movements; forced labor contracts resembling slavery.

### Sharecropping and the Crop-Lien System
- With land redistribution ("40 acres") reversed, most freedpeople and many poor whites became **sharecroppers** — landlords provided land, seed, and tools for a share (often half) of the harvest.
- The **crop-lien system** let croppers buy supplies on credit from local merchants against their future crop, at high interest — falling cotton prices after the war meant many never paid off their debt, creating a cycle of **debt peonage** that functioned as a new, informal form of economic bondage tying Black (and poor white) labor to the land.

### Redeemers and "Redemption"
- **Redeemers** — conservative Southern Democrats who used a mix of violence (KKK), fraud, and race-baiting to "redeem" (retake) state governments from Republican control through the 1870s.
- Once in power, Redeemer governments cut spending on public schools and services, and began laying the groundwork for the segregation laws and Black voter suppression (poll taxes, literacy tests) that would fully emerge as **Jim Crow** in the 1880s–90s (Period 6) — the "**Solid (Democratic) South**" that resulted would last until the mid-20th century.

### Compromise of 1877
- Disputed 1876 election (Hayes vs. Tilden) resolved: **Rutherford B. Hayes** (R) became president in exchange for:
  - Withdrawal of federal troops from the South.
  - Support for a Southern transcontinental railroad.
- **Marked the end of Reconstruction** → rollback of Black rights; rise of Jim Crow.

### Historiography of Reconstruction
- **Dunning School** (early 20th century, led by Columbia historian William A. Dunning): portrayed Reconstruction as a corrupt, vindictive failure imposed on a prostrate South by vengeful Radical Republicans, carpetbaggers, and "unfit" Black officeholders — used to justify Jim Crow and Black disenfranchisement as a "correction" of Reconstruction's supposed errors. This view dominated textbooks (and popular culture, e.g. *Birth of a Nation*, 1915) for decades.
- **W.E.B. Du Bois**, in *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935), directly challenged this narrative, arguing Black political participation was a genuine, capable democratic experiment and that Reconstruction's real tragedy was its abandonment, not its attempt.
- **Modern ("revisionist") historiography**, especially **Eric Foner's** *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (1988), reframes Reconstruction as a flawed but genuine effort at interracial democracy whose collapse — due to Northern will fading, Southern white terror, and the Compromise of 1877 — was the true failure, not Black or Republican misgovernment.
- **Why this matters for the exam:** APUSH questions increasingly test this shift directly — expect prompts asking you to evaluate *how* historical interpretations of Reconstruction changed over time and *why* (linking historiography to the civil rights movement's influence on historians in the mid-20th century).

## 5.12 — Comparison in Period 5

### North vs. South on the Eve of War

| Category | Union (North) | Confederacy (South) |
|---|---|---|
| Economy | Diversified — industry, finance, farming | Agrarian; dependent on cotton exports and enslaved labor |
| Population/Labor | Larger population (~22 million); free-labor wage system | Smaller population (~9 million, incl. ~3.5 million enslaved); ~4 million enslaved people as the labor base |
| Infrastructure | Extensive railroads and factories; ~90% of manufacturing | Limited industry and rail; reliant on imports through the naval blockade |
| Politics | Centralized federal war effort | States'-rights ideology weakened central Confederate authority, ironically undermining the war effort it was fought to protect |
| Military (early war) | Untested leadership; had to invade and hold territory | Experienced officers (many West Point-trained); only had to defend, and knew the terrain |

### Comparing Political Ideologies Across the Period
- **Popular sovereignty (Cass, Douglas)** vs. **free-soil non-extension (Republicans)** vs. **federal slave-code protection (Southern "fire-eaters," Breckinridge Democrats)** were three competing answers to the same question — who decides slavery's fate in the territories? — and their inability to coexist within one party system explains the Democratic Party's 1860 collapse.
- Compare **Compromise of 1850** and **Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854)**: both tried to use popular sovereignty to defuse the territorial slavery question, but each one *increased* sectional conflict instead of resolving it — a pattern showing that by the 1850s, no legislative compromise could satisfy both sections.

### Comparing Reconstruction Plans

| Plan | Author(s) | Terms | Fate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10% Plan** | Lincoln | 10% of voters take loyalty oath, accept emancipation | Lincoln assassinated before full implementation |
| **Wade-Davis Bill** | Radical Republicans (1864) | 50% majority oath; only non-Confederates draft new constitutions | Pocket-vetoed by Lincoln |
| **Presidential Reconstruction** | Andrew Johnson | Lenient pardons; minimal conditions for Southern states' return | Congress rejected it after Black Codes passed; led to Radical takeover |
| **Congressional/Radical Reconstruction** | Republican Congress | Military districts; 14th Amendment ratification required; Black male suffrage guaranteed | Implemented, but unraveled by 1877 |

- **Underlying comparison:** every plan reflects the same core tension — restore the Union *quickly* vs. remake Southern society to *guarantee* Black civil rights first — and each successive plan (10% → Wade-Davis → Johnson → Congressional) shows Congress moving further from leniency as Southern resistance (Black Codes, violence) proved leniency wasn't working.

### Historiographical Comparison: Was the Civil War Inevitable?
- **"Irrepressible conflict" view** (contemporary term coined by Senator William Seward, 1858; later echoed by historians like James McPherson): slavery and the North/South economic systems were fundamentally incompatible, making some form of confrontation unavoidable once compromise options (Missouri Compromise, Compromise of 1850) ran out.
- **"Blundering generation" / contingency view** (historians Avery Craven and James G. Randall, 1930s–40s): the war was **not** inevitable — it resulted from failures of political leadership, needless agitation by extremists on both sides (fire-eaters, radical abolitionists), and could have been avoided or delayed with wiser statesmanship.
- **Exam framing:** this debate is a classic APUSH "causation" comparison — pair it with the Reconstruction historiography shift (Dunning School → Du Bois → Foner, see Module 5.11) to see how historians' *own* eras and politics shaped how they interpreted the same primary-source evidence.

> **Key Comparison:** The North's structural advantages in population, industry, and infrastructure ultimately outweighed the South's early tactical and leadership advantages, and the same underlying conflict — how much change the South should be forced to accept, and who gets to decide — resurfaced during Reconstruction just as it had during the sectional crisis over the territories.
