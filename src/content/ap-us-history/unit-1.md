# Period 1 — A New World (1491–1607)

---

## 1.1 — Contextualizing Period 1

### The World Before 1491
- No sustained contact yet existed between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres.
- **The Americas:** Hundreds of distinct Native societies — from centralized empires (Aztec, Inca) to small kin-based bands — with no immunity to Old World diseases.
- **Europe:** Emerging from feudalism; Renaissance curiosity, rising monarchies (Spain, Portugal, England, France) consolidating power and seeking new revenue.
- **Africa:** Established kingdoms (Mali, Songhai, Kongo) with existing trans-Saharan trade networks in gold, salt, and slaves — a system Europeans would later extend across the Atlantic.
- **Asia:** Ottoman control of Mediterranean/overland trade routes pushed Europeans to seek sea routes to Asia, setting the stage for transatlantic voyages.

### Scope and Dates
- Period 1 runs **1491 (the year before Columbus)** to **1607 (the founding of Jamestown)** — a short but disproportionately tested period on the AP exam (roughly 5% of the total course).
- The exam's two Key Concepts for this period: **1.1** — Native societies developed complex, adaptive civilizations across diverse environments before contact; **1.2** — Contact among Europeans, Native Americans, and Africans produced the Columbian Exchange and transformed societies on both sides of the Atlantic.
- **Historiographical note:** Older textbooks framed 1492 as the "discovery" of a nearly empty continent. Most historians today reject that framing — the Americas held an estimated **50–100 million people** in 1491 (more than Europe at the time), organized into complex, dynamic societies, not a "New World" waiting to be found.

> **Big Picture:** Period 1 covers the collision of these separate worlds — contact that permanently reshaped demographics, economies, and cultures on every continent involved.

## 1.2 — Native American Societies Before European Contact

### Early Migration and Settlement
- First inhabitants migrated from Asia across a **land bridge** (Beringia, connecting Siberia → Alaska) at least 10,000–40,000 years ago, when lower sea levels during the last Ice Age exposed the land bridge.
- Migrants spread from the Arctic Circle to the tip of South America, evolving into hundreds of tribes with unique languages, over thousands of years of independent development completely isolated from Afro-Eurasia.
- **Key Concept 1.1 framing:** Native societies were not static or "primitive" — they were dynamic, complex civilizations that continuously adapted to, and transformed, their environments through innovations in agriculture, resource use, and social/political organization. Comparison of these adaptations is a heavily tested skill for this module.
- The spread of **maize (corn) cultivation** northward from Mesoamerica, beginning roughly 5,000 years ago, was the single biggest driver of social complexity across North America — it enabled denser, more settled populations, food surpluses, social stratification, and specialized labor wherever it took hold.

### Central and South American Civilizations

| Civilization | Region | Key Traits |
|---|---|---|
| **Mayas** (300–900 AD) | Yucatán Peninsula (Guatemala, Belize, Mexico) | Rainforest city-states, sophisticated writing system, advanced calendar and astronomy; declined before European contact (causes debated — drought, warfare, overpopulation) |
| **Aztecs** (Mexica) | Central Mexico; capital **Tenochtitlán** (built on an island in Lake Texcoco, population ~200,000 — larger than any European city of the time) | Tribute empire ruling ~5–6 million subject peoples; chinampas (floating farms); corn cultivation; large-scale ritual human sacrifice tied to religious cosmology |
| **Incas** | Andes, Peru, western South America | Largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas (~10 million people); extensive road/relay system connecting the empire; terrace farming, potato cultivation; no written language but used **quipu** (knotted cords) for record-keeping |

- **Common Traits:** Highly organized, hierarchical societies; extensive trade networks; advanced calendars/astronomy; stable food surpluses supporting large urban populations and specialized non-farming classes (priests, artisans, warriors).

### North American Regional Cultures

| Region | Groups / Notes |
|---|---|
| **Southwest** | Hohokam, Anasazi (Ancestral Puebloans), Pueblos — adobe/cliff dwellings (e.g., Mesa Verde, Chaco Canyon), extensive maize irrigation canals in an arid environment, multi-story pueblo architecture, turquoise trade networks reaching into Mesoamerica |
| **Northwest** | Pacific coast (Chinook, Tlingit) — abundant salmon/fishing supported large, permanent, non-farming settlements; longhouses, carved totem poles, and the **potlatch** — a ceremonial feast redistributing wealth and displaying status |
| **Great Basin / Plateau** | Ute, Shoshone, Paiute — arid, resource-poor terrain forced small, mobile, seasonal hunter-gatherer bands (foraging for roots, seeds, small game); among the least densely populated regions |
| **Great Plains** | Nomadic buffalo (bison) hunters using bow and dog-drawn travois (no horses yet — horses arrive later via Spanish contact); teepees for mobility; some semi-sedentary riverine farming villages (e.g., Mandan, Hidatsa) along the Missouri River |
| **Mississippi Valley** | Mississippian mound-building culture; **Cahokia** (near present-day East St. Louis, IL) — largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico, population estimated **10,000–20,000+** at its 12th-century peak, with a central plaza and Monks Mound; **declined and was largely abandoned by ~1350, well before European contact** — historians debate causes including overpopulation, deforestation, resource depletion, and climatic stress, evidence that Native societies rose and fell on their own terms independent of Europe |
| **Northeast** | Descendants of Adena-Hopewell mound-building traditions; farming (the **"Three Sisters"** — corn, beans, and squash grown together) combined with hunting; permanent bark longhouse villages |
| **Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee, "People of the Longhouse")** | Alliance of five nations in this period — **Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida, Mohawk** (the Tuscarora joined later, in 1722, after this period) — bound by the **Great Law of Peace**, an oral constitution establishing a Grand Council for resolving disputes without warfare. Society was **matrilineal**: clan membership, land, and political nomination passed through the mother's line, and **Clan Mothers** selected and could remove male chiefs. Often cited (with debated influence) as a model some Founders referenced when designing federalism. |
| **Atlantic Seaboard** | Algonquian- and Iroquoian-speaking peoples (Powhatan, Lumbee, Cherokee) — mixed farming, hunting, and river/ocean fishing; bark or timber lodgings; would be the first groups to encounter permanent English settlement |

> **Comparison point:** Across these regions, social/political complexity tracked closely with food supply reliability — sedentary, agriculture-based societies (Southwest, Mississippian, Northeast) developed denser populations, social hierarchy, and centralized political structures, while resource-scarce regions (Great Basin, Great Plains before the horse) stayed small, mobile, and egalitarian. This cause-and-effect relationship between environment and social structure is a favorite AP comparison prompt.

## 1.3 — European Exploration in the Americas

### Why Europeans Came

1. **Changes in Thought & Technology** — Renaissance curiosity and revival of classical/scientific learning; the printing press (Gutenberg, 1450s) spread navigational and geographic knowledge quickly; improved shipbuilding (the Portuguese **caravel** — smaller, faster, able to sail against the wind); the magnetic compass and astrolabe for open-ocean navigation; gunpowder weapons gave Europeans a decisive military edge over Native forces. Portugal's **Prince Henry the Navigator** sponsored much of this early exploration technology and training in the early-to-mid 1400s.
2. **Religious Conflict** — Completion of the **Reconquista** (1492) — Spain's centuries-long reconquest of the Iberian Peninsula from Muslim rule — left Spain unified, militarized, and eager to fund new conquests; the Protestant Reformation (starting 1517) fractured Christian Europe and fueled competition between Catholic and Protestant powers to spread (and fund the spread of) their faith in the Americas.
3. **Expanding Trade** — Ottoman control of Constantinople (1453) blocked/taxed overland land routes to Asian luxury goods (spices, silk); Portugal responded by pioneering sea routes around Africa (Vasco da Gama reached India, 1498), while Spain gambled on reaching Asia by sailing *west* — the voyage that ran into the Americas instead.
4. **Slave Trading** — Portuguese began trading enslaved West Africans in the 15th century, initially to work sugar plantations on Atlantic islands (Madeira, the Azores); this sugar-plantation-plus-enslaved-labor model was later exported wholesale to the Caribbean and Brazil.
5. **Rise of Centralized Monarchies & Mercantilism** — Newly unified nation-states (Spain, Portugal, France, England) had the tax revenue and political stability to fund risky overseas ventures. Exploration and colonization were driven by **mercantilism** — the belief that a nation's wealth and power depended on accumulating gold/silver and maintaining a favorable balance of trade, making colonies valuable as sources of raw materials and captive markets.

> Just remember: **God, Glory, and Gold.**

### Dividing the Americas

| Agreement | Year | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Papal Line of Demarcation** | 1493 | Issued by Pope Alexander VI; divided newly claimed lands along a meridian — Spain (west) / Portugal (east) |
| **Treaty of Tordesillas** | 1494 | Spain and Portugal renegotiated the line further west; Portugal gets Brazil; Spain gets the rest of the Americas |

### English, French, and Later Claims
- **English Claims:** **John Cabot** (Italian-born, sailing for Henry VII, 1497) explored Newfoundland and claimed it for England — but England's colonization lagged nearly a century behind Spain's because the crown was consumed first by the Wars of the Roses and then by the upheaval of the English Reformation under Henry VIII and Elizabeth I. Once stabilized, **Sir Francis Drake** raided Spanish treasure ships (1570s–80s), and **Sir Walter Raleigh** sponsored the **Roanoke colony** (1585, resettled 1587) off present-day North Carolina — England's first colonization attempt, which failed when the entire colony vanished by 1590, leaving only the word **"CROATOAN"** carved into a post (the "Lost Colony" — likely explanation is that colonists assimilated into a nearby Native community rather than being massacred, though this remains debated).
- **French Claims:** **Giovanni da Verrazzano** (1524, sailing for France) explored the eastern coast searching for a Northwest Passage; **Jacques Cartier** (1534–1542) explored the St. Lawrence River and claimed the region for France, laying groundwork for the fur-trade-based colony of New France that would be formally founded at Quebec in 1608.

## 1.4 — The Columbian Exchange, Spanish Exploration, and Conquest

### Christopher Columbus
- An Italian navigator gained support from **Isabella and Ferdinand** of Spain in 1492, fresh off the Reconquista and eager to compete with Portugal's African trade routes.
- Landed in the **Bahamas, October 12, 1492**, believing he had reached the East Indies (hence "Indians") → triggered sustained, permanent European contact with the Americas. Columbus made three more voyages (through 1504) and died still believing he had reached Asia.

### The Columbian Exchange
The widespread transfer of plants, animals, people, diseases, and ideas between the Americas and the Old World — arguably the most significant ecological event since the end of the last Ice Age. Historian **Alfred Crosby** coined the term in 1972.

| Direction | Transferred |
|---|---|
| **Americas → Europe/Old World** | Corn (maize), potatoes, tomatoes, tobacco, beans, squash, cacao, peanuts; syphilis (disputed origin, but commonly cited as an American disease introduced to Europe) |
| **Europe/Old World → Americas** | Sugar cane, wheat, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, iron tools, guns; **smallpox, measles, influenza, and typhus** |

> **Impact — the demographic catastrophe:** Native populations had no prior exposure to Old World pathogens, producing **"virgin soil epidemics"** that killed with extraordinary speed. Historians' estimates vary, but a widely cited demographic study (Cook & Borah) found central Mexico's population collapsed from roughly **25 million in 1519 to barely over 1 million by 1605** — a decline of 90%+ in under a century. Across the hemisphere, historians estimate **80–95% of the pre-contact Native population died within about 100 years of contact**, making this arguably the largest demographic disaster in human history. Death came not only from disease directly but from its second-order effects: collapse of farming labor, famine, breakdown of political/religious authority, and social demoralization.
- The exchange ran both ways demographically: American crops (maize, potatoes) were calorie-dense and easy to grow, and their spread to Afro-Eurasia is credited by many historians with fueling major **population growth in Europe, Africa, and Asia** over the following centuries — even as the Americas' own population collapsed.

### Economic Changes in Europe
- **Decline of Feudalism → Rise of Capitalism**: Control of capital (money & machinery) overtook land ownership as the basis of wealth and status.
- **Joint-Stock Companies**: Spread financial risk of ocean voyages among many investors → encouraged exploration and, later, colonization ventures like the Virginia Company (Jamestown, 1607).
- **Price Revolution**: Massive inflows of Spanish silver and gold from the Americas caused significant inflation across Europe over the 16th century, reshaping European economies.

### Spanish Conquest

| Conquistador | Achievement |
|---|---|
| **Vasco Núñez de Balboa** | Crossed the Isthmus of Panama (1513), first European to see the Pacific Ocean from the Americas |
| **Ferdinand Magellan** | Led the first expedition to circumnavigate the globe (1519–1522, though Magellan himself died in the Philippines before completion) |
| **Hernán Cortés** | Landed 1519; conquered the Aztec Empire by 1521 with a small force, aided by Native allies (notably the Tlaxcalans, rivals of the Aztecs), superior weaponry, and — decisively — smallpox, which devastated Tenochtitlán during the siege |
| **Francisco Pizarro** | Conquered the Inca Empire by 1533, exploiting a recent Inca civil war and, again, a smallpox epidemic that had already killed the Inca emperor before Spanish forces arrived |

### Spanish Exploration and Borderlands North of Mexico
Spain also pushed into territory that is now the continental United States, well before England established a single permanent colony:
- **Juan Ponce de León** (1513) explored Florida (searching, per legend, for the "Fountain of Youth").
- **Francisco Vázquez de Coronado** (1540–1542) led a large expedition through the present-day Southwest and Great Plains searching for the mythical golden **"Seven Cities of Cíbola"** — found none, but mapped vast new territory and encountered Pueblo and Plains peoples.
- **Hernando de Soto** (1539–1542) marched through the Southeast and became the first European to reach the Mississippi River, leaving a trail of violence and disease among Mississippian peoples.
- **St. Augustine, Florida (1565)**, founded by Pedro Menéndez de Avilés, is the **oldest continuously occupied European-founded settlement in the present-day United States** — 42 years before Jamestown.
- **Juan de Oñate** colonized present-day New Mexico starting in 1598. After Acoma Pueblo warriors killed several Spanish soldiers (including Oñate's nephew), Oñate ordered brutal retaliation in the **1599 Acoma Massacre**, killing an estimated 800+ Acoma people and mutilating (amputating a foot from) male survivors — a stark early example of the violence underlying Spain's colonial project in North America.

## 1.5 — Labor, Slavery, and Caste in the Spanish Colonial System

### Labor Systems

- **Encomienda System**: Spanish colonists (*encomenderos*) were legally granted control over a Native population to extract forced labor and tribute in agriculture and mining, in exchange for a supposed duty to protect and Christianize them. In practice, the protective obligation was routinely ignored — encomienda functioned as a near-slavery system and was a direct cause of the population catastrophe described in 1.4 (overwork, malnutrition, and disease compounded each other).
- **Repartimiento System**: After reformers exposed encomienda's abuses, the **New Laws of 1542** (see 1.6) formally restricted it, and Spain gradually replaced it with **repartimiento** — a system of rotating, compulsory (but nominally paid) Native wage labor. In practice, conditions often remained brutal, and repartimiento is generally seen by historians as a relabeling rather than a genuine reform.
- **Cause and effect — the turn to African labor**: As Native populations collapsed from disease under the encomienda system, Spanish colonists faced a severe labor shortage just as demand for sugar and silver-mine labor was rising. This shortage was the direct economic driver behind the dramatic expansion of the Atlantic slave trade — Africans were seen (wrongly, and for self-serving reasons) as more disease-resistant and were already being trafficked via existing Portuguese/West African networks.
- **Asiento System**: A licensing system through which the Spanish crown contracted out (mostly to Portuguese, later Dutch and English, traders) the right to sell enslaved Africans into Spanish colonies, taxed per individual. Over the full multi-century span of the Atlantic slave trade, an estimated **10–15 million Africans** were forcibly transported to the Americas; mortality during the *Middle Passage* is estimated at roughly 10–20% of those who embarked, on top of high death tolls during capture and the overland march to African coastal ports.
- Atlantic slavery differed from earlier, more localized African and Mediterranean slave systems in becoming **hereditary and racially defined** — a child born to an enslaved mother was enslaved for life, regardless of the father's status, cementing race as the basis of a permanent underclass.

### African Resistance
- Escape (including forming independent **maroon communities** in remote terrain), sabotage, work slowdowns, armed revolts, and preserving cultural heritage (music, religious practice, kinship/community structures) despite forced separation from families.
- One of the earliest documented slave revolts in what is now the United States occurred in **1526** at the short-lived Spanish colony of **San Miguel de Gualdape** (coastal Georgia/South Carolina) — enslaved Africans rebelled and fled to live among local Native peoples, and the colony collapsed within months.

### Spanish Caste System (*Sistema de Castas*)
Spain formalized a racial hierarchy that determined legal status, taxation, and social privilege:

| Level | Group |
|---|---|
| Top | *Peninsulares* — Spaniards born in Spain |
| High-Middle | *Criollos* — Spaniards born in the colonies |
| Middle | Mixed-heritage individuals — **mestizo** (Spanish + Native), **mulatto** (Spanish + African) |
| Lower-Middle | **Zambo** (Native + African heritage) |
| Bottom | Enslaved Africans and subjugated Native Americans |

> The caste system entrenched racial discrimination and social stratification throughout the colonies — and established a template of race-based hierarchy that would echo through the rest of colonial and early American history.

## 1.6 — Cultural Interactions Between Europeans, Native Americans, and Africans

### European Policies Toward Native Americans

| Power | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Spanish** | Subjugation via encomienda, justified in advance by the **Requerimiento** (1513) — a legal document, read aloud in Spanish (often to Native people who couldn't understand it), declaring Spain's God-given right to the land and warning that resistance justified war and enslavement. Reformer Bartolomé de Las Casas advocated for Native rights → *New Laws of 1542*; culminated in the **Valladolid Debate** (1550–51, Las Casas vs. Sepúlveda, below). |
| **French** | Alliances, intermarriage, and fur trade conducted by small numbers of male traders (*coureurs des bois*, "runners of the woods") who lived among and traded directly with Native communities (e.g., the Huron); minimal colonial imposition, few permanent settlers, and — as a direct result — generally the best and most durable relations with Native Americans among the European powers. |
| **English** | Initial trade/peaceful relations (e.g., early contact with the Powhatan Confederacy in Virginia) → land conflicts and displacement as large numbers of English families arrived seeking permanent agricultural settlement, not just trade. Unlike Spanish or French colonization, English colonists largely intended to live *separately from*, not intermarried or integrated with, Native societies — a pattern that would define English/Native relations for the rest of the colonial era. |
| **Dutch** | (Colonization proper begins just after this period, ~1609 onward) Similar to the French — small trading-post model centered on the fur trade, minimal settlement. |

### The Valladolid Debate (1550–1551)
Convened by Emperor Charles V in Spain — the first formal European debate over the morality and legality of colonizing and enslaving another people:
- **Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda** argued, drawing on Aristotle's concept of "natural slavery," that Native peoples were inherently inferior and incapable of self-government, making war and forced subjugation a justified — even necessary — prelude to their conversion to Christianity.
- **Bartolomé de Las Casas** (a former encomendero turned Dominican friar) argued Native peoples were fully rational human beings entitled to just treatment and voluntary, peaceful conversion, and documented Spanish atrocities in detail (*A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies*, 1552).
- No formal winner was declared, but the debate influenced the **New Laws of 1542**, which nominally banned Native slavery. **Historiographical nuance commonly tested on the AP exam:** Las Casas's defense of Native Americans did not initially extend to Africans — he at one point endorsed importing enslaved Africans as an alternative labor source before later condemning African slavery too, a reminder that "humanitarian" reform in this period was selective and did not equate to opposition to slavery itself.

### Native American Strategies
- Allied with Europeans against rival tribes to gain military and trade advantages (a strategy that often backfired long-term, as European powers exploited these rivalries — an early instance of the "divide and conquer" dynamic that would recur throughout colonial history).
- Migrated westward to avoid conflict and disease, disrupting existing territorial balances among Native nations who had not yet had direct European contact.
- Fragmented tribal/linguistic identities (hundreds of distinct languages and polities, not a unified "Native America") hindered any hemisphere-wide unified resistance — a key structural reason European powers could establish footholds despite being vastly outnumbered.
- Some groups, particularly in the Spanish borderlands (e.g., Acoma Pueblo, 1.4), engaged in direct armed resistance against colonization, though often at devastating cost.

### African Cultural Influence
- Enslaved and free Africans introduced agricultural knowledge (rice cultivation techniques, in particular, would become crucial to English colonies in the next period), culinary traditions, and musical instruments/traditions (e.g., the banjo, derived from West African stringed instruments).
- Religious and cultural **syncretism** emerged as enslaved Africans blended West African spiritual practices with imposed Catholicism or Protestantism, producing new hybrid traditions across the Americas.
- Europeans rationalized slavery through selective biblical interpretation (e.g., the "Curse of Ham") and emerging pseudo-scientific racial theories, providing an ideological justification that hardened over time into systemic racism — a legacy that would shape American society for centuries beyond this period.
