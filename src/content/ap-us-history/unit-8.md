# Period 8 — Cold War (1945–1980)

---

## 8.1 — Contextualizing Period 8

### Emerging From World War II
- WWII left the U.S. as one of two global superpowers, with its homeland untouched and its economy dominant, while the Soviet Union emerged as its ideological and military rival.
- Wartime cooperation between the U.S. and USSR quickly gave way to mutual suspicion over the postwar order in Europe.
- Domestically, the war set up decades of economic growth, suburbanization, and demographic change (the Baby Boom) — the backdrop against which the Civil Rights Movement and 1960s social change unfolded.
- This period covers the Cold War abroad and a series of overlapping social movements at home.

### The Wartime Conferences That Set the Stage
- **Yalta Conference (Feb. 1945):** FDR, Churchill, Stalin met near war's end; Stalin agreed to free elections in Soviet-occupied Eastern Europe and to enter the war against Japan; agreement to divide Germany into occupation zones.
- Stalin's failure to honor free elections in Poland and elsewhere became an early flashpoint and a lasting source of U.S. distrust.
- **Potsdam Conference (July 1945):** Truman (now president after FDR's death, April 1945), Churchill/Attlee, Stalin met to finalize German occupation terms; Truman informed Stalin of the atomic bomb.
- **Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (Aug. 1945)** ended WWII in the Pacific and gave the U.S. a temporary nuclear monopoly — a major source of leverage (and Soviet anxiety) in the earliest Cold War years.
- **United Nations founded (1945):** intended as a forum for collective security, but the U.S.-USSR rivalry frequently paralyzed its Security Council (each held veto power).

### Two Throughlines of the Period (College Board framing)
- **Key Concept 8.1:** The U.S. asserted and worked to maintain a position of global leadership after WWII, with far-reaching domestic and international consequences (the Cold War, containment, military-industrial buildup).
- **Key Concept 8.2:** New movements for civil rights and liberal efforts to expand the role of government (Great Society) generated a range of political and cultural responses, including a growing conservative backlash that would shape Period 9.
- Domestically: reconversion from a wartime to a peacetime economy, the GI Bill, and sustained defense spending (a "military-industrial complex," in Eisenhower's later phrase) fueled two decades of economic growth and suburbanization.

## 8.2 — The Cold War from 1945 to 1980

### Origins
- Intense U.S.-Soviet rivalry from late 1940s until Soviet collapse (1991).
- Historical mistrust since 1917 Bolshevik Revolution; deepened by Soviet-Nazi Non-Aggression Pact (1939).

### Kennan and the Intellectual Origins of Containment
- **George Kennan's "Long Telegram" (Feb. 22, 1946):** sent from the U.S. embassy in Moscow; argued the Soviets were inherently expansionist and could not be placated through diplomacy, but could be checked by patient, long-term counter-pressure.
- **"X Article" (*Foreign Affairs*, July 1947):** Kennan published (anonymously, as "X") "The Sources of Soviet Conduct," publicly introducing the term **containment** as the strategic basis for U.S. Cold War policy.
- **Historiography — who started the Cold War?** Orthodox historians (writing mostly in the 1950s) blamed Soviet aggression and expansionism; revisionist historians (1960s–70s) blamed U.S. economic imperialism and atomic diplomacy; post-revisionists argue both sides acted from genuine (if mutually reinforcing) security fears and miscommunication. This debate is a common AP short-answer prompt.

### U.S.-Soviet Friction Points

| Issue | Details |
|---|---|
| Germany | Divided into Soviet, French, British, U.S. zones; Soviets wanted heavy reparations; U.S./Britain prioritized recovery |
| Atomic Energy | Soviets rejected U.S. Baruch Plan for international nuclear regulation → seen as aggression |
| **Iron Curtain** (March 1946) | Churchill (with Truman present): "iron curtain" descended across Europe; called for Western unity |

### Containment Policy (1947)
- Truman adopted **containment** — prevent Soviet expansion without provoking war.
- **Truman Doctrine:** $400M in economic/military aid to Greece and Turkey against communist threats.

### The Marshall Plan (1948)
- George Marshall proposed large-scale U.S. economic aid to rebuild Western Europe.
- Congress approved **$12 billion** for Western Europe over 4 years.
- Soviets and Eastern Europe **refused.**
- **Success:** Spurred Western European recovery; prevented spread of communism.
- **Effect:** Deepened East-West divide.

### Berlin Airlift (1948–49)
- Soviets blockaded West Berlin to force Allies out.
- Truman initiated **11-month airlift** of essential supplies.
- Soviets lifted blockade (May 1949); Germany formally divided: **West Germany** (U.S. ally) and **East Germany** (Soviet satellite).

### NATO (1949)
- First U.S. permanent military alliance with Western Europe and Canada.
- Soviets countered with **Warsaw Pact (1955)**.

### National Security Act (1947)
- Created: **Department of Defense, National Security Council (NSC), Central Intelligence Agency (CIA)**.
- Instituted peacetime draft (1948).

### Arms Race
- U.S. atomic monopoly ended (1949) when Soviets tested their own.
- **Hydrogen bomb** added to U.S. arsenal (1952).
- **NSC-68 (1950):** Called for significant increase in defense spending; global alliances against communism.

### Korean War (1950–1953)
- North Korea (Soviet-backed) invaded South Korea (June 25, 1950).
- Truman applied containment; UN authorized force; U.S. troops under Gen. **Douglas MacArthur**.
- **Inchon Landing** pushed North Koreans back → Chinese counterattacked → stalemate at 38th parallel.
- **MacArthur dismissed** for publicly advocating expansion of war into China.
- **Korean Armistice (July 1953):** Eisenhower + nuclear threats + Stalin's death → armistice; Korea remained divided.

### Eisenhower's "New Look" (1953–1961)
- Secretary of State **John Foster Dulles** championed **"massive retaliation"** — threatening large-scale nuclear response to Soviet aggression — to deter communism more cheaply than maintaining large conventional forces ("more bang for the buck").
- Relied heavily on the CIA for covert action rather than open military commitment:
  - **Operation Ajax (Iran, 1953):** CIA helped overthrow elected Prime Minister **Mohammad Mossadegh** (who had nationalized British oil interests) and restored the Shah to power — later fueled anti-American resentment culminating in the 1979 Iranian Revolution.
  - **Guatemala (1954):** CIA-backed coup overthrew president **Jacobo Árbenz** after his land reforms threatened U.S.-owned United Fruit Company holdings.
- **SEATO (Southeast Asia Treaty Organization, 1954):** collective-defense pact modeled on NATO, formed partly in response to the French defeat in Vietnam.

### Cold War Flashpoints (Eisenhower Era)

| Event | Date | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Sputnik (1957)** | 1957 | Soviet first satellite → U.S. panic; Congress passed National Defense Education Act (NDEA); NASA created |
| **Second Berlin Crisis** | 1958–59 | Khrushchev demanded West withdraw from West Berlin; defused at Camp David |
| **U-2 Incident (1960)** | 1960 | U.S. spy plane shot down over USSR; killed Paris summit |

### Election of 1960
- **John F. Kennedy (D)** narrowly defeated **Richard Nixon (R)** — closest popular-vote margin of the 20th century.
- First televised presidential debates: Kennedy's confident TV presence contrasted with a pale, sweating Nixon; radio listeners reportedly scored the debate for Nixon, TV viewers for Kennedy — an early sign of television's growing power in politics.
- Kennedy accused the Eisenhower administration of allowing a **"missile gap"** with the USSR (later shown to be exaggerated).

### Cold War (Kennedy Era) — "Flexible Response"
- Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with **flexible response**: building up conventional forces and special-operations capability (e.g., the **Green Berets**) so the U.S. could respond proportionally to threats short of nuclear war — reflected the belief that over-reliance on nuclear deterrence left no good options in Third World conflicts like Vietnam.
- **Peace Corps (1961)** and **Alliance for Progress (1961):** soft-power/economic tools — Peace Corps sent volunteers abroad for development work; Alliance for Progress offered aid to Latin America to counter the appeal of Castro-style revolution.

| Event | Date | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Bay of Pigs (1961)** | 1961 | CIA-planned Cuban exile invasion failed; strengthened Castro-Soviet ties |
| **Berlin Wall (1961)** | 1961 | East Germany built wall; Kennedy: "Ich bin ein Berliner" (1963) |
| **Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)** | 1962 | U.S. discovered Soviet missiles in Cuba; naval blockade; Khrushchev removed missiles in exchange for U.S. pledge not to invade Cuba + removal of U.S. missiles from Turkey; **Nuclear Test Ban Treaty (1963)** |

### Nixon's Détente
- **Realist foreign policy** with Henry Kissinger — pragmatic; reduce Cold War tensions.
- **Visit to China (1972):** Nixon met Mao Zedong; initiated diplomatic relations; U.S. recognized Communist China (1979).
- **SALT I Agreement:** Froze number of ballistic missiles with nuclear warheads; limited ABMs.
- Nixon's resignation (1974) + fall of South Vietnam (1975) → loss of U.S. trust.

### The End of Détente (Carter Era)
- **SALT II (1979):** further limited strategic arms, but Carter withdrew it from Senate ratification after the Soviet invasion.
- **Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (Dec. 1979):** ended détente; Carter responded with the **Carter Doctrine** (U.S. would use force to protect access to the Persian Gulf), a grain embargo against the USSR, and a boycott of the 1980 Moscow Olympics.
- Set the stage for the renewed Cold War confrontation and defense buildup of the Reagan years (Period 9).

### Containment vs. Détente — Compare/Contrast

| | Containment (Truman–Eisenhower) | Détente (Nixon–Ford–early Carter) |
|---|---|---|
| Core assumption | Communism is a monolithic threat that must be stopped everywhere | Communist states can be dealt with pragmatically; not all communism is equally threatening to U.S. interests |
| Preferred tools | Alliances (NATO/SEATO), foreign aid, covert action, military intervention | Diplomacy, arms-control treaties (SALT I), trade, summitry |
| Attitude toward USSR/China | Treat as unified enemy bloc | Exploit Sino-Soviet split; open relations with China (1972) |
| Ended by | — | Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979) revived Cold War tension |

## 8.3 — The Red Scare

### Anti-Communist Measures

| Measure | Details |
|---|---|
| **Loyalty Review Board (1947–51)** | Investigated 3M+ federal employees; thousands dismissed |
| **HUAC (House Un-American Activities Committee)** | Investigated alleged communist influence in government, unions, and Hollywood; the **"Hollywood Ten"** (writers/directors) refused to testify, were jailed for contempt, and were **blacklisted** from the film industry |
| **Alger Hiss Case (1948)** | Former State Department official accused of espionage by ex-communist **Whittaker Chambers**; freshman Congressman **Richard Nixon** built his career pursuing the case; Hiss convicted of perjury (1950) — fed public fear that communists had infiltrated the highest levels of government |
| **Smith Act Prosecutions** | Leaders of American Communist Party jailed; upheld in *Dennis v. United States* (1951) |
| **McCarran Internal Security Act (1950)** | Made it illegal to support totalitarian governments; authorized detention camps |
| **Rosenberg Case** | Julius and Ethel Rosenberg executed (1953) for passing atomic secrets to Soviets |

- These episodes are sometimes grouped as the **"Second Red Scare"** (the first followed WWI) — reflected genuine espionage concerns (Soviet spies did operate in the U.S. government) but also produced serious civil-liberties abuses through guilt-by-association and unproven accusations.

### McCarthyism
- Senator Joseph McCarthy claimed Communist infiltration of government; used unproven accusations.
- **Army-McCarthy Hearings (1954):** Televised; exposed McCarthy's bullying tactics → Senate censured him.
- Economic boom and Korean armistice shifted public attention away from communist fears.

## 8.4 — Economy after 1945

### Postwar Economy Under Truman
- **Reconversion (1945–46):** shifting factories from wartime to peacetime production caused shortages, inflation (price controls lifted), and the largest strike wave in U.S. history (over 4.6 million workers, incl. steel, auto, coal, rail) as unions sought wage gains after wartime no-strike pledges ended.
- **Employment Act of 1946:** Created Council of Economic Advisers; less ambitious than Truman hoped.
- **GI Bill (1944):** 15 million veterans received education funds, home/farm/business loans → boosted economy; Black veterans faced discrimination in how benefits were administered (esp. in the South), widening the postwar racial wealth gap.

### Baby Boom
- 50 million babies born 1945–1960 → lasting economic/social effects.
- One-third of married women employed by 1960.

### Suburban Growth
- **Levittown** — mass-produced suburban development; White families only; federal policies supported segregation in housing.
- Cities became increasingly poor and racially divided by 1960s.

### Taft-Hartley Act (1947)
- Probusiness law passed over Truman's veto:
  - Outlawed closed shop (union membership before hiring).
  - Allowed "right to work" laws.
  - Banned secondary boycotts.
  - President could impose 80-day cooling-off period before strikes.

### Election of 1948
- Truman defeated Dewey; proposed **Fair Deal** (national health insurance, civil rights) — mostly blocked by Congress.

### Eisenhower's Presidency (1953–1961)
- **Interstate Highway System (1956 Highway Act):** 42,000 miles; boosted economy, suburban growth.
- Steady economic growth; low inflation; average family had double the real income of 1920s.

### Kennedy's New Frontier
- Domestic agenda: education, health care, urban renewal, civil rights.
- Many programs struggled in Congress → passed under LBJ.

### Nixon's Domestic Policy
- **New Federalism:** Shifted responsibilities to state and local governments; $30B in block grants.
- **Stagflation:** 90-day wage/price freeze; took dollar off gold standard; import surtax.

## 8.5 — Culture after 1945

### Consumer Culture
- **Television:** By early 1960s, one set per 3.3 Americans; reinforced conservative suburban values (*Leave It to Beaver*, *Father Knows Best*).
- **Advertising** and suburban shopping centers; credit cards; chain restaurants.

### Women's Roles
- Baby boom and suburban living made homemaking a full-time job.
- Despite increasing workforce participation, lower wages and traditional expectations persisted.

### Beatniks
- **Beat Generation (Jack Kerouac, Allen Ginsberg):** Spontaneity, rebellion against norms; laid groundwork for 1960s youth rebellion.

### Rock and Roll
- **Elvis Presley** and others blended Black rhythm-and-blues with country/pop for a mass White teenage audience — controversial for both its Black musical roots and its perceived sexuality; an early sign of youth culture breaking from parents' tastes and, indirectly, of White teens' growing exposure to Black culture.

### Critiques of Conformity
- **David Riesman, *The Lonely Crowd* (1950)** and **William H. Whyte, *The Organization Man* (1956):** sociological bestsellers criticizing suburban/corporate conformity and the loss of individualism — intellectual precursors to Beat and 1960s counterculture critiques.
- **Cold War religiosity:** "under God" added to the Pledge of Allegiance (1954); "In God We Trust" made the national motto (1956) — reflected framing of the Cold War as a struggle between godly America and "godless" Soviet communism.

### Kennedy's Assassination
- **November 22, 1963, Dallas, Texas:** Kennedy shot; Lee Harvey Oswald killed on live TV → tragic end to postwar optimism.

## 8.6 — Early Steps in the Civil Rights Movement (1945–1960)

### Presidential Leadership
- **Jackie Robinson (1947):** broke Major League Baseball's color line with the Brooklyn Dodgers — high-profile early sign of shifting racial attitudes, though most institutions remained segregated.
- **To Secure These Rights (1947):** report of Truman's President's Committee on Civil Rights, recommended ending segregation and lynching; politically risky for Truman but signaled growing federal engagement with civil rights.
- **Harry Truman (1948):** Executive Order 9981 — desegregated U.S. military.
- Cold War context: racial segregation was hypocritical given U.S. promotion of freedom vs. communism — Soviet propaganda regularly cited American segregation to discredit U.S. claims of moral leadership, giving civil rights a foreign-policy dimension.

### Desegregating Schools
- **NAACP** worked through courts to overturn *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896).
- **Brown v. Board of Education (1954):** NAACP lawyer **Thurgood Marshall** argued before Supreme Court; Chief Justice **Earl Warren's** unanimous decision: "separate facilities are inherently unequal."

### Resistance in the South
- 101 members of Congress signed "Southern Manifesto" condemning the ruling.
- **1956 Little Rock:** Governor Orval Faubus blocked 9 Black students; Eisenhower sent federal troops.
- By 1964: only **2% of Black students** in South attended integrated schools.

### Emmett Till (August 1955)
- 14-year-old Black Chicagoan **Emmett Till** was brutally murdered in Mississippi after allegedly whistling at a White woman; his mother held an open-casket funeral and let *Jet* magazine publish photos of his mutilated body, exposing the brutality of Southern racism to a national audience.
- His killers were acquitted by an all-White jury; the case galvanized Black activism and is often cited alongside *Brown* and Montgomery as a catalyst for the mass movement phase of civil rights.

### Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–56)
- **Rosa Parks** arrested for refusing to give up bus seat.
- Led by **Martin Luther King Jr.** → Supreme Court ruled segregation on buses unconstitutional.

### Nonviolent Protests
- **SCLC (1957):** MLK Jr. mobilized churches in civil rights struggle.
- **Sit-in Movement (1960, Greensboro, NC):** Protested segregated lunch counters → formed **SNCC (Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee)**.

### Federal Laws
- Eisenhower signed Civil Rights Acts of **1957 and 1960** — established Civil Rights Commission; protected Black voting rights.

## 8.7 — America as a World Power

### Middle East

| Event | Date | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Suez Crisis** | 1956 | Nasser nationalized Suez Canal; Britain/France/Israel attacked; Eisenhower condemned → decline of British/French influence; U.S. prominence rose |
| **Eisenhower Doctrine** | 1957 | U.S. aid to any Middle Eastern country threatened by communism |
| **OPEC formed** | 1960 | Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Iraq, Iran, Venezuela; coordinate oil policies |
| **Yom Kippur War** | 1973 | Syria/Egypt attacked Israel; U.S. supported Israel; Arab OPEC oil embargo → global oil crisis |
| **Camp David Accords** | 1978 | Carter brokered Egypt-Israel peace; Egypt first Arab nation to recognize Israel |
| **Iran Hostage Crisis** | 1979 | Khomeini overthrew Shah; 50+ Americans held 444 days; failed rescue mission damaged Carter's presidency |

### Latin America
- **Kennedy's Alliance for Progress (1961):** economic aid to Latin America meant to counter the appeal of Castro-style revolution through development (see 8.2); had limited long-term success.
- **Johnson:** prioritized anti-communism over democracy; troops sent to Dominican Republic (1965) to prevent communist takeover; resembled Roosevelt's "Big Stick" approach.
- Pattern across the period: U.S. covert and military intervention in Iran (1953), Guatemala (1954), Cuba (1961), and the Dominican Republic (1965) reflected a consistent Cold War priority — stopping the spread of communism, even at the expense of supporting undemocratic regimes.

### Panama Canal
- Carter renegotiated 1903 treaty; Senate ratified (1978) — transfer of Canal to Panama by 2000.

### Human Rights Diplomacy (Carter)
- Promoted human rights globally; appointed Andrew Young as UN ambassador.
- Cut aid to Argentina and Chile over human rights violations.

## 8.8 — The Vietnam War

### Background
- After WWII, Vietnamese (Ho Chi Minh) resisted French recolonization.
- U.S. supported French; China and USSR supported Viet Minh.
- **French defeat at Dien Bien Phu (1954):** Geneva Accords divided Vietnam at 17th parallel.
- **Domino Theory (Eisenhower):** If one country falls to communism, neighbors follow.

### Escalation Under LBJ

| Event | Date | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Tonkin Gulf Resolution (1964)** | 1964 | Alleged attack on U.S. ships → "blank check" for Johnson to take "all necessary measures"; never a formal declaration of war |
| **Operation Rolling Thunder (1965)** | 1965 | Prolonged bombing of North Vietnam; 184,000 troops by end of 1965; 485,000 by 1967 |
| **Credibility Gap** | — | Misinformation from military leaders; public lost trust in government |
| **Tet Offensive (Jan 1968)** | 1968 | Surprise Vietcong attack on multiple cities; military failure for N. Vietnam but political victory; demoralized American public |
| **Johnson Ends Escalation** | March 31, 1968 | Limited bombing; sought peace talks; declined reelection |

### Nixon's Vietnam Policy
- **Vietnamization:** Gradual withdrawal of U.S. troops; transfer combat to South Vietnamese.
- U.S. troops: 540,000 (1969) → under 30,000 (1972).
- Invaded Cambodia → protests; **Kent State and Jackson State** deaths.
- Senate repealed Gulf of Tonkin Resolution.
- **My Lai massacre** and **Pentagon Papers** (exposed government deceptions) revealed to public.
- **Paris Accords (1973):** U.S. troop withdrawal; POW return; war continued between North and South Vietnam.
- **War Powers Act (1973):** President must report to Congress within 48 hours of military action; Congressional approval needed for actions >60 days.
- **Fall of Saigon (April 1975):** North Vietnamese forces captured South Vietnam's capital shortly after the last U.S. personnel evacuated; Vietnam reunified under communist rule.

### The Draft and Its Inequities
- College deferments let wealthier and White students avoid combat at higher rates; working-class and Black draftees served (and died) in disproportionate numbers, feeding accusations that the war's burdens were unfairly distributed.
- **Draft lottery (1969):** replaced the deferment-heavy system with a birthdate lottery in an attempt to make conscription more equitable — did not end the underlying protest movement.
- **"Vietnam Syndrome":** the postwar public and political reluctance to commit U.S. ground troops to foreign conflicts, a legacy that shaped foreign policy debates into the 1980s and beyond.

## 8.9 — The Great Society

### LBJ's War on Poverty
- Inspired by Michael Harrington's *The Other America* (1962) — 40 million Americans in poverty.
- LBJ declared "unconditional war on poverty" (1964).
- **Economic Opportunity Act (1964):** created the **Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO)** to run the war on poverty's signature programs:
  - **Job Corps** — job training for disadvantaged youth.
  - **VISTA (Volunteers in Service to America)** — a domestic Peace Corps.
  - **Head Start** — early-childhood education for low-income children.
  - **Community Action Program** — funded local antipoverty agencies with "maximum feasible participation" of the poor themselves.

### Election of 1964
- LBJ won by landslide (61% popular vote); Goldwater (R) wanted to end the welfare state.
- Democrats controlled 2/3 of Congress → enabled sweeping reforms.

### Great Society Programs

| Program | Year | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Food Stamp Act | 1964 | Federal assistance for low-income food purchases |
| Medicare | 1965 | Health insurance for those 65+ |
| Medicaid | 1965 | Medical care for poor and disabled |
| Elementary and Secondary Education Act | 1965 | Funded poor school districts; special education |
| Higher Education Act | 1965 | Federal scholarships for postsecondary education |
| **Immigration Act** | 1965 | Abolished discriminatory national-origin quotas → opened U.S. to global immigration |
| Child Nutrition Act | 1966 | Added breakfast to school lunch program |

- **New cabinet departments:** Department of Housing and Urban Development (**HUD**, 1965) and Department of Transportation (1966) institutionalized federal roles in urban policy and infrastructure.

### Immigration Changes Post-1965
- By 1980s: 47% of immigrants from Latin America, 37% from Asia, fewer than 13% from Europe.

### Assessing the Great Society
- **Successes:** the national poverty rate fell from about 22% (1959) to about 11% (1973); Medicare/Medicaid dramatically expanded health coverage for the elderly and poor; school funding for poor districts increased.
- **Criticisms:** conservatives (and later Reagan-era politicians) argued the programs created dependency and expanded federal power without solving poverty's root causes; the Vietnam War increasingly diverted funding and political attention away from Great Society programs after 1965–66.
- This debate over the proper size and effectiveness of the federal safety net carried directly into Period 9's conservative resurgence.

## 8.10 — The African American Civil Rights Movement (1960s)

### Freedom Rides (1961)
- Organized by **CORE (Congress of Racial Equality)**, led by **James Farmer**; Black and White activists rode interstate buses into the Deep South to test enforcement of Supreme Court rulings banning segregated interstate travel.
- Riders were attacked by mobs (a bus firebombed near Anniston, Alabama) with local police often failing to intervene; forced the Kennedy administration to send federal marshals and push the Interstate Commerce Commission to enforce desegregation.

### Leadership of MLK Jr.
- Civil rights activists (freedom riders) faced violence while registering voters and integrating public spaces.
- **James Meredith integrates Ole Miss (1962):** first Black student at the University of Mississippi; required federal troops to quell rioting.
- **George Wallace "stands in the schoolhouse door" (1963):** Alabama governor symbolically blocked Black students from enrolling at the University of Alabama before yielding to federalized National Guard troops; famously vowed "segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever."
- **1963 Birmingham:** MLK jailed during illegal march → **"Letter from Birmingham Jail"** — nonviolent protest upholds democratic values; Birmingham police (Bull Connor) used fire hoses and police dogs on peaceful protesters, including children, and the televised brutality shocked national opinion.
- **16th Street Baptist Church bombing (Sept. 1963):** Ku Klux Klan members bombed a Black church in Birmingham, killing four young girls — galvanized support for stronger federal civil rights legislation.
- Inspired Kennedy to support a tougher civil rights bill.

### March on Washington (1963)
- ~200,000 participants; MLK delivered **"I Have a Dream"** speech.

### Federal Civil Rights Legislation

| Law | Year | Key Provision |
|---|---|---|
| **Civil Rights Act of 1964** | 1964 | Segregation illegal in all public facilities; created Equal Employment Opportunity Commission |
| **24th Amendment** | 1964 | Abolished poll tax |
| **Voting Rights Act of 1965** | 1965 | Ended literacy tests; federal registrars in areas of discrimination |
| **Civil Rights Act of 1968 (Fair Housing Act)** | 1968 | Banned discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of housing; passed one week after MLK's assassination, partly as a memorial response |

### "Bloody Sunday" (March 1965)
- Voting rights march from Selma to Montgomery met with violent resistance.
- Televised violence → national outrage → passage of Voting Rights Act.

### Malcolm X and Black Power
- **Elijah Muhammad (Black Muslims):** Black nationalism; separatism.
- **Malcolm X:** Advocated self-defense "by any means necessary"; criticized MLK's integrationism as naive; broke with the Nation of Islam and moderated his views after a 1964 pilgrimage to Mecca (Hajj); assassinated 1965 by rival Nation of Islam members.
- **Stokely Carmichael (SNCC):** Rejected nonviolence; promoted "black power" (coined the slogan in 1966).
- **Black Panthers (1966):** Huey Newton, Bobby Seale; revolutionary socialist movement combining armed self-defense with community programs (free breakfast for children, health clinics).

### MLK vs. Malcolm X — Compare/Contrast

| | Martin Luther King Jr. | Malcolm X (early period) |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy | Nonviolent direct action, civil disobedience | Self-defense; skeptical that nonviolence alone would win rights |
| Goal | Integration into mainstream American society | Black separatism and self-reliance (Nation of Islam period) |
| Appeal to | Christian churches, moral conscience of White America, federal government | Urban North, those skeptical that White America would grant rights voluntarily |
| Base | Southern churches (SCLC) | Nation of Islam, later broader Black nationalist audience |
| Legacy | Landmark federal legislation (1964, 1965 acts) | Inspired the later Black Power and Black Panther movements |

- Despite their differences, both men were responding to the same reality of persistent racism after legal segregation's collapse, and their approaches increasingly converged near the end of their lives — MLK grew more critical of capitalism and the Vietnam War, while Malcolm X moderated his separatism after 1964.

### Urban Riots
- **Watts riots (1965):** 34 deaths; 700+ buildings destroyed.
- Race riots continued through 1968.
- **Kerner Commission:** Blamed racism and segregation; warned U.S. becoming "two societies, one black, one white."

### Assassination of MLK Jr. (April 4, 1968)
- Shot in Memphis, Tennessee, where he had gone to support striking sanitation workers; assassin **James Earl Ray** convicted.
- Triggered riots in over 100 cities; Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1968 (Fair Housing Act) within days, partly in response.

## 8.11 — The Civil Rights Movement Expands

### The Women's Movement
- Factors: increased education and employment; civil rights movement; sexual revolution.
- **Betty Friedan:** *The Feminine Mystique* (1963) → co-founded **NOW (National Organization for Women, 1966)**.
- **Equal Pay Act (1963)** and **Civil Rights Act (1964)** — prohibited sex-based discrimination; Title VII of the 1964 act (banning employment discrimination "on the basis of sex") was added partly as a strategic (and initially half-hearted) amendment, but became a major tool for women's workplace rights.
- **Title IX (Education Amendments of 1972):** Banned sex discrimination in any federally funded education program; transformed girls'/women's access to school athletics and professional/graduate programs.
- **ERA (Equal Rights Amendment, 1972):** Passed Congress; failed to be ratified by 38 states, in large part due to conservative opposition led by **Phyllis Schlafly**'s **STOP ERA** campaign, which argued the amendment would eliminate legal protections for homemakers and mandate women in combat — an early and effective mobilization of the emerging New Right.
- Achievements: more women in business, law, medicine, politics; concept of "glass ceiling" persisted.

### The Warren Court and Individual Rights

*Chief Justice Earl Warren (1953–1969) — emphasized individual rights over property rights.*

| Case | Year | Ruling |
|---|---|---|
| **Brown v. Board** | 1954 | Ended school segregation |
| **Mapp v. Ohio** | 1961 | Illegally seized evidence inadmissible |
| **Gideon v. Wainwright** | 1963 | State courts must provide attorneys for those who can't afford one |
| **Escobedo v. Illinois** | 1964 | Right to attorney during police questioning |
| **Griswold v. Connecticut** | 1965 | Struck down a state ban on contraceptives for married couples; established a constitutional **"right to privacy"** (though not explicitly named in the Constitution) that later grounded *Roe v. Wade* (1973) |
| **Miranda v. Arizona** | 1966 | Police must inform arrested individuals of their rights ("Miranda rights") |

### The Chicano Movement
- **César Chávez** and **Dolores Huerta** founded the **National Farm Workers Association (1962)**, which became the **United Farm Workers (UFW)**; organized mostly Mexican American migrant laborers for better wages and working conditions.
- **Delano grape strike and nationwide grape boycott (1965–1970):** consumer boycott pressured growers into recognizing the UFW and signing contracts with higher wages and benefits — modeled directly on civil rights movement nonviolent tactics.
- Broader Chicano movement also pushed for bilingual education, Mexican American studies, and political representation in the Southwest.

### The American Indian Movement (AIM)
- **AIM founded (1968)** by Russell Means, Dennis Banks, and others to address poverty, treaty violations, and demand greater tribal self-determination.
- **Alcatraz Island occupation (1969–1971):** activists occupied the former federal prison island to draw attention to broken treaties and demand the land be used for Native American purposes.
- **Wounded Knee occupation (1973):** AIM activists occupied the South Dakota site of the 1890 massacre for 71 days to protest corruption and demand federal treaty enforcement; ended in a violent standoff with U.S. marshals and FBI agents.
- Reflected the broader pattern of the era: groups beyond the Black civil rights movement (women, Chicano Americans, Native Americans, gay Americans) adopted its language and tactics of direct action and legal challenge.

### Gay Rights Movement
- **Stonewall Inn Riot (1969):** Police raid sparked riots → birth of modern gay rights movement.
- By mid-1970s: homosexuality no longer classified as mental illness.

## 8.12 — Youth Culture of the 1960s

### Baby Boom Generation
- College enrollment quadrupled (1945–1970).
- Not shaped by Depression or WWII; influenced by civil rights and demands for equality.

### New Left / SDS
- **Students for a Democratic Society (SDS, 1962):** Port Huron Statement — participatory democracy.
- **Free Speech Movement (Berkeley, 1964):** First major student protest; demanded political freedom on campus.

### Anti-Vietnam Protests
- Draft-card burning; sit-ins; opposition to ROTC; disrupted campuses.
- **Assassinations of MLK Jr. and RFK (1968)** intensified protests.
- **Chicago Convention (1968):** Democratic National Convention protests → "police riot."
- **Weather Underground:** Most radical SDS faction; bombings of Capitol, Pentagon, State Dept.

### Election of 1968
- A year of national trauma: Tet Offensive, LBJ's withdrawal from the race, MLK's and RFK's assassinations, and the chaotic Chicago convention.
- **Richard Nixon (R)** narrowly defeated **Hubert Humphrey (D, LBJ's VP)**; third-party candidate **George Wallace (American Independent Party)** won 5 states running on segregationist, law-and-order appeals, revealing a fractured Democratic coalition.
- Nixon campaigned on restoring "law and order" and a secret plan to end the Vietnam War — an early expression of the "silent majority" strategy (see 8.14).

### Counterculture
- "Hippies" — rejected mainstream values; communal living; drug use.
- Folk music (Baez, Dylan); rock (Beatles, Rolling Stones, Joplin, Morrison).
- **Woodstock (Aug. 1969):** three-day music festival in rural New York drew roughly 400,000 attendees; became the era's iconic symbol of countercultural peace and community (contrasted a few months later by violence at the Altamont Free Concert).

### Sexual Revolution
- Birth control pill (1960); antibiotics for STDs.
- Premarital sex, contraception, abortion, homosexuality became more visible and accepted.
- 1980s backlash: illegitimate births, AIDS crisis.

## 8.13 — The Environment and Natural Resources (1968–1980)

### Environmental Movement
- **Rachel Carson, *Silent Spring* (1962):** exposed the ecological dangers of pesticides like DDT; widely credited as the founding text of the modern environmental movement (led to a nationwide DDT ban in 1972).
- **Santa Barbara oil spill (1969)** and the **Cuyahoga River catching fire (1969, Cleveland)** — high-profile industrial pollution disasters that turned public opinion and helped push Nixon and Congress toward environmental legislation.
- **Earth Day (1970):** 100,000 in NYC; millions nationwide.
- Apollo photos of Earth from space ("Earthrise") raised awareness.
- Sierra Club membership: 123,000 (1960) → 819,000 (1970).

### Key Legislation

| Law | Year | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **EPA (Environmental Protection Agency)** | 1970 | Enforce pollution, radiation, pesticide, waste programs |
| **Clean Air Act** | 1970 | Regulated air emissions; EPA set standards |
| **Endangered Species Act** | 1973 | Protected critically endangered species |

### Oil Crisis and Fuel Economy
- 1973 OPEC embargo → speed limits reduced; fuel economy standards enacted.

### Antinuclear Movement
- Peaked in 1970s–1980s; Three Mile Island accident (PA) increased concerns; slowed nuclear plant construction.

## 8.14 — Society in Transition

### American Society
- By 1970s: half of Americans in South and West; population aging; minorities = 25% by 1990.
- Cultural pluralism replacing the melting pot model.

### Nixon's Southern Strategy
- Appealed to "silent majority" — conservative Americans disillusioned by civil rights and counterculture.
- United southern Whites, northern blue-collar workers, suburbanites into a Republican majority.

### Watergate Scandal
- Break-in at Democratic headquarters by Nixon's reelection committee.
- Broader illegal activities: wiretaps, "plumbers" unit, enemies list.
- Nixon involved in cover-up → Senate investigation, televised hearings.
- **Oval Office tapes** → Nixon resigned under threat of impeachment.
- **25th Amendment (ratified 1967) in action:** VP **Spiro Agnew** resigned separately (1973, over unrelated tax-evasion/bribery charges); Nixon nominated **Gerald Ford** as VP under the amendment's vacancy provision. When Nixon then resigned, Ford became the only U.S. president never elected to either the presidency or vice presidency.
- **Gerald Ford** became president; pardoned Nixon (citing the need for the country to move past the scandal) → lost significant public goodwill and likely cost him the 1976 election.

### Burger Court
- Nixon appointed **Warren Burger** as Chief Justice; more conservative (but not as conservative as hoped).
- **Roe v. Wade (1973):** Struck down state laws prohibiting abortion — woman's right to privacy.
- Ordered Nixon to turn over tapes (*United States v. Nixon*, 1974).

### Election of 1976
- Jimmy Carter (outsider; former Georgia governor) won due to post-Watergate distrust; 97% of Black vote.

### Carter's Presidency
- Ended "imperial presidency" with informal style.
- Iranian hostage crisis and economic problems → 23% approval rating by 1980.

## 8.15 — Continuity and Change in Period 8

### What Changed
- Legal segregation was dismantled (*Brown v. Board*, Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act), and formerly excluded groups (African Americans, women, Latino and Native Americans, gay Americans) won major legal gains — but the movements also diversified in tactics, from MLK's nonviolence to Black Power, Chicano, AIM, and gay-rights activism, each borrowing and adapting the civil rights template.
- U.S. foreign policy shifted from confrontational containment (Truman/Eisenhower/Kennedy) toward negotiated détente (Nixon/Ford) and back toward confrontation again by 1980 (Carter Doctrine, Afghanistan) — showing containment's basic logic never fully disappeared even during détente.
- The federal government's role expanded dramatically through the Great Society (Medicare, Medicaid, education funding, OEO programs) — the high-water mark of New Deal-style liberalism — but by the mid-1970s a resurgent conservative movement (Nixon's Southern Strategy, Schlafly's STOP ERA, opposition to busing and Great Society spending) began pushing back.
- Trust in government declined sharply after Vietnam (credibility gap, Pentagon Papers) and Watergate, ending the postwar consensus of the Truman–Eisenhower years and contributing to record-low voter turnout and political cynicism by the late 1970s.
- Women's and family roles changed substantially: rising workforce participation, the birth control pill, no-fault divorce laws spreading in the 1970s, and Title IX — alongside a growing conservative backlash defending "traditional" gender roles.

### What Continued
- Cold War containment remained the guiding framework for U.S. foreign policy throughout the period, regardless of party in power — even Nixon's détente and Carter's human rights diplomacy operated within an anti-Soviet framework.
- Racial and economic inequality persisted despite legal victories — de facto segregation (especially in Northern cities), urban poverty, White flight to suburbs, and unequal enforcement of new laws continued; the Kerner Commission's warning of "two societies" captured this gap between legal change and lived reality.
- Debates over the proper size and role of the federal government (New Deal/Great Society liberalism vs. small-government conservatism) carried forward from the 1930s through this period and directly set up Reagan-era politics in Period 9.
- Suburbanization, begun under Truman/Eisenhower (Levittown, interstate highways), continued and intensified, reinforcing racial and economic segregation between cities and suburbs even as legal segregation ended.

### Looking Ahead to Period 9
- The conservative coalition Nixon built (Southern Strategy, silent majority) and the backlash movements of the 1970s (anti-ERA, anti-busing, evangelical political mobilization) provided the electoral base for Reagan's 1980 landslide.
- Stagflation and the energy crisis of the 1970s discredited Keynesian liberal economics in the eyes of many voters, opening the door to the supply-side conservatism of the 1980s.
- The end of détente after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979) set up a renewed, more confrontational Cold War under Reagan.
