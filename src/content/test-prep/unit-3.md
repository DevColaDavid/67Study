# Unit 3: Craft and Structure

## What This Domain Tests

> [!note] Key Idea
> "Craft and Structure" questions test how a passage is *built*, not just what it says: word choice in context, why the author organized things a certain way, and how two related passages connect or disagree. For raw vocabulary drilling, use the interactive **Vocab** practice tool in the Language section — this unit covers the *strategy* around words and structure.

---

## Words in Context

The current SAT and SSAT don't test isolated word-definition questions like older versions did — but strong vocabulary still speeds up reading and directly powers "words in context" questions.

1. Cover the answer choices.
2. Reread the sentence and predict your own word.
3. Match your prediction to the closest choice.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> Common words are often used in *uncommon* ways on these tests (e.g., "arresting" meaning "striking/captivating," not "detaining"). Never pick the most familiar definition without checking it fits the sentence.

**Common meaning traps:**

| Word | Common meaning trap | Tested meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Arresting | Detaining someone | Striking, attention-grabbing |
| Novel (adj.) | Book | New, original |
| Sound (adj.) | Noise | Solid, well-founded ("a sound argument") |
| Table (verb) | Furniture | To postpone/set aside |
| Qualified | Certified | Limited, with reservations ("qualified support") |
| Plastic | Material | Easily shaped, adaptable |
| Volatile | Explosive | Unpredictable, quick to change |
| Austere | Strict | Plain, without decoration |
| Candid | Honest | Unposed, spontaneous (also honest) |
| Terse | Rude | Brief, using few words |

> [!warning] Common Mistake
> Picking the first definition you know instead of the one that fits *this* sentence. If your choice makes the sentence read strangely, it's the wrong sense of the word.

### Word Roots Worth Knowing

Recognizing roots lets you guess unfamiliar words on the fly.

| Root | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| bene- | good | benevolent, benefit |
| mal- | bad | malevolent, malfunction |
| omni- | all | omniscient, omnipresent |
| -ology | study of | biology, psychology |
| pre- | before | precede, premonition |
| post- | after | postpone, posterity |
| in-/im- | not | incoherent, impossible |
| trans- | across | transport, transform |
| dict- | to speak | predict, verdict |
| vid-/vis- | to see | visible, evident |

> [!example] Worked Example
> "Malevolent" breaks down to *mal-* (bad) + *-vol-* (will/wish) → someone wishing bad things on others.

## Text Structure & Purpose

These questions ask *why* the author included a sentence, paragraph, or example — not what it means literally.

| Sentence's job | Signals |
|---|---|
| Introduce a claim | Topic sentence, often first in a paragraph |
| Support with evidence | "for example," "in fact," specific data or a quote |
| Complicate/qualify the claim | "however," "although," "some argue" |
| Conclude / synthesize | "therefore," "ultimately," last sentence of a passage |

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> Ask "what job is this sentence doing for the argument?" rather than "what is this sentence about?" A sentence about the same topic can still serve a different structural role (evidence vs. counterargument).

> [!example] Worked Example
> A passage argues city trees reduce summer temperatures, then adds: "Some researchers note the effect is smaller in high-wind areas."
>
> That sentence's *purpose* is to qualify/limit the claim — not to disprove it. A question asking "why does the author include this sentence" should point to a nuance/limitation, not a rebuttal.

## Cross-Text Connections

Some questions give you two short passages on a related topic and ask how they relate — do they agree, disagree, or address different aspects of the same issue?

> [!important] Compare Claims, Not Topics
> Two passages can be about the exact same subject and still not "agree." Identify each author's specific claim or conclusion, then compare *those* — not just the general topic.

> [!example] Worked Example
> Passage 1: "The new policy will likely reduce costs." Passage 2: "Similar policies elsewhere raised costs due to unforeseen compliance expenses."
>
> These passages **disagree** on the likely outcome, even though both discuss the same policy. A question might ask which choice describes Passage 2's relationship to Passage 1 — the answer should reflect this contradiction, not just "they're both about the policy."

## SSAT Synonym & Analogy Strategy

> [!important] Synonyms
> Before looking at choices, define the word yourself in one word. Then find the closest match — don't get pulled in by a related-but-wrong-degree word (e.g., "annoyed" is not the same as "furious").

> [!important] Analogies
> Build a specific sentence connecting the first pair of words, then apply that exact sentence structure to each answer choice. "Puppy is to dog as ___ is to ___" → "a young version of."

> [!example] Worked Example: Analogy
> HAPPY : ECSTATIC :: annoyed : ?
>
> Relationship: second word is an *extreme degree* of the first. "Annoyed" → extreme version is "furious" or "irate."

## Building Vocabulary Long-Term

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> Don't cram hundreds of flashcards the week before. Use the **Vocab** practice tool a little every day, and read a bit of challenging material (news articles, classic novels) over months — vocabulary retention from context beats memorized lists that fade in days.
