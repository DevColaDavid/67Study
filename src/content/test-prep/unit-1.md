# Unit 1: Test Overview & Strategy

## Which Test Do You Actually Need?

> [!note] Key Idea
> SAT, ACT, and SSAT test overlapping skills but differ in format, timing, and what colleges/schools expect. Pick based on your target schools and your own strengths.

| | SAT | ACT | SSAT (Upper Level) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Used for | College admissions | College admissions | Private high school admissions |
| Sections | Reading, Writing & Language, Math (no calc + calc) | English, Math, Reading, Science (+ optional Essay) | Verbal, Quantitative (x2), Reading |
| Science section | No | Yes (reasoning, not memorized facts) | No |
| Calculator | Some Math, some not | All of Math | Quantitative sections |
| Total time | ~2h 14m | ~2h 55m (with essay) | ~3h |
| Scoring | 400–1600 | 1–36 | Scaled + percentile |
| Guessing penalty | None | None | Yes — small penalty for wrong answers |

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> SAT and ACT scores are treated equally by virtually every college. Take a full-length practice test of each and go with whichever score is relatively higher — that's the one you should train for.

> [!warning] SSAT Guessing Penalty
> Unlike SAT/ACT, wrong SSAT answers cost you a fraction of a point. Skipping a question you can't narrow down is better than a blind guess on 5 options — but if you can eliminate even one choice, guessing is still worth it statistically.

---

## How the Tests Are Scored

- **SAT**: Two section scores (Reading & Writing, Math), each 200–800, summed to 400–1600
- **ACT**: Four section scores (1–36) averaged into a composite; Science and Reading count fully even though no algebra is "needed" for them
- **SSAT**: Raw score (right answers minus a fraction of wrong ones) converted to a scaled score, then compared to a percentile among your grade/gender cohort

> [!example] Why Percentile Matters More Than Raw Score (SSAT)
> A raw score that puts you in the 85th percentile means you scored higher than 85% of your peer group nationally — schools care about this number more than the raw scaled score itself.

## Building a Study Plan

1. **Take a full diagnostic test first.** Don't study blind — figure out which section is actually costing you points.
2. **Target your weakest section, not your favorite.** Ten more points in your worst section is easier than ten more in your best.
3. **Timed practice beats untimed practice** once you understand the content — the clock is often the real opponent, not the material.
4. **Review every wrong answer** and categorize *why* you missed it: content gap, careless error, or ran out of time. Each has a different fix.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> Careless errors and timing errors are fixed by more timed practice under real conditions (see the Pacing & Test-Day Tactics unit). Content gaps are fixed by going back to the Language or Math units below. Don't treat all misses the same way.

## General Test-Taking Strategy

> [!important] Process of Elimination First
> On any multiple-choice question you're unsure of, physically cross out answers you know are wrong before picking from what's left. Removing 1–2 choices turns a guess into much better odds.

- **Answer every question** on the SAT and ACT — there's no penalty for wrong answers, so a blind guess beats a blank.
- **Don't get stuck.** If a question is taking too long, mark it, move on, and come back if time allows.
- **Read the question before the passage** on Reading sections when you're short on time — it tells you what to look for.
- **Bubble/enter in batches** (paper tests) rather than after every single question, to save time — but always leave a couple minutes at the end to double check alignment.

> [!example] Worked Example: Time Budgeting
> SAT Reading & Writing: 54 questions in ~64 minutes (2 modules) → roughly 1.2 min/question. If you're spending 3 minutes on one question, you're borrowing time from three others. Flag it and move.
