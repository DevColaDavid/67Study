# Unit 2: Information and Ideas

## What This Domain Tests

> [!note] Key Idea
> "Information and Ideas" questions test whether you can find, summarize, and reason about what a passage actually says — central ideas, key details, and how evidence (text or data) supports a claim. Nothing here requires outside knowledge; every answer must be provable using only what's in front of you.

- SAT: short passages (~25–150 words), one question each, across literature, history, social science, and science
- ACT: longer passages (~750 words), 10 questions each, across the same four domains, all under one strict timer
- SSAT: shorter passages, ~5–8 questions each, covering fiction, poetry, and nonfiction

---

## Central Ideas & Details

| Type | What it asks | How to approach |
|---|---|---|
| Main idea / purpose | What's the passage's overall point? | Summarize in your own words *before* looking at choices |
| Detail | What does the text explicitly say? | Find the exact line, don't rely on memory |
| Inference | What's implied but not stated? | Must be *directly supported*, not just plausible |

> [!warning] Common Mistake
> Picking the answer that's *true in real life* instead of the answer that's *supported by this specific passage*. The test only cares about what's on the page.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> For inference questions, treat the correct answer as "almost restating the text" rather than "a clever leap." If an inference requires three logical steps, it's probably wrong.

---

## Command of Evidence — Textual

Some questions give you a claim, then ask which quotation from the passage (or which answer choice) best supports it.

> [!important] Answer the Content Question First
> Don't hunt for evidence blind. First decide what you believe the answer *is* based on the passage, then find the quote/choice that backs up that specific claim — not just any quote that's topically related.

> [!example] Worked Example
> Claim: "The author suggests the experiment's results were inconclusive."
>
> A supporting quote must literally express doubt or incompleteness — e.g., "the sample size was too small to draw firm conclusions" — not just a sentence that mentions the experiment.

---

## Command of Evidence — Quantitative (Graphs & Tables)

Some passages pair with a chart, graph, or table. These questions test whether you can read data accurately and match it to a claim in the text.

- **Read axis labels and units first** — before looking at the trend, know what's actually being measured.
- **Check whether the claim matches the data's direction** (increasing, decreasing, no correlation) — don't assume a graph confirms the text just because it's nearby.
- **Watch for scale tricks**: a graph starting at a non-zero baseline can visually exaggerate a small change.

> [!warning] Common Mistake
> Picking an answer that sounds plausible but contradicts what the actual numbers show. Always check the data before trusting the answer choice's description of it.

> [!example] Worked Example
> A bar graph shows Group A at 45% and Group B at 30%. An answer choice claims "Group B outperformed Group A" — that's false regardless of how reasonable it sounds; the data says otherwise.

---

## Reading the Passage Efficiently

- **Skim actively, don't read passively.** Track who the characters/subjects are, what changes, and what the author's attitude seems to be.
- **Annotate lightly**: underline claims, circle contrast words (*but, however, although, yet*) — these mark where an argument turns.
- **Don't memorize details.** Note *where* something was discussed (paragraph 2, near the end) so you can look it up fast.

> [!example] Worked Example: Contrast Words
> "Many scientists believed the theory was sound. Dr. Chen, however, argued the data was incomplete."
>
> The word "however" signals the sentence is about to disagree with the previous claim — a question asking about Dr. Chen's view should point to skepticism, not agreement.

---

## Pacing on Reading Sections

> [!important] Don't Reread Everything
> Going back to reread the whole passage for every question destroys your pace. Instead, go back to the *specific area* the question points to (a line number, a paragraph) and reread just that.

- SAT: ~1 passage/question pair per ~1.25 minutes (short passages, 1 Q each)
- ACT: budget ~8–9 minutes per passage including all 10 questions — don't spend it all reading
- SSAT: shorter passages but still budget by the clock, not by "finishing the passage"

> [!example] Worked Example: ACT Reading Pacing
> 4 passages, 35 minutes total → ~8.75 min/passage. Spend ~3 minutes reading/skimming, ~5.5 minutes on the 10 questions. If you're at minute 9 on passage 1, you need to speed up immediately, not "make it up later."
