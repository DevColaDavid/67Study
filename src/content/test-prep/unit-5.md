# Unit 5: Standard English Conventions

## What This Domain Tests

> [!note] Key Idea
> Standard English Conventions questions test grammar rules, punctuation, and sentence structure directly — not creative writing. Most questions have one objectively correct answer, unlike the more judgment-based Expression of Ideas questions.

---

## Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb must match its subject in number, even when other nouns sit between them.

> [!example] Worked Example
> "The **box** of old photographs **was** (not *were*) found in the attic." — the subject is "box" (singular), not "photographs."

---

## Pronoun Agreement & Clarity

A pronoun must clearly refer to one specific noun and match it in number.

- "Each student must bring **their** own laptop." → ambiguous/informal on some tests; watch for "his or her" vs "their" depending on the test's style guide
- Vague pronoun: "The report criticized the policy, which upset the committee." — what does "which" refer to? Unclear.

> [!warning] Common Mistake
> Assuming a pronoun is fine just because a nearby noun matches in number. If more than one noun could be the antecedent, the sentence is ambiguous and needs to be rewritten.

---

## Comma Rules

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Commas set off nonessential info | "My brother, who lives in Texas, called." |
| No comma before essential clauses | "The book that I borrowed is overdue." |
| Comma before FANBOYS joining two independent clauses | "I studied hard, but the test was still hard." |
| No comma splice — don't join two full sentences with just a comma | Wrong: "I studied hard, the test was still hard." |

> [!warning] Common Mistake
> The comma splice is one of the most frequently tested errors: joining two complete sentences with only a comma. Fix with a period, semicolon, or conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so).

---

## Semicolons and Colons

- **Semicolon**: joins two independent clauses (each side must be a complete sentence)
- **Colon**: introduces a list, explanation, or example — must follow a complete sentence

> [!example] Worked Example
> Correct: "I have one goal; I will not give up." (both sides complete)
> Correct: "I have one goal: to finish this test." (only first side needs to be complete)

---

## Apostrophes

- **its** = possessive ("the dog wagged its tail")
- **it's** = "it is" ("it's raining")
- Plural ≠ possessive: "the students' notebooks" (belonging to students) vs "the students" (just plural)

> [!warning] Common Mistake
> Confusing "its" and "it's" is one of the most common errors even among strong writers — the apostrophe version is *always* a contraction of "it is" or "it has," never possessive.

---

## Parallel Structure

Items in a list or comparison must share the same grammatical form.

> [!example] Worked Example
> Wrong: "She likes running, to swim, and biking."
> Right: "She likes running, swimming, and biking." (all gerunds — matching form)

---

## Modifier Placement

A modifying phrase should sit next to the thing it describes — a "dangling modifier" attaches to the wrong noun.

> [!example] Worked Example
> Wrong: "Walking to school, the rain started falling." (implies the rain was walking)
> Right: "Walking to school, I got caught in the rain."

---

## Strategy for This Section

1. Read a full sentence (not just the underlined part) before answering — grammar errors often depend on context outside the underline.
2. When in doubt between two similar choices, pick the shorter, simpler one.
3. Try reading each option in place of the underlined portion out loud (in your head) — errors are often easier to hear than to see.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> "NO CHANGE" is correct roughly as often as any other option — don't avoid it out of suspicion.
