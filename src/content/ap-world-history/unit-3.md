# Unit 3 — Land-Based Empires (c. 1450 to c. 1750)

Between 1450 and 1750, large multiethnic land empires — the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals ("Gunpowder Empires"), along with Qing China and Romanov Russia — expanded using gunpowder weaponry, then had to solve the same three problems: how to **administer** vast, diverse populations, how to **legitimize** the ruler's authority, and how to **consolidate** power away from rival elites. **Legitimizing power** means winning acceptance of your authority; **consolidating power** means taking power away from others who might threaten it. Rulers leaned on bureaucracy, religion, art and monumental architecture, and tax systems to do both. The Ottoman–Safavid rivalry shows how these empires' politics were entangled with the deepening Sunni–Shi'a split within Islam, while Europe's Protestant Reformation and South Asia's new Sikh faith show religious change happening on parallel tracks elsewhere in the world during the same era.

---

## 3.1 — Empires Expand

Gunpowder weapons — cannons, muskets, and the armies built around them — were the single biggest driver of territorial expansion for land-based empires in this era. Bigger armies with better firearms could break down city walls, defeat mounted nomadic warriors, and hold huge new territories together, but gunpowder access cut both ways: empires that fell behind in gunpowder technology could be conquered just as fast as they had once conquered others.

### The Ottoman Empire
- Used **gunpowder weapons and massive siege cannons** to expand across Anatolia, Southeastern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa.
- **1453**: Sultan **Mehmed II** used enormous cannons (built by the Hungarian engineer Orban) to breach the ancient walls of **Constantinople**, ending the **Byzantine Empire** after over 1,000 years. He renamed the city **Istanbul** and made it the Ottoman capital.
- Continued expansion under later sultans brought the empire to its height around the Mediterranean, controlling trade routes linking Europe, Asia, and Africa.
- Built a loyal, professional military and bureaucracy through the **Devshirme System** (see 3.2), which produced the elite **Janissary** infantry corps.

### The Safavid Empire
- Founded in **1501** by **Shah Ismail**, who united Persia under the **Safavid Sufi religious order** and its **qizilbash** ("red-head," for their distinctive red caps) Turkic cavalry.
- Expanded and defended Persia and parts of Central Asia using **gunpowder weapons** alongside qizilbash cavalry.
- **Shah Abbas** (r. 1588–1629) modernized the Safavid military with **gunpowder artillery and muskets** and created a **new slave-soldier corps (ghulams)** — enslaved Christians levied from the **Caucasus** (Georgians, Armenians, Circassians), modeled directly on the Ottoman Janissary system — to reduce reliance on the less-controllable qizilbash tribal forces.
- Moved the capital to **Isfahan**, which became a showcase of Safavid wealth, architecture, and Silk Road trade (contemporaries called it "Isfahan is half the world").

### The Mughal Empire
- **Babur**, a Central Asian prince descended from both Timur and Genghis Khan, invaded South Asia and defeated the much larger army of the **Delhi Sultanate's Ibrahim Lodi** at the **First Battle of Panipat (1526)**, using cannons and matchlock muskets to overcome superior numbers → founded the Mughal Empire.
- **Emperor Akbar** (r. 1556–1605), Babur's grandson, greatly expanded and consolidated Mughal territory across South Asia and legitimized his rule over the Hindu majority through **religious tolerance** — including abolishing the **jizya** (poll tax on non-Muslims) and marrying into Hindu Rajput royal families (see 3.3).

### The Qing (Manchu) Dynasty
- The Manchus, a semi-nomadic people from Manchuria (northeast of China), organized their society and military into the **Banner System** and used it to overthrow the collapsing Ming Dynasty, establishing the **Qing Dynasty in 1644**.
- Expanded Qing control over China proper, Mongolia, Tibet, and Central Asia through the 17th and 18th centuries, becoming one of the largest land empires in world history and a major challenge for the small non-Han Manchu minority to rule over an overwhelmingly Han Chinese population (see 3.2–3.3).

### Songhai Empire and the Limits of Gunpowder
- Rising from the decline of the Mali Empire, the **Songhai Empire** dominated the trans-Saharan gold and salt trade under rulers like **Sunni Ali** and, later, **Askia Muhammad** (r. 1493–1528), who expanded and professionalized the empire's rule (see 3.2).
- Songhai illustrates the flip side of the "gunpowder empires" story: in **1591**, an invading Moroccan army — though far smaller — defeated Songhai's much larger traditional force at the **Battle of Tondibi** using **matchlock muskets and cannons**, collapsing the empire almost overnight.
- **Takeaway:** access to gunpowder technology, not just the size of an empire, determined who conquered and who was conquered in this era.

### Rivalries Between Empires
- **Ottoman–Safavid Rivalry**: Ottomans were **Sunni**; Safavids were **Shi'a**. The Sunni–Shi'a divide originated centuries earlier as a political dispute over who should rightfully lead the Muslim community after Muhammad's death, but by this era both empires genuinely believed they were the true representatives of Islam — making their border wars both territorial and religious.
- **Safavid–Mughal Conflict**: Fought over contested lands in modern **Afghanistan** (notably the city of Kandahar), with the same Shi'a (Safavid) vs. Sunni (Mughal) religious overlay adding to the territorial dispute.

> **Big picture:** Every empire in this unit expanded first through military force built around gunpowder — but conquest was only step one. Holding onto that territory required the administrative and religious strategies covered in 3.2 and 3.3.

## 3.2 — Empires: Administration

Once an empire finished conquering territory, its rulers faced a harder problem: how to govern huge, ethnically and religiously diverse populations without the whole thing falling apart. Every empire in this unit reached for some combination of a loyal bureaucracy, a professional standing military separated from local elites, and taxation systems that extracted revenue without provoking constant revolt.

> [!note] Critical Definitions
> - **Legitimizing power**: methods rulers use to *establish* and win acceptance of their authority.
> - **Consolidating power**: methods rulers use to *transfer* power away from rival groups (nobles, religious authorities, local elites) and concentrate it in their own hands.

### Ottoman Empire: The Devshirme System and the Millet System
- **Devshirme System**: Ottoman officials periodically levied Christian boys (mostly from Balkan provinces such as Serbia, Bosnia, and Albania), enslaved and forcibly converted them to Islam, and educated them in palace schools in Istanbul.
  - The most talented were funneled into elite **bureaucratic and administrative positions** (some rose as high as Grand Vizier).
  - The rest became **Janissaries** — an elite, salaried infantry corps personally loyal to the sultan.
  - Because these men were taken from their families as children and owed everything to the sultan, they had **no independent noble family power base** to threaten him — a deliberate strategy for both administering the empire and consolidating the sultan's power against a hereditary aristocracy.
- **Millet System**: Non-Muslim religious communities (Orthodox Christians, Armenian Christians, Jews) were organized into self-governing **millets**, each allowed to run its own courts, schools, and religious affairs under its own leaders — in exchange for political loyalty and the **jizya** (a poll tax on non-Muslim subjects). This bought the loyalty of conquered populations without forcing mass conversion.
- **Tax farming**: The state granted private individuals the right to collect taxes in a given region, letting them keep a cut — an efficient (if often corrupt) way to extract revenue across a huge, hard-to-administer territory.

### Safavid Empire: Land Grants and a New Slave Corps
- Distributed **land grants (in exchange for military service)** to qizilbash tribal leaders, similar to feudal arrangements — but this left the shahs dependent on tribal loyalty they didn't fully control.
- Shah Abbas's creation of the **ghulam** slave-soldier and slave-bureaucrat corps (see 3.1) mirrored the Ottoman devshirme strategy: officials with no independent family power base, loyal only to the shah, used to counterbalance the qizilbash nobility.

### Mughal Empire: Mansabdars and Zamindars
- **Mansabdari System**: Imperial officials (mansabdars, many of them military officers) were ranked in a numbered hierarchy and paid according to rank, in exchange for supplying a set number of cavalry to the emperor — a system for administering both the bureaucracy and the military at once.
- **Zamindars**: Local landowning elites (Hindu and Muslim) collected taxes from the peasantry on the empire's behalf and passed a share up to the central government — letting the Mughals govern through existing local power structures rather than replacing them outright.

### Qing Dynasty: The Banner System and the Examination System
- **Banner System**: Organized Manchu (and later some Mongol and Han) military households into color-coded "banners" that were simultaneously military units and administrative/social units — the backbone of Qing control, especially in the early conquest period.
- Kept China's centuries-old **Confucian civil service examination system**, through which Han scholars competed for government office by mastering the Confucian classics — preserving a bureaucracy Han elites recognized as legitimate rather than imposing an entirely foreign administrative structure.
- Required Han men to adopt the Manchu **queue hairstyle** (shaved forehead, braided tail) as a visible, enforced sign of submission to Qing rule.

### Russia: Centralizing Under the Tsars
- **Ivan IV ("Ivan the Terrible")** was crowned the first **Tsar** of Russia in 1547 and worked to break the power of the **boyars** (Russia's hereditary landed nobility), replacing their independent influence with a new service nobility that received land in exchange for loyal service to the crown — the same "loyalty over inherited power" logic seen in the Ottoman and Safavid systems.
- Expanded Russian territory east and south, capturing the Khanates of **Kazan (1552)** and **Astrakhan (1556)** along the Volga River.
- Over this period, the state increasingly tied peasants to the land as **serfs**, formalizing a labor system that gave the nobility (and through them, the tsar) a stable tax base and source of military manpower.

### Songhai Empire: Provincial Administration
- **Askia Muhammad** professionalized Songhai's government by dividing the empire into **provinces run by appointed governors** (rather than leaving power with independent local rulers) and standardizing taxation — a centralizing strategy similar in spirit to the other empires' moves against local elites.

## 3.3 — Empires: Belief Systems

Religion was one of the most powerful legitimizing tools available to rulers in this era — used to justify authority, unify (or divide) diverse populations, and, in Europe and South Asia, to spark entirely new religious movements.

### Religious Legitimacy Across Empires
- **Divine Right of Kings** (Europe): monarchs claimed to rule as God's representatives on Earth — to question the king was to question God himself. Absolutist rulers like France's Louis XIV reinforced this with monumental architecture (the Palace of Versailles) and elaborate court ritual designed to overawe nobles and subjects alike.
- **Ottoman Empire**: the sultan claimed the title of **Caliph**, positioning himself as leader of the global Sunni Muslim community, and sponsored massive mosque-building projects to display religious authority and piety.
- **Safavid Empire**: **Shah Ismail** declared **Twelver Shi'a Islam** the official state religion immediately upon founding the empire in 1501 — a sharp break from the Sunni-majority region around it. Sunni Muslims within Safavid territory were publicly executed, and the Safavid military reportedly cursed the first three Sunni caliphs as illegitimate usurpers. This deliberate, enforced religious identity both unified Safavid subjects and deepened the empire's rivalry with its Sunni neighbors (see 3.1).
- **Mughal Empire**: **Akbar** pursued active religious tolerance toward his Hindu-majority subjects — abolishing the jizya tax, marrying Rajput Hindu princesses, and inviting scholars of many faiths (Muslim, Hindu, Christian, Zoroastrian, Jain) to his court, ultimately synthesizing elements of several traditions into a new court faith, **Din-i-Ilahi** ("Divine Faith"), though it never spread beyond a small circle of nobles. (Later Mughal emperors, notably **Aurangzeb**, reversed this tolerance and reinstated the jizya — a reminder that "legitimizing strategy" could swing sharply between rulers of the same empire.)
- **Qing Dynasty**: Manchu emperors invoked the Chinese **Mandate of Heaven** — the traditional belief that heaven grants a just ruler the right to govern — and had themselves portrayed in official court paintings holding **Confucian texts** and performing Confucian rites, deliberately appealing to Han Chinese elites despite being ethnic outsiders.
- **Russia**: the Tsars worked closely with the **Russian Orthodox Church**, which promoted Moscow as the "**Third Rome**" — successor to Constantinople (fallen to the Ottomans in 1453, see 3.1) as the true center of Orthodox Christianity — giving Russian rulers a claim to sacred as well as political authority.

### The Protestant Reformation
- **Catholic corruption** fueled growing criticism across Europe: **simony** (buying and selling Church offices) and the sale of **indulgences** (paying money to reduce punishment for sin).
- **Martin Luther**, a German monk, concluded these practices had no basis in the Bible and published his **95 Theses** (1517) criticizing them.
- The recently invented **printing press** allowed Luther's ideas to spread across Europe far faster than any earlier religious dissent could have — turning a local theological dispute into a continent-wide movement and permanently splitting Western Christianity into Catholic and Protestant churches.
- The Catholic Church responded with its own internal reforms — the **Catholic (Counter-) Reformation**, centered on the **Council of Trent**, which addressed corruption while reaffirming core Catholic doctrine.
- Together, the two movements represent **change and continuity**: Christianity fractured institutionally but continued to grow and spread globally through this period.

### Sikhism — A New Syncretic Religion
- Founded by **Guru Nanak** in the Punjab region of South Asia in the late 15th century, blending elements of the two major faiths around it:
  - From **Islam**: strict belief in **one God**.
  - From **Hinduism**: the concept of a **cycle of reincarnation**.
- Distinctly its own: Sikhism explicitly **rejected the caste system** and **gender hierarchies**, teaching radical equality among believers — a sharper break from existing social hierarchy than either parent tradition.

## 3.4 — Comparison in Land-Based Empires

Every empire in this unit solved the same three problems — how to **expand**, how to **administer**, and how to **legitimize** rule — but reached different answers depending on local geography, religion, and existing social structures. Comparing these solutions side by side is one of the most heavily tested skills on the AP exam for this unit.

### Methods of Expansion

| Empire | Core Expansion Method | Key Turning Point |
|---|---|---|
| **Ottoman** | Gunpowder siege cannons + professional Janissary infantry | Conquest of Constantinople, 1453 |
| **Safavid** | Qizilbash cavalry + gunpowder artillery/muskets | Founding under Shah Ismail, 1501 |
| **Mughal** | Gunpowder artillery + matchlock muskets vs. larger armies | First Battle of Panipat, 1526 |
| **Qing (Manchu)** | Banner-system military organization | Conquest of Ming China, 1644 |
| **Russia** | Firearms + Cossack forces against steppe khanates | Capture of Kazan (1552) and Astrakhan (1556) |
| **Songhai** | Cavalry-based conquest of trans-Saharan trade routes — until it was defeated by superior Moroccan gunpowder forces | Battle of Tondibi, 1591 (Songhai's collapse) |

### Administrative Systems

| Empire | How It Built a Loyal Bureaucracy/Military | How It Governed Local Populations |
|---|---|---|
| **Ottoman** | Devshirme — enslaved, converted Christian boys with no independent power base | Millet system: self-governing non-Muslim religious communities + jizya tax |
| **Safavid** | Ghulam slave-soldier corps (Caucasus captives), modeled on the Janissaries | Land grants to qizilbash tribal leaders |
| **Mughal** | Mansabdari ranked-official system tied to military service | Zamindars (local elites) collected taxes and governed locally |
| **Qing** | Banner system (Manchu military-social units) | Kept the Confucian civil service exam; forced Han submission via the queue order |
| **Russia** | Service nobility granted land for loyalty, replacing independent boyar power | Serfdom tied peasants to noble estates, funding the state |
| **Songhai** | Appointed provincial governors under Askia Muhammad | Standardized taxation across provinces |

### Religious / Legitimation Strategies

| Empire | Legitimizing Strategy |
|---|---|
| **Ottoman** | Sultan as Caliph of Sunni Islam; monumental mosque-building |
| **Safavid** | Enforced Twelver Shi'a Islam as state religion; sharp break from Sunni neighbors |
| **Mughal** | Akbar: religious tolerance + Din-i-Ilahi synthesis (later reversed under Aurangzeb) |
| **Qing** | Mandate of Heaven + Confucian imagery aimed at the Han majority |
| **Russia** | Orthodox Church backing; Moscow as the "Third Rome" |
| **European monarchies** | Divine Right of Kings; palace architecture (e.g., Versailles) |

> **Synthesis:** Notice the recurring pattern — nearly every empire built a class of administrators or soldiers who owed their position entirely to the ruler (devshirme recruits, ghulams, mansabdars, banner troops, service nobility) rather than to inherited family status, because loyalty without independent power was the safest kind of loyalty. At the same time, every empire had to make its rule feel legitimate to a population that didn't necessarily share the ruler's own faith or ethnicity — whether through religious tolerance (Akbar), enforced religious identity (Safavid Shi'ism), appeals to a *different* culture's own traditions (Qing Confucianism), or claims of divine sanction (Ottoman caliphate, European divine right, Russian Orthodoxy). The empires that balanced expansion, administration, and legitimacy most successfully lasted centuries; Songhai's collapse in 1591 is the unit's clearest reminder that falling behind on gunpowder technology could unravel even a well-administered empire almost overnight.
