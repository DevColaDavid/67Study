# Unit 1: The Global Tapestry (c. 1200 to c. 1450)

Unit 1 surveys how six world regions — East Asia, Dar al-Islam, South/Southeast Asia, the Americas, Africa, and Europe — organized states and legitimized rulers on the eve of the Mongol era and sustained transoceanic contact. No region developed in isolation: the Song used a tribute system to spread Confucian culture, Dar al-Islam's trade and scholarly networks linked North Africa to South Asia, and Sufi missionaries carried Islam along the same routes merchants used. Across all six regions, rulers reached for the same basic toolkit — **religion, bureaucracy, military force, and control of trade** — but combined them differently: the Song built a **centralized** bureaucratic state on Neo-Confucianism and merit exams, the Inca ran an equally **centralized** empire on divine kingship and forced labor, while Aztec tribute-taking, African city-states, and European feudalism all left more power in local or regional hands. Comparing these different paths to authority is the throughline of this unit and the explicit subject of Topic 1.7.

## 1.1 — Developments in East Asia

### The Song Dynasty (960–1279)
- The **Song Dynasty** reunified China after a period of fragmentation and ruled a prosperous, urbanized, and technologically advanced state until the Mongols conquered it in 1279 and established the **Yuan Dynasty**.
- **State legitimacy — Neo-Confucianism**: Song scholars revived classical Confucian ethics and fused them with elements of Buddhist and Daoist metaphysics, producing **Neo-Confucianism** — a philosophy that reinforced hierarchical social relationships (ruler–subject, father–son, husband–wife) as natural and cosmically ordered. Framing this as a *revival* of ancient tradition, rather than a new invention, made it more persuasive as a basis for authority.
- **Civil service examination system**: Bureaucrats were selected through standardized exams testing mastery of the Confucian classics — a practice that originated under the **Han Dynasty**, making the Song's use of it a clear example of **continuity**. In theory the system was meritocratic and open to any man; in practice, wealthy families could better afford the years of tutoring needed to pass, so social mobility through the exams was real but limited.
- **Confucian social order**: Strictly hierarchical — state above subject, elder above younger, men above women. **Filial piety** (children's duty to honor and obey parents and ancestors) was the model for loyalty to the state itself.
- **Women in Song China**: Legally subordinate — could not own property or initiate remarriage. Elite **footbinding** spread during this period as a marker of status and beauty, though it also physically restricted women's mobility and labor.

### Economy and Technology
- **Champa rice**: A fast-maturing, drought-resistant rice strain introduced from Vietnam through the tribute system, enabling **double-cropping** and driving a major population boom.
- **Commercial revolution**: Expansion of the **Grand Canal** linked northern and southern China, integrating markets; China led the world in porcelain, silk, and iron/steel production, and the Song government issued the world's first government-backed **paper money** to handle the resulting volume of trade.
- **Cultural diffusion via the tribute system**: Korea, Japan, and Vietnam all sent tribute missions and, in exchange, absorbed **Confucianism**, **Mahayana Buddhism**, the Chinese writing system, and bureaucratic models — without ever being politically absorbed into China itself.

### Buddhism in East Asia
| Branch | Character | Region |
|---|---|---|
| **Theravada** ("Teaching of the Elders") | Older, more austere tradition; emphasizes individual monastic discipline to reach nirvana | Southeast Asia, Sri Lanka |
| **Mahayana** ("Great Vehicle") | More flexible/popular; **bodhisattvas** (enlightened beings) delay their own nirvana to help others reach it | East Asia (China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam) |

- **Four Noble Truths**: life is suffering; suffering is caused by desire/attachment; ending desire ends suffering; the **Eightfold Path** is the way to end desire.
- **Goal**: escape the cycle of reincarnation and reach **nirvana**.

> **Big Picture:** The Song never conquered its neighbors, but Confucianism, Buddhism, and Chinese bureaucratic models spread across East Asia anyway — proof that cultural influence and tribute diplomacy could project power just as effectively as armies.

## 1.2 — Developments in Dar al-Islam

### The Fragmentation of the Abbasid Caliphate
- The **Abbasid Caliphate** (750–1258) had already been losing real political control to regional governors for over a century before it formally ended — the Mongols sacked **Baghdad in 1258**, killed the last Abbasid caliph, and destroyed the city's canal system and libraries.
- Political fragmentation did **not** mean cultural or religious collapse: a shared Arabic scholarly language, Islamic law, and long-distance trade networks kept **Dar al-Islam** ("the House of Islam") culturally unified even as it split into competing states.

### Successor States
| State | Base | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Seljuk Empire** | Anatolia, Persia, Central Asia | Turkic dynasty; seized power from the weakened Abbasids while nominally recognizing the caliph's religious authority |
| **Mamluk Sultanate** | Egypt, Levant | Ruled by former enslaved soldier-elites (*mamluks*); defeated the Mongols at the **Battle of Ain Jalut (1260)** — halting the Mongol advance into Africa and the Islamic heartland |
| **Delhi Sultanate** | Northern South Asia | Turkic Muslim rulers governing a Hindu-majority population; discussed further in Topic 1.3 |

- These successor states were still **Turkic-led military dynasties**, not restorations of Arab Abbasid rule — a key distinction the AP exam tests directly.

### Intellectual Life and the Spread of Islam
- The **House of Wisdom** in Baghdad had been a center of translation and scholarship, preserving and expanding on Greek, Persian, and Indian works in mathematics, medicine, and astronomy; this knowledge later flowed into Europe and helped fuel the **Renaissance**.
- **Nasir al-Din al-Tusi**, a Persian astronomer working after the Mongol conquest, made major advances in **trigonometry** and planetary models.
- Islam spread through three main channels: **merchants** carrying the faith along trade routes, **missionaries**, and **Sufis** — mystics who emphasized a personal, emotional connection to God and tolerated blending Islam with local customs, making conversion far more appealing to new populations in Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia.

> **Big Picture:** Political fragmentation actually helped Islam spread further — with no single caliph to answer to, Sufi missionaries and merchant communities carried the faith along trade networks independent of any one state's power.

## 1.3 — Developments in South and Southeast Asia

### South Asia: Competing Faiths
- **Hinduism** remained dominant, organized around the **caste system** — but the **Bhakti movement**, a devotional practice emphasizing a direct, personal relationship with a chosen deity, gained popularity because it offered a path to religious meaning that cut across caste and gender lines.
- **Buddhism**, once widespread, had declined to isolated monastic communities in its South Asian birthplace by c. 1200 — losing ground to a resurgent Hinduism and to the destruction of monasteries during Muslim conquests further north.
- The **Delhi Sultanate** brought Turkic Muslim rule to northern India; Islam became primarily the religion of the ruling elite, while the Hindu majority largely retained its own faith — producing centuries of religious coexistence and occasional tension side by side.
- Hindu states such as the **Rajput Kingdoms** in northwestern India used religious patronage — temple building, sponsorship of Brahmin priests — to legitimize their resistance to Sultanate expansion.

### Southeast Asia: A Crossroads of Religions
| State | Location | Primary Faith | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Khmer Empire** | Cambodia | Started Hindu, shifted to Buddhist | **Angkor Wat** was built as a Hindu temple, later converted for Buddhist worship — reflecting the transition |
| **Srivijaya** | Sumatra | Buddhist | Earlier maritime trade power, in decline by this period |
| **Majapahit** | Java | Hindu-Buddhist synthesis | Dominant maritime empire of the era; controlled shipping lanes and trade through the Indonesian archipelago |
| **Sukhothai** | Thailand | Theravada Buddhist | Adopted Buddhism as a unifying state religion |

- Southeast Asia sat at the maritime crossroads of the **Indian Ocean trade network**: diasporic merchant communities from India and the Middle East settled in port cities and brought Hinduism, Buddhism, and Islam with them. Local rulers often adopted whichever faith best strengthened trade relationships with these merchant communities, rather than through conquest or top-down conversion.

> **Big Picture:** Unlike Europe or Dar al-Islam, no single faith dominated South or Southeast Asia in this period — Hinduism, Buddhism, and Islam coexisted and competed for royal patronage, often within the same state or even the same monument (Angkor Wat).

## 1.4 — State Building in the Americas

### Two Models of Empire in Mesoamerica and the Andes
| Empire | Founded | Capital | Governance Model | Key Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Aztec (Mexica)** | 1345 | **Tenochtitlan** (island city in Lake Texcoco) | **Decentralized tribute empire** — conquered city-states kept local rulers but owed tribute and labor | Military conquest + **tribute extraction**; ritual warfare and human sacrifice reinforced religious/political authority; **chinampas** (floating raised-bed farms) supported a dense urban population |
| **Inca** | c. 1400 (rapid expansion) | Cuzco | **Highly centralized** — direct administrative control over conquered territory, not just tribute-taking | **Mit'a system** — a rotational labor tax requiring communities to work on roads, terraces, and state projects instead of paying in goods; extensive road network tied the empire together; **quipu** (knotted, colored cords) recorded census and tribute data without a written script; the **Sapa Inca** ruled as a semi-divine figure |

- The Aztec and Inca models represent opposite strategies for the same problem — controlling a large, ethnically diverse population — and are a favorite AP comparison prompt: the Aztec left conquered elites in place and relied on the threat of force to extract tribute, while the Inca rebuilt administration from the top down and physically integrated new territory into a single road-and-labor system.

### North America: The Mississippian Culture
- Centered on the **Mississippi River Valley**; a farming culture supporting large **mound-building** population centers.
- **Cahokia** (near present-day St. Louis) was the largest such center — roughly 80 earthen mounds, some over 100 feet tall, organized around a central plaza; it had gone into decline by the time of sustained European contact, evidence that Native societies rose and fell on their own internal timelines.

## 1.5 — State Building in Africa

### Trade-Based State Building
- **Swahili city-states** (e.g., Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu) grew wealthy from **Indian Ocean trade**, exporting gold, ivory, and enslaved people in exchange for textiles and porcelain; the **Swahili language** — a blend of Bantu grammar and Arabic vocabulary — reflects this coastal East African–Arab trading relationship.
- **Mali, Ghana, and Songhai** built **centralized** empires in West Africa on the strength of the **trans-Saharan trade** in gold and salt, facilitated by ties to Dar al-Islam. **Mansa Musa**, ruler of Mali, made a famous **hajj to Mecca in 1324** that was so lavish with gold that it reportedly depressed gold prices in the regions he passed through — a striking demonstration of Mali's wealth and its integration into the wider Islamic world.
- The **Hausa Kingdoms** were a collection of **decentralized** city-states in the Sahel that competed and cooperated to broker trans-Saharan trade, gradually integrating Islamic law and Muslim scholars into local governance without becoming a single unified state.

### States That Broke the Pattern
- **Great Zimbabwe**: a stone-walled city in southern Africa that grew wealthy controlling regional **gold trade** with the Swahili coast — but its rulers, unlike Mali or the Hausa, **never adopted Islam**, relying instead on indigenous religious and political traditions to legitimize rule.
- **Ethiopia**: a **Christian** kingdom tracing its religious and political legitimacy back to the earlier **Aksumite** kingdom — unique among African states of this period for building state authority on Christianity rather than Islam or indigenous belief systems, and maintaining monastic communities as centers of both education and political power.

> **Big Picture:** Africa in this period shows nearly every governance model in miniature — centralized Islamic empires (Mali), decentralized Islamic city-states (Hausa, Swahili coast), a wealthy trading state that rejected Islam (Great Zimbabwe), and a Christian kingdom (Ethiopia) — making it the region AP most often tests for its internal diversity.

## 1.6 — Developments in Europe

### Political and Religious Landscape
- The **Byzantine Empire** preserved **Orthodox Christianity** and Roman imperial tradition in the east, and had spread Orthodoxy to **Kievan Rus** through the conversion of Prince Vladimir in 988.
- Western Europe had no comparable centralized empire; the **Roman Catholic Church** was the one institution binding together a politically fragmented region — crowning kings, running the era's only major educational institutions, and providing a shared religious identity across rival kingdoms.
- **Muslim rule** persisted in parts of the **Iberian Peninsula** (Al-Andalus) following the 8th-century conquest, creating centuries of periodic coexistence and conflict with Christian kingdoms to the north.
- **Jewish communities** lived throughout Europe as a frequently persecuted religious minority, often restricted in where they could live and what occupations they could hold.

### Feudalism and Manorialism
| System | Type | How It Worked |
|---|---|---|
| **Feudalism** | Political/military | **Lords** granted land (**fiefs**) to **vassals** in exchange for military service and loyalty — a decentralized web of personal obligations rather than a unified state bureaucracy |
| **Manorialism** | Economic | **Serfs** were legally bound to work the land on a lord's manor in exchange for protection; unlike enslaved people, serfs could not be bought or sold, but they also could not leave the land |

- These systems left European monarchs comparatively weak. Later in this period, sustained conflicts like the **Hundred Years' War** between England and France exposed the limits of feudal military organization and pushed both crowns toward stronger centralized taxation and standing armies — a shift that would accelerate in later units.

> **Big Picture:** Europe's decentralization looks like a weakness next to the Song bureaucracy or the Inca road network — but the Catholic Church filled the unifying role that a central government played elsewhere, showing that religious authority could substitute for political centralization.

## 1.7 — Comparison in the Period c. 1200 to c. 1450

This module is explicitly about comparison — the AP exam directly tests how governance strategies across Unit 1's regions were similar and different. Three lenses cover most comparison prompts: **centralization**, **religious legitimacy**, and **trade/tribute networks**.

### Centralized vs. Decentralized Governance
| Highly Centralized | Decentralized / Fragmented |
|---|---|
| **Song China** — unified bureaucracy, civil service exam, direct imperial control | **Medieval Europe** — feudalism spread power across lords and vassals; no single unifying state |
| **Inca Empire** — direct administrative control, state-run mit'a labor, integrated road network | **Aztec Empire** — conquered city-states kept local rulers; empire held together by tribute and fear, not administration |
| **Mali/Songhai** — centralized Islamic empires controlling trans-Saharan trade | **Hausa Kingdoms** and **Swahili city-states** — independent, competing city-states |
| — | **Dar al-Islam after 1258** — culturally unified but politically split among Seljuks, Mamluks, and the Delhi Sultanate |

### Religion as a Tool of Legitimacy
- **China**: **Neo-Confucianism** framed hierarchy (ruler over subject) as a natural, cosmic order — legitimacy came from moral philosophy and historical continuity, not from a single deity.
- **Dar al-Islam, Mali, the Delhi Sultanate**: rulers legitimized power as defenders or spreaders of **Islam**, and Islamic law provided a shared legal/cultural framework even across politically separate states.
- **Inca**: the **Sapa Inca** ruled as a semi-divine descendant of the sun god — religious and political authority were the same office.
- **Europe**: kings needed the **Catholic Church's** blessing (often literal coronation by the Church) to be seen as legitimate; the Church, not any king, was Western Europe's most unifying institution.
- **Southeast Asia**: states like the Khmer Empire and Majapahit blended **Hindu and Buddhist** symbolism, often within the same monument, to appeal to diverse subjects and trading partners rather than enforcing one faith.

### Trade and Tribute as Governance Tools
- **Tribute systems** extended influence without direct conquest: China's tribute relationships spread Confucian culture to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam; the Aztec Empire used tribute from conquered city-states to fund its capital without administering those territories directly.
- **Trade networks doubled as diplomatic and religious channels**: the trans-Saharan trade tied West African empires into Dar al-Islam; the Indian Ocean trade carried Hinduism, Buddhism, and Islam into Southeast Asia through merchant communities rather than armies; the Swahili Coast's Indian Ocean trade produced a hybrid Bantu-Arabic language and culture.
- **Forced labor as a state-building tool**: the Inca **mit'a** and European **manorialism/serfdom** both extracted labor (not just goods) from ordinary people to fund the state or the lord — but the Inca system was state-administered and rotational, while manorialism bound serfs to a single lord's land indefinitely.

> **Big Picture:** No two regions built authority the same way, but every region combined the same four ingredients — religion, bureaucracy or personal loyalty, military force, and control of trade or tribute — in different proportions. Identifying which ingredient a state leaned on hardest, and comparing that choice across regions, is the core skill Unit 1 is building toward for the rest of the course.
