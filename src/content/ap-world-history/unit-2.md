# Unit 2: Networks of Exchange (c. 1200 to c. 1450)

Between roughly 1200 and 1450, three great networks — the overland **Silk Roads**, the maritime **Indian Ocean** network, and the **Trans-Saharan** routes — expanded and thickened, moving more people, goods, ideas, religions, technologies, and diseases across Afro-Eurasia than ever before. New tools (the **compass**, **astrolabe**, improved **camel saddles**) and new financial instruments (**paper money**, **bills of exchange**) made longer, safer, higher-volume trade possible. The **Mongol Empire** was the era's great facilitator — the **Pax Mongolica** made the Silk Roads safer than they had ever been. The consequences went far beyond commerce: Islam and Buddhism spread along trade routes, Greek and Islamic learning flowed back into Europe, staple crops like **Champa rice** reshaped populations, and the **Bubonic Plague** rode the same routes as silk and spices to become the darkest consequence of a newly connected world.

---

## 2.1 — The Silk Roads

### Overview
- The Silk Roads were a network of overland trade routes linking **China, Central Asia, the Middle East, and Europe**, in continuous use in some form since antiquity but reaching a peak of volume and safety in this period.
- Because the journey across Central Asia was long, dangerous, and expensive, only **high-value, low-bulk luxury goods** made economic sense to transport — this is the central cause-and-effect relationship the AP exam tests for this topic.

### Goods Traded
- **From China**: silk (the network's namesake), porcelain, tea, paper.
- **From Central/South/West Asia**: cotton textiles, precious stones, carpets, glassware, horses.
- **From Europe**: wool, linen, silver, wine.
- Rising demand for luxury exports **reshaped production** in China — some farmers shifted from subsistence food crops to cash crops and craft goods (raw silk, finished textiles) to feed export demand.

### Infrastructure and Commercial Innovations
- **Caravanserai**: roadside inns spaced roughly a day's travel apart along the routes. They offered merchants, camels, and goods shelter, security, water, and a place to trade information and rest — without them, sustained long-distance overland trade at this scale would not have been possible.
- **Paper money** (China, especially under the Mongol-ruled Yuan Dynasty): far lighter and more portable than metal coinage, easing large transactions.
- **"Flying cash" / letters of credit** (China): a merchant could deposit money in one city and withdraw an equivalent sum in another, reducing the risk of carrying cash over long distances — an early proto-banking system.
- **Bills of exchange** (used increasingly by Islamic and later Italian merchants): a written promise to pay a set sum at a future date or in another location, functioning much like a modern check and allowing merchants to extend and manage credit across long distances.
- **Kashgar**: a Central Asian oasis city where two major branches of the Silk Roads converged; it grew wealthy as a hub where merchants exchanged goods, currencies, and information.

> [!note] Key Concept
> The Silk Roads' defining feature was **selectivity**: high transport cost meant only compact, expensive luxury goods were profitable, unlike the bulk-goods trade of the Indian Ocean (2.3).

## 2.2 — The Mongol Empire and the Making of the Modern World

### Rise and Political Structure
- **Chinggis (Genghis) Khan** united the Mongol and Turkic nomadic tribes of the Central Asian steppe in the early 1200s and launched a wave of conquest that built the **largest contiguous land empire in world history**.
- The Mongols toppled major existing powers, including the **Song Dynasty** in China and the **Abbasid Caliphate** in Baghdad (1258).
- After Chinggis Khan's death, the empire eventually divided into four major **khanates**, each ruled by a **khan** descended from Chinggis Khan:
  - **Yuan Dynasty** (China) — founded by **Kublai Khan**.
  - **Ilkhanate** (Persia/Middle East).
  - **Golden Horde** (Russia/Eastern Europe).
  - **Chagatai Khanate** (Central Asia).

### Military and Administrative Strengths
- Mongol success rested on **superior cavalry mobility**, composite bows usable on horseback, disciplined unit organization, and effective use of intelligence and psychological warfare (a reputation for brutality toward cities that resisted often induced other cities to surrender without a fight).
- Despite their reputation for destruction, the Mongols were pragmatic administrators: they generally **tolerated local religions**, employed local bureaucrats and administrators, and prioritized keeping trade routes open and taxable over imposing uniform Mongol culture.

### The Pax Mongolica ("Mongol Peace")
- With most of the Silk Roads' length under a single (if internally divided) political umbrella, the Mongols **suppressed banditry, standardized protections for merchants, and paid high prices for foreign luxury goods** to encourage trade.
- The result was the safest and most active period the overland Silk Roads had ever seen — trade volume, distance traveled, and the diversity of goods and travelers all increased sharply.

### Cultural and Technological Transfers Enabled by Mongol Rule
- Mongol-controlled routes carried **Greek and Islamic medical, scientific, and astronomical knowledge** across Eurasia.
- The Mongols themselves had no native writing system; they adopted the **Uyghur script** to create a written form of the Mongolian language, administer their empire, and keep records.
- Mongol rule facilitated the westward spread of **gunpowder** technology from China (see 2.5).

> [!important] Why the Mongols Belong in a "Networks of Exchange" Unit
> The Mongols are covered here — not in a separate "empires" unit — specifically because their conquests **facilitated** rather than disrupted long-distance trade. The Pax Mongolica is the direct cause of the Silk Roads' 13th–14th century boom.

## 2.3 — Exchange in the Indian Ocean

### Overview
- The Indian Ocean network linked **East Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, South Asia (India), Southeast Asia, and China** via maritime routes — and, unlike the Silk Roads, it moved both **bulk staple goods** (textiles, grain, spices) and luxury items, because ships could carry vastly more cargo than camel caravans.

### Enabling Technology
- **Monsoon winds**: seasonal winds that blow reliably in one direction for part of the year and reverse for the rest — merchants timed voyages around this predictable cycle, which made long open-water crossings (rather than just coast-hugging) practical.
- **Magnetic compass** (originally a Chinese innovation): allowed sailors to hold a course out of sight of land.
- **Astrolabe**: allowed navigators to calculate latitude by measuring the angle of the sun or stars, improving open-ocean accuracy.
- **Dhows** (Arab/Indian Ocean vessels with triangular lateen sails, good for tacking against the wind) and **Chinese junks** (large, sturdy ships with watertight compartments and big cargo holds) were the era's key ship designs.

### Key States and Port Cities
- **Swahili city-states** (East African coast, e.g., Kilwa, Mombasa, Mogadishu): grew wealthy as intermediary ports linking interior African goods (gold, ivory) to the wider **Dar al-Islam** trading world.
- **Arab and Persian merchant diasporic communities** settled along the East African coast, intermarrying with local Bantu-speaking populations. This exchange produced the **Swahili language** (a Bantu language with heavy Arabic vocabulary and Arabic-script writing) and spread **Islam** along the coast.
- **Gujarat** (India) and the **Sultanate of Malacca** (Southeast Asia, controlling the strategic Strait of Malacca) were major hub states connecting the western and eastern halves of the network.

### Zheng He and Chinese Maritime Power
- **Zheng He**, a Muslim eunuch admiral serving the Ming Dynasty, led seven massive naval expeditions (1405–1433) through the Indian Ocean as far as East Africa and Arabia, commanding fleets of "treasure ships" far larger than contemporary European vessels.
- The voyages were meant to **expand and display China's tributary system** — demonstrating Ming power and collecting tribute/recognition from foreign rulers, not to establish permanent colonies.
- The voyages spread Chinese navigational knowledge and shipbuilding methods, though China ended the program after 1433 and turned more inward.

> [!note] Bulk vs. Luxury
> This is the AP exam's favorite Silk Roads vs. Indian Ocean comparison point: the Silk Roads' overland cost structure limited trade to luxury goods, while Indian Ocean shipping's much greater cargo capacity supported large-scale **bulk** trade in everyday goods alongside luxuries.

## 2.4 — Trans-Saharan Trade Networks

### Overview
- The Trans-Saharan network connected **West Africa** (source of gold, and later, enslaved people) to **North Africa and the wider Islamic Mediterranean world** (source of salt, textiles, manufactured goods, and connections into the Silk Roads/Indian Ocean systems) via routes crossing the Sahara Desert.

### Enabling Technology
- **Improved camel saddles**: camels (already domesticated for desert travel) could carry substantially larger cargo loads once saddle design improved, making the economics of a multi-week desert crossing viable at a much larger scale.
- Organized **caravans**, often numbering in the thousands of camels, crossed the desert together for safety and efficiency, guided by Berber and other desert-experienced traders who knew locations of scarce oases.

### The Mali Empire
- **Mali** grew into one of the wealthiest states in the world during this period by controlling and taxing the trans-Saharan **gold-salt trade** and by connecting itself to the broader **Dar al-Islam**.
- **Mansa Musa**, Mali's most famous ruler, made a legendary **hajj (pilgrimage) to Mecca in 1324**, traveling with an enormous retinue and distributing so much gold along the way (particularly in Cairo) that he reportedly depressed regional gold prices for years afterward. The pilgrimage advertised Mali's wealth to the wider Islamic and Mediterranean world and drew scholars, architects, and merchants back to Mali.
- **Timbuktu**, under Mali's control, became a major center of trade **and** Islamic scholarship, home to mosques, libraries, and a university that drew students from across West Africa and the Islamic world.

### Islam's Spread in West Africa
- Islam entered West Africa gradually and largely peacefully through **merchant networks and traveling scholars**, rather than conquest.
- Conversion was initially concentrated among **ruling elites and urban merchant classes** who saw commercial and diplomatic advantages in shared religious/legal frameworks with North African and Middle Eastern trading partners; rural populations often blended Islam with existing local religious practices — a pattern of religious syncretism common across the era's trade networks.

## 2.5 — Cultural Consequences of Connectivity

### Religious Diffusion
- **Islam** spread along trade routes into **West Africa** (via trans-Saharan trade, 2.4) and into **Southeast Asia** (via Indian Ocean trade, especially to the Sultanate of Malacca and the Indonesian archipelago) — in both cases carried primarily by **merchants**, not conquering armies.
- **Buddhism** continued to spread along the Silk Roads into East Asia and remained influential in Southeast Asia, often existing alongside (rather than fully replacing) other religious traditions.
- **Hinduism** also spread into Southeast Asia via Indian Ocean trade contacts, influencing states like the Khmer Empire.

### Technological and Intellectual Transfers
- **Gunpowder** — the single most historically significant technology transfer of the era — originated in China and moved westward via the Mongols and Islamic intermediaries, eventually reaching Europe, where it would reshape warfare and state-building in later centuries.
- The **House of Wisdom** in Abbasid Baghdad had, in earlier centuries, translated and preserved **Greek and Roman philosophical and scientific texts**; Mongol-era and trade-route contacts helped carry this preserved Classical knowledge — along with original Islamic advances in mathematics, astronomy, and medicine — back into Western Europe, where it helped fuel the intellectual foundations of the later **Renaissance**.
- **Paper-making technology**, developed centuries earlier in China, diffused westward along the Silk Roads and reached the Islamic world and eventually Europe.

### Urban Growth and Decline
- **Hangzhou** (China), located at the terminus of the Grand Canal and connected to Silk Road and coastal trade, grew into one of the largest and wealthiest cities in the world during this period.
- **Baghdad**, the Abbasid capital and a major center of Islamic learning and trade, was **destroyed by Mongol forces in 1258** — a catastrophic blow to Islamic intellectual and political life that historians still debate the long-term significance of.

### Travelers Who Documented the Era
- **Ibn Battuta**: a Muslim scholar from Morocco who spent nearly **30 years** traveling across almost the entire Dar al-Islam (and beyond, into China), documenting the societies, customs, and Islamic communities he encountered — the single richest firsthand source historians have for this era's interconnected world.
- **Marco Polo**: a Venetian merchant whose (partly disputed) account of his years traveling in Mongol-ruled China introduced Europeans to detailed descriptions of Asian wealth and society.
- **Margery Kempe**: an English Christian mystic whose account of her pilgrimages illustrates how ordinary (non-elite) travelers also moved across this connected world for religious purposes.

## 2.6 — Environmental Consequences of Connectivity

### Crop Diffusion and Population Growth
- **Champa rice**, a fast-ripening, drought-resistant rice variety, reached China from the **Champa Kingdom** (present-day Vietnam) via tributary and trade contacts. Its ability to produce **multiple harvests per year** dramatically increased China's food supply and fueled significant population growth.
- Other crops — including bananas and citrus fruits — spread along Indian Ocean and Silk Road routes, similarly reshaping agriculture and diets in the regions that adopted them.

### Disease: The Bubonic Plague
- The **Bubonic Plague** originated in Central Asia and spread **westward along the same Silk Road routes** that had been carrying silk, porcelain, and Mongol armies — reaching the Middle East and then Europe by the mid-14th century (as the **Black Death**).
- The plague killed an estimated **one-third to one-half of Europe's population**, along with massive death tolls across the Middle East and parts of Asia, making it, in the AP framework's own terms, the **darkest consequence of increased connectivity**: the very networks that spread goods, religions, and ideas also spread pathogens with unprecedented speed and reach.
- Historians link the plague's long-term aftermath to major social and economic shifts — including labor shortages in Europe that undermined the feudal system and increased bargaining power for surviving peasants and workers.

> [!warning] Cause and Effect, Not Coincidence
> The AP exam consistently tests this as a direct causal chain: **wider, faster trade networks → wider, faster disease transmission.** The plague is the clearest illustration in the whole course of how a network of exchange carries costs along with benefits.

## 2.7 — Comparison of Economic Exchange

The AP exam frequently asks students to directly compare the Silk Roads, Indian Ocean network, and Trans-Saharan trade. Use this table as a quick-reference study tool.

| Feature | Silk Roads | Indian Ocean Network | Trans-Saharan Trade |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Terrain/Method** | Overland caravan routes across steppe, desert, and mountain terrain | Maritime routes across open ocean and coastal waters | Overland caravan routes across the Sahara Desert |
| **Key Transport Technology** | Camels, caravanserai for rest/resupply | Dhows and junks; monsoon winds; compass; astrolabe | Camels with improved saddles for larger loads |
| **Goods Traded** | Mostly **luxury** goods (silk, porcelain, precious stones) — high value relative to bulk, due to high transport cost | **Bulk** staple goods (textiles, grain, spices) alongside luxury goods — ships carried far more cargo | Gold and enslaved people (south to north); salt and manufactured/textile goods (north to south) |
| **Degree of State Involvement** | Significant — especially under the Mongols, whose Pax Mongolica actively protected and encouraged trade | Moderate — port city-states (e.g., Swahili coast, Malacca) taxed and regulated trade, but no single empire controlled the whole network; China's Zheng He voyages were a state-sponsored exception | Significant — the Mali Empire (and later Songhai) directly controlled and taxed trade routes and gold sources as a core state function |
| **Primary Mechanism of Cultural Diffusion** | Diplomats, monks, and merchants moving along a continuous overland corridor; state-level contact (Mongol khanates) | Merchant communities settling permanently in port cities (e.g., Arab/Persian diaspora on the Swahili coast), producing blended cultures (Swahili language) | Muslim merchants and traveling scholars converting West African elites; pilgrimage (Mansa Musa's hajj) as a diplomatic/religious bridge |
| **Religion Spread** | Buddhism (into East Asia) | Islam and Hinduism (into Southeast Asia); Islam (into East Africa) | Islam (into West Africa, concentrated among elites/urban merchants) |

### Key Comparative Takeaways
- **Transport cost determined cargo type**: overland routes (Silk Roads, Trans-Saharan) were limited to high-value goods that justified the expense and risk of the journey; maritime routes (Indian Ocean) could move bulk goods profitably because ships carried vastly more cargo per trip.
- **State involvement varied by network structure**: a single network could span many independent states (Indian Ocean), be dominated by one empire's direct control (Trans-Saharan trade under Mali), or be transformed by one empire's protection of existing routes without direct control of all commerce (Silk Roads under the Mongols).
- **Cultural diffusion mechanisms differed by contact type**: overland networks spread ideas through mobile individuals (monks, scholars, diplomats) traveling the full length of a route, while maritime networks spread ideas through **permanent diasporic merchant communities** settling in port cities — producing more durable, blended local cultures (like Swahili civilization) than purely transient overland contact typically did.
- All three networks, despite their differences, shared the same basic pattern: **increased connectivity produced both major benefits (wealth, technology, ideas, food security) and major costs (disease, and in some cases, expanded slave trading)** — the throughline that unifies Unit 2 as a whole.
