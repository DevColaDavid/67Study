# Unit 7: Global Conflict (c. 1900–Present)

---

The 20th century opened with revolutions that toppled the **Ottoman, Russian, and Qing** empires, then descended into two catastrophic **World Wars** driven by militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism (**MAIN**) — and, the second time, by fascism and the unresolved grievances of the first. New technology made both wars deadlier and more total than anything before them, and the era produced some of history's worst **mass atrocities**, from the Armenian Genocide to the Holocaust to the atomic bombings that ended the Pacific War. By 1945 the old Western-dominated imperial order had been shattered, clearing the way for decolonization and a new Cold War order built on institutions like the United Nations.

---

## 7.1 — Shifting Power After 1900

### The Ottoman Empire
- **Young Turk Revolution (1908):** Reformist military officers forced Sultan Abdul Hamid II to restore a constitution; the new government secularized schools and law and made Turkish the official language.
- This nationalist "Turkification" alienated the empire's many non-Turkish, non-Muslim minorities (Arabs, Armenians, Greeks) and accelerated the empire's fragmentation rather than saving it.
- The Ottomans allied with Germany and Austria-Hungary in WWI; defeat in 1918 ended the empire outright — its Anatolian core became the Republic of Turkey (1923, under Mustafa Kemal "Atatürk"), while its Arab provinces were carved into League of Nations mandates (see 7.5).

### Russia → Soviet Union
- **Revolution of 1905:** A growing industrial working class and reform-minded middle class demanded political voice; the "Bloody Sunday" massacre of peaceful protesters triggered strikes across the empire. Tsar Nicholas II granted a limited constitution and elected legislature (the Duma) but clawed back most of its power soon after.
- WWI's staggering casualties, food shortages, and military defeats destroyed what legitimacy the tsarist regime had left, sparking the **February Revolution of 1917**, which forced Nicholas II to abdicate.
- The provisional government that followed kept Russia in the war and lost popular support; in the **October Revolution (1917)**, **Vladimir Lenin** and the Bolsheviks seized power promising "Peace, Land, and Bread."
- Russia exited WWI via the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918), ceding significant territory to Germany. A brutal civil war (Reds vs. Whites) followed; the Bolshevik victory produced the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR, 1922)** — the world's first Communist state.

### Qing China
- Decades of foreign humiliation (the Opium Wars, unequal treaties, the Boxer Rebellion's failure) had gutted the Qing dynasty's credibility.
- The **Xinhai Revolution (1911)**, led by revolutionaries inspired by **Sun Yat-sen** and his "Three Principles of the People" (nationalism, democracy, livelihood), forced the last Qing emperor to abdicate in 1912 — ending **over 2,000 years** of Chinese imperial rule.
- The new Republic of China quickly fractured under regional warlords. A prolonged civil war between the Nationalists (Kuomintang) and Communists ultimately ended with **Mao Zedong**'s Communist Party taking power in 1949.

### Mexico
- The **Mexican Revolution (beginning 1910)** ousted the long-ruling dictator **Porfirio Díaz**, whose regime had favored foreign investors and large landowners over the peasantry.
- Reformer Francisco Madero was assassinated; peasant leaders **Pancho Villa** (north) and **Emiliano Zapata** (south, under the cry "Tierra y Libertad" — Land and Liberty) led armed movements demanding land redistribution.
- The **Constitution of 1917** enshrined land reform, labor rights, and limits on the Catholic Church and foreign ownership — one of the most progressive constitutions in the world at the time.

> **Big Picture:** All four upheavals share a pattern — old, land-based or dynastic authority proved unable to meet the demands of an industrializing, increasingly nationalist populace, and collapsed under internal pressure that WWI (for Russia and the Ottomans) then accelerated.

## 7.2 — Causes of World War I

### The MAIN Causes

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Militarism** | A decades-long arms race, especially the Anglo-German naval rivalry (dreadnought battleships) and large standing conscript armies across the continent, made war planning aggressive and rapid mobilization schedules (like Germany's Schlieffen Plan) hard to stop once started. |
| **Alliances** | Europe split into two rigid blocs: the **Triple Alliance** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the **Triple Entente** (France, Russia, Britain). A conflict between any two members threatened to drag in everyone else automatically. |
| **Imperialism** | Decades of rivalry over African and Asian colonies (e.g., the Moroccan Crises between France and Germany) bred deep mutual suspicion among the great powers. |
| **Nationalism** | Ethnic pride hardened into aggressive state rivalry (especially Franco-German tension over Alsace-Lorraine, lost by France in 1871) and fueled minority independence movements within multiethnic empires — especially in the volatile **Balkans**, dubbed the "powder keg of Europe." |

### The Spark
- **June 28, 1914:** **Gavrilo Princip**, a Bosnian Serb nationalist tied to the secret society the Black Hand, assassinated **Archduke Franz Ferdinand**, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, in Sarajevo.
- Austria-Hungary issued Serbia an ultimatum designed to be rejected, then declared war. The alliance system pulled in Russia (Serbia's protector), then Germany, France, and Britain within about a month — turning a regional Balkan crisis into a continental, and soon global, war.

> [!tip] Memory Device
> **MAIN** = Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism. The assassination was the spark, not the underlying cause — remove any one of the MAIN pressures and the Sarajevo shooting likely stays a regional crisis.

## 7.3 — Conducting World War I

### Total War
- **Total war** mobilizes an entire society — industry, labor, civilians — for the war effort, blurring the line between combatant and civilian target. WWI was the first true total war in modern history.
- Governments introduced conscription, rationing, and war-bond drives, and drew women into factory and farm labor on an unprecedented scale as men left for the front.

### New Military Technology
- **Machine guns**, **long-range artillery**, **poison gas**, and eventually **tanks** and military aircraft turned the Western Front into a stalemate of **trench warfare** — battles like the **Somme** and **Verdun** (both 1916) each produced roughly a million casualties for only a few miles of ground.
- New technology dramatically outpaced tactics: commanders kept ordering infantry charges into machine-gun fire long after the old style of offensive warfare had become obsolete.

### Propaganda
- Governments used **propaganda** — biased, emotionally manipulative state messaging — to demonize the enemy, sustain morale, and justify years of sacrifice and mounting casualties to civilian populations.

### A Truly Global War
- This was not just a European war. **Colonial and dominion troops** were essential to the fighting: over 500,000 French colonial soldiers from Africa and Southeast Asia served on the Western Front; Indian, Australian, New Zealand (ANZAC), and Canadian troops fought at **Gallipoli** (1915) — a failed British-led attempt to knock the Ottoman Empire out of the war by seizing the Dardanelles Strait — and on other fronts.
- China, though neutral, contributed the **Chinese Labor Corps** — roughly 140,000–200,000 laborers who dug trenches, built railways, and supported Allied logistics in Europe.
- **Japan**, allied with Britain, declared war on Germany in 1914 and seized German colonial holdings in the Pacific (the Mariana, Caroline, and Marshall Islands) and the German concession at Qingdao, China — expanding Japanese power in Asia well beyond the European battlefields.
- Fighting also raged in German East and Southwest Africa and in the Middle East, where Ottoman forces fought the British; Arab nationalists — encouraged by vague British promises of postwar independence — revolted against Ottoman rule (the "Arab Revolt").

### Outcome and Peace
- The United States, provoked by unrestricted German submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram, entered the war in **1917**, tipping the balance toward the Allies.
- President **Woodrow Wilson**'s **Fourteen Points (1918)** called for free trade, self-determination for nations, and a **League of Nations** to prevent future wars — but the other Allied leaders, wanting to punish Germany, watered most of it down at the peace table.
- The **Treaty of Versailles (1919)** forced Germany to accept full blame for the war (the "war guilt clause"), pay massive reparations, cede territory, and drastically shrink its military — terms that humiliated Germany without permanently weakening it, planting seeds of resentment revisited in 7.6.
- The **League of Nations** was created to mediate international disputes, but its lack of enforcement power (and the US Senate's refusal to join) crippled its effectiveness from the start.

> WWI killed an estimated **9–10 million soldiers** and millions more civilians, and left much of a generation of young European men dead or maimed — a scale of loss that shaped interwar politics and culture for the next two decades.

## 7.4 — Economy in the Interwar Period

### Why the Great Depression Went Global
- WWI left European economies deeply indebted, and their postwar recovery leaned heavily on **loans from the United States**. When the US stock market crashed in **October 1929**, American banks called in those loans and investment dried up — collapsing trade and production worldwide.
- Falling demand, bank failures, and mass unemployment fed on each other in a downward spiral; by the early 1930s, unemployment topped 25% in the US and Germany alike.

### Government Responses

| Country / Bloc | Response |
|---|---|
| **United States** | FDR's **New Deal**: large-scale public works and infrastructure projects, banking reform, and new social programs (unemployment relief, old-age pensions) — expanding the government's role in the economy without abandoning capitalism or democracy. |
| **Soviet Union** | Lenin's limited market-based **New Economic Policy** gave way under Stalin to **Five-Year Plans** — centrally planned, breakneck industrialization — and the forced **collectivization of agriculture**, which seized land into state-run collective farms. |
| **Germany / Italy** | Fascist **corporatism**: the state directed industry and labor (often through public-works and rearmament spending) while suppressing independent unions and political opposition — a route to "recovery" bound tightly to militarization. |
| **Britain / France** | More cautious, largely orthodox responses (tariffs, limited relief); slower recoveries left both politically vulnerable through the 1930s. |

> [!warning] Holodomor
> Ukraine was the USSR's breadbasket. Stalin's forced collectivization — combined with grain quotas set deliberately too high — produced a catastrophic, state-engineered famine known as the **Holodomor** ("death by hunger," 1932–1933), which killed an estimated **3.5–5 million Ukrainians**. Many historians classify it as genocide.

### Cause and Effect
- The economic devastation of the Depression discredited liberal democracy and free-market capitalism in the eyes of many desperate citizens — fueling the extremist political movements (fascism, and hardened communism) that reshaped the 1930s and drove the world toward war (see 7.6, 7.9).

## 7.5 — Unresolved Tensions After World War I

### The Mandate System — Disguised Imperialism
- Colonial troops from Africa, Asia, and the Pacific fought and died for the Allies in WWI, expecting independence or at least greater autonomy as their reward — but the peace settlement offered neither.
- The League of Nations' **mandate system** redistributed the German and Ottoman empires' former territories to Britain and France (e.g., Britain got Iraq and Palestine; France got Syria and Lebanon) under the fiction that these powers would prepare the territories for eventual self-rule — in practice, colonial administration by another name.
- British wartime promises were contradictory: the secret **Sykes-Picot Agreement** (1916) divided the Middle East between Britain and France, while the **Balfour Declaration** (1917) promised a Jewish national home in Palestine — commitments that clashed with promises of Arab independence and seeded lasting regional conflict.

### Japan's Expansion
- Japan invaded Manchuria in **1931** (the Mukden Incident) and established the puppet state of **Manchukuo** in 1932; when the League of Nations condemned the invasion, Japan simply **withdrew from the League (1933)** and continued expanding under the banner of a "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere."
- The League's inability to stop or punish Japan exposed its fundamental weakness and emboldened other revisionist powers (Italy, Germany) to test the postwar order in the years that followed.

### Rising Anti-Colonial Movements
- **India:** The **Indian National Congress**, energized by **Mohandas Gandhi**'s philosophy of nonviolent resistance (*satyagraha*) and civil disobedience, built a mass independence movement — anger at British rule deepened after the **Amritsar Massacre (1919)**, in which British troops killed hundreds of unarmed protesters.
- **West Africa:** Colonial subjects who had served in WWI or supplied its war economy organized labor strikes and early nationalist associations, pressing (largely unsuccessfully at this stage) for political rights and economic fairness from colonial administrations.
- **China:** The **May Fourth Movement (1919)** erupted after the Treaty of Versailles handed Germany's former concessions in Shandong to Japan rather than returning them to China — a betrayal that discredited Western-style liberalism among Chinese intellectuals and helped fuel the rise of Chinese nationalism and communism.

> **Big Picture:** WWI was fought partly in the name of "self-determination," but the postwar settlement extended empire rather than dismantling it. That contradiction — promises of freedom paired with continued colonial control — is the throughline connecting the interwar years to the decolonization movements that would explode after WWII.

## 7.6 — Causes of World War II

### Four Interlocking Causes

1. **Treaty of Versailles humiliation:** Germany's forced war guilt, reparations, and territorial losses bred deep resentment; Italy, despite fighting on the winning side, felt cheated of the territory it had been promised (a grievance nationalists called the "mutilated victory").
2. **Imperial aspirations:** Japan's invasion of Manchuria (1931), Italy's invasion of Ethiopia (1935), and Germany's drive to reclaim "lost" territory and *Lebensraum* ("living space") in Eastern Europe all revived pre-WWI-style territorial expansion.
3. **The Great Depression:** Mass unemployment and desperation made populations across Europe and Japan receptive to extremist movements promising national revival, jobs, and someone to blame.
4. **Rise of fascist and totalitarian regimes:** **Benito Mussolini** built the first fascist state in Italy (from 1922); **Adolf Hitler** and the **Nazi Party** took power in Germany (1933); militarist factions dominated the government in Japan; **Joseph Stalin** ran a totalitarian Communist USSR.

> [!note] Key Definitions
> - **Fascism:** Far-right ultranationalism combined with authoritarian, one-party rule, militarism, and suppression of dissent.
> - **Totalitarianism:** A political system in which the state seeks to control virtually every aspect of citizens' public and private lives.

### The Road to War
- Hitler openly violated Versailles — canceling reparations, rebuilding the military, remilitarizing the Rhineland (1936) — while Britain and France pursued **appeasement**, conceding to his demands (most notoriously at the **Munich Agreement, 1938**, which handed Germany the Sudetenland) to avoid another war.
- Appeasement only emboldened further aggression: Germany annexed Austria (the *Anschluss*, 1938) and the rest of Czechoslovakia (1939) without resistance.
- The **Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact (August 1939)** — a nonaggression treaty between Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union that secretly divided Eastern Europe between them — cleared the way for invasion.
- **September 1, 1939:** Germany invaded **Poland**; Britain and France, having pledged to defend Poland, declared war on Germany — WWII officially began in Europe. (Japan's war in Asia had already been underway since its full-scale invasion of China in 1937.)

## 7.7 — Conducting World War II

### A Second Total War, Fought Worldwide
- Both sides fought as coalitions: the **Axis** (Germany, Italy, Japan) versus the **Allies** (Britain, France, the USSR after Germany's 1941 invasion, and the United States after Pearl Harbor).
- Germany's **blitzkrieg** ("lightning war") — fast, coordinated tank and air assaults — overran Poland and Western Europe in weeks, a sharp contrast to WWI's static trenches.
- **Pacific theater:** Japan's surprise attack on **Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941)** brought the US fully into the war; the Allies then fought a grinding island-hopping campaign (Midway, Guadalcanal, Iwo Jima, Okinawa) back across the Pacific toward Japan.
- **Eastern Front:** The German invasion of the USSR (Operation Barbarossa, 1941) opened the war's largest and deadliest front — the Soviet defense at **Stalingrad (1942–43)** turned the tide in Europe and cost millions of lives on both sides.
- Ideological mobilization was even more total than in WWI: both fascist and Allied propaganda framed the war as an existential struggle over entire ways of life, not just territory.

### Wartime Injustice on the Home Front
- **United States:** After Pearl Harbor, over 110,000 Japanese Americans — most of them citizens — were forcibly removed to **internment camps** based solely on ancestry.
- **Nazi Germany:** The Nuremberg Laws (1935) had already stripped German Jews of citizenship and rights; after 1939, Jews and other targeted groups were confined to **ghettos** and later deported to **concentration and death camps** (see 7.8).

### New, Deadlier Technology
- **Strategic firebombing** of cities (Dresden, Tokyo) killed hundreds of thousands of civilians in single raids, extending "total war" directly onto civilian populations far from any front line.
- The United States dropped **atomic bombs** on **Hiroshima (August 6, 1945)** and **Nagasaki (August 9, 1945)**, killing an estimated 100,000–200,000 people, mostly civilians, and forcing Japan's surrender within days — ending WWII and opening the nuclear age.

> WWII was the deadliest conflict in human history — estimates of total deaths (military and civilian) range from **70 to 85 million**, roughly 3% of the world's population at the time.

## 7.8 — Mass Atrocities After 1900

### Armenian Genocide (1915–1923)
- Ottoman authorities under the **Young Turk** government, already reeling from wartime losses, viewed Armenian Christians as disloyal potential collaborators with Russia and used WWI as cover for mass extermination.
- Armenians were killed outright, worked to death, or sent on **forced death marches** into the Syrian desert without food or water.
- An estimated **600,000–1.5 million Armenians** died. Turkey has never officially acknowledged the killings as genocide, making it a persistent point of international dispute.

### The Holocaust (1941–1945)
- Nazi racial ideology held that Germans belonged to a "master race" and that Jews, along with Roma, disabled people, gay people, and political dissidents, were subhuman threats to be eliminated.
- Hitler's regime moved from discrimination (Nuremberg Laws) to ghettoization to the **"Final Solution"** — industrialized mass murder at extermination camps like Auschwitz-Birkenau.
- **Roughly 6 million Jews** and an additional **5 million** Roma, disabled people, political prisoners, and others were murdered.
- The Holocaust's roots connect to pseudo-scientific racial hierarchies and **Social Darwinism** (Unit 6) that had already been used to justify colonial violence and racial caste systems (Unit 4) decades earlier.

### The Nanjing Massacre (1937–1938)
- After capturing the Chinese capital of Nanjing during the Second Sino-Japanese War (which merged into WWII), Imperial Japanese troops carried out weeks of mass killing, looting, and sexual violence against civilians and disarmed soldiers.
- Death toll estimates vary widely by source — from roughly 40,000 up to 300,000 — but the systematic brutality against a civilian population is not disputed by mainstream historians.
- This atrocity, alongside Japan's system of forced military sexual slavery ("comfort women") across occupied Asia, is a reminder that WWII-era mass atrocities were not confined to the European theater.

### Later 20th-Century Genocides (Context/Legacy)
- **Cambodian Genocide (1975–1979):** The Khmer Rouge regime under Pol Pot, pursuing a radical agrarian-communist utopia, killed an estimated **1.5–2 million** Cambodians through executions, forced labor, and starvation.
- **Rwandan Genocide (1994):** Ethnic Hutu extremists killed an estimated **500,000–800,000** Tutsi and moderate Hutu civilians in roughly 100 days, while international powers largely failed to intervene.

### Accountability and Prevention
- The **Nuremberg Trials (1945–1946)** prosecuted top Nazi leaders for war crimes and "crimes against humanity" — establishing, for the first time, that individuals (not just states) could be held internationally accountable for atrocities, even when following government orders.
- The **UN Genocide Convention (1948)** formally defined genocide as an international crime — a direct legal response to the Holocaust and the Armenian Genocide, though enforcement in later genocides (Cambodia, Rwanda) proved inconsistent at best.

## 7.9 — Causation in Global Conflict

### Tracing the Cause-and-Effect Chain
The two World Wars are best understood not as separate events but as one connected chain of cause and effect spanning nearly half a century:

1. **WWI's unresolved tensions** (7.5) — the punitive Treaty of Versailles, the mandate system's disguised imperialism, and Japan's unchecked expansion — left both a humiliated Germany and a discredited League of Nations unable to enforce the peace it had written.
2. **The Great Depression** (7.4) then destroyed what fragile stability interwar democracies had, radicalizing politics across Europe and Japan and handing fascist and militarist movements the popular support they needed to seize power.
3. **Those two pressures combined** — nationalist resentment plus economic desperation — directly produced the aggressive, expansionist regimes (7.6) whose invasions (Manchuria, Ethiopia, the Rhineland, Poland) triggered WWII.
4. **WWII's conduct** (7.7–7.8) — total war, occupation, and genocide on an even larger scale than WWI — then reshaped the postwar world: European empires were fatally weakened (accelerating decolonization), the United States and USSR emerged as rival superpowers, and new institutions (the **United Nations**, founded 1945; the Genocide Convention, 1948) were built specifically to prevent the failures of the interwar system from repeating.

### Weighing Relative Significance
AP World History's synthesis skill asks you to rank and defend which causes mattered *most*, not just list them:
- Some historians emphasize **structural/systemic causes** — the alliance system, imperial competition, and economic cycles that made conflict likely regardless of individual choices.
- Others emphasize **contingency and individual agency** — specific decisions (the assassination in Sarajevo, Hitler's personal ideology and choices, appeasement at Munich) that could plausibly have gone differently and changed the outcome.
- The strongest argument acknowledges both: structural pressures created the *conditions* for war, but specific human decisions determined *when* and *how* those pressures erupted into catastrophe.

> **Big Picture:** Global Conflict is fundamentally a unit about causation operating on multiple time scales at once — immediate sparks (an assassination, an invasion), medium-term pressures (economic collapse, unresolved treaties), and long-term structural forces (imperialism, nationalism, industrial military technology) all have to be weighed together to explain why the 20th century's first half was so catastrophically violent.
