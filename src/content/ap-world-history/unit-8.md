# Unit 8: Cold War and Decolonization (1900–Present)

After World War II left Europe devastated, the United States and the Soviet Union emerged as rival superpowers locked in an ideological struggle between **democratic capitalism** and **authoritarian communism** — the Cold War, fought through alliances, an arms race, and dozens of **proxy wars** rather than direct combat between the two superpowers. At the same time, a war-weakened Europe could no longer hold its overseas empires, and a wave of **decolonization** swept Asia, Africa, and the Middle East — some colonies won independence through negotiation and nonviolent protest, others through years of armed struggle. The two stories are inseparable: newly independent states became Cold War battlegrounds, and the borders colonial powers left behind still shape conflicts today.

---

## 8.1 — Setting the Stage for the Cold War and Decolonization

### Why the US Became a Superpower
- **Economic strength**: WWII dramatically boosted US industrial production, while Europe and Asia's major economies lay in ruins — the US ended the war as the only major power with its infrastructure intact.
- **Marshall Plan** (1948): Billions of dollars in aid to rebuild Western Europe → tied European recovery to American economic and political influence.
- **Military edge**: Sole possessor of the **atomic bomb** from 1945 until the USSR caught up in 1949.

### Why the USSR Became a Superpower
- **Economy**: A centralized command economy let the state direct all resources toward rapid postwar reconstruction and military production.
- **Military**: Tested its own atomic bomb in **August 1949**, ending the US nuclear monopoly and establishing rough military parity.
- **Territorial control**: Soviet armies occupied Eastern Europe at war's end, giving Moscow a ready-made sphere of influence.

### Why Decolonization Accelerated After WWII
- Colonial troops from Africa and Asia fought and died for Britain and France in both World Wars, often on the promise (implicit or explicit) of greater self-rule afterward — a promise repeatedly broken.
- **Europe was too economically and militarily exhausted** after 1945 to keep suppressing independence movements the way it had before the war.
- The war itself discredited European claims of racial and civilizational superiority — Japan's early victories over European colonial forces in Asia (1941–42) shattered the myth of European invincibility.
- New international norms — the UN Charter's language on self-determination, and both superpowers' rhetorical opposition to old-style colonialism (for very different ideological reasons) — gave independence movements diplomatic cover.

> **Big Picture:** Two exhausted empires (Britain, France) losing their grip at the exact moment two ideologically opposed new superpowers were rising to fill the vacuum — that collision is the engine driving the rest of this unit.

---

## 8.2 — The Cold War

### Two Root Causes
1. **Conflicting, universalizing ideologies**: the US promoted democratic capitalism as the model for the postwar world; the USSR promoted authoritarian, one-party communism. Each saw the other's system as a threat to its own security and legitimacy.
2. **Mutual mistrust**: Stalin had promised free elections in the Eastern European countries the Red Army liberated/occupied; he broke that promise, installing communist governments instead. These became Soviet **satellite states**, cementing Western suspicion of Soviet intentions.

### The Arms Race and "Hot" Flashpoints
- Both sides built massive nuclear arsenals through the 1950s–80s; the resulting doctrine of **Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD)** — any nuclear first strike would trigger a retaliatory strike destroying both sides — is credited with preventing direct US–USSR war despite decades of tension.
- **Berlin Blockade and Airlift (1948–49)**: Stalin cut off land access to West Berlin (deep inside Soviet-occupied East Germany); the US and allies airlifted supplies for nearly a year rather than abandon the city — an early Cold War test of resolve.
- **Berlin Wall (built 1961)**: East Germany walled off West Berlin to stop the flood of East Germans fleeing to the West — became the era's most visible symbol of the "Iron Curtain" dividing Europe.
- **Cuban Missile Crisis (1962)**: The USSR secretly placed nuclear missiles in Cuba, 90 miles from Florida; a tense 13-day standoff with the US ended in a negotiated Soviet withdrawal (in exchange for a secret US pledge to remove missiles from Turkey) — the closest the two superpowers came to direct nuclear war.

### Military Alliances
- **NATO** (North Atlantic Treaty Organization, 1949): US + Western European allies — an attack on one member is an attack on all.
- **Warsaw Pact** (1955): USSR + Eastern European satellite states, formed as a direct counter to NATO.
- These blocs divided Europe into two armed camps for the rest of the Cold War.

## 8.3 — Effects of the Cold War

### Proxy Wars
Rather than fighting each other directly, the US and USSR armed, funded, and advised opposing sides in smaller regional conflicts across Asia, Latin America, and Africa:

| Conflict | US-backed side | USSR/communist-backed side | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Korean War** (1950–53) | Anti-communist South Korea | Communist North Korea (aided by China) | Stalemate; peninsula divided at the **38th parallel**, still divided today |
| **Vietnam War** (1955–75) | Anti-communist South Vietnam | Communist North Vietnam (Viet Minh/Viet Cong) | North Vietnamese victory; country reunified under communist rule (1975) |
| **Nicaraguan Contra War** (1979–90) | Anti-communist Contras | Sandinista socialist government, backed by Cuba and the USSR | Negotiated elections; Sandinistas voted out in 1990 |
| **Angolan Civil War** (1975–2002) | Anti-communist UNITA/FNLA factions | Communist MPLA, backed by Cuba and the USSR | Decades of devastating civil war after independence from Portugal |

### Global Alignment: The Non-Aligned Movement
- Not every new nation wanted to pick a side. The **1955 Bandung Conference** in Indonesia, hosted by President **Sukarno** and attended by leaders like India's **Jawaharlal Nehru** and Egypt's **Gamal Abdel Nasser**, brought Asian and African states together around anti-colonialism and neutrality.
- That cooperation formalized into the **Non-Aligned Movement**, founded at the **1961 Belgrade Conference** by Nehru, Nasser, Yugoslavia's **Tito**, Sukarno, and Ghana's **Kwame Nkrumah** — nations explicitly refusing to align with either superpower bloc.
- Non-aligned and newly independent nations became known collectively as the **"Third World"** (distinct from the capitalist "First World" and communist "Second World").

### Other Effects
- An expensive **arms race** and **space race** (Soviet Sputnik launch, 1957; US Moon landing, 1969) consumed enormous resources on both sides, partly as prestige competition.
- Both superpowers used foreign aid, military bases, and covert action (including CIA- and KGB-backed coups) to pull newly independent states into their orbit — a pattern explored further in **8.9**.

## 8.4 — Spread of Communism After 1900

### China
- Civil war between the Nationalist Party (**Chiang Kai-shek**) and the Communist Party (**Mao Zedong**) paused to fight Japan in WWII, then resumed.
- The Soviet-backed Communists won in **1949**; Chiang's Nationalists fled to Taiwan.
- **Great Leap Forward** (1958–62): Mao's forced-collectivization and rapid-industrialization campaign caused a catastrophic famine, killing an estimated **tens of millions** (scholarly estimates commonly range from 15 to 45+ million).
- The **Sino-Soviet Split** (from the late 1950s) later divided the communist world: China and the USSR competed for leadership of global communism rather than acting as a unified bloc.

### Vietnam
- Ho Chi Minh's communist **Viet Minh** declared independence from France in 1945; France fought to reclaim its colony until its defeat at **Dien Bien Phu (1954)**.
- The 1954 Geneva Accords temporarily split Vietnam at the 17th parallel — communist North, anti-communist South — pending elections that never happened, setting up the Vietnam War (see 8.3).

### Cuba
- **Fidel Castro** began his guerrilla campaign against dictator Fulgencio Batista in **1953** (Moncada Barracks attack), landed with rebel forces from the yacht *Granma* in **1956**, and took power on **January 1, 1959**, after Batista fled.
- Castro ended Cuba's economic dependence on the US, nationalized US-owned land and businesses, redistributed land and wages, and allied with the USSR.
- A US-backed attempt to overthrow him (the 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion) failed and only pushed Castro closer to Moscow, helping set up the Cuban Missile Crisis the following year.

## 8.5 — Decolonization After 1900

Decolonization unfolded on every continent with a European empire, and the path to independence varied enormously by region.

### South Asia: India and Pakistan (Negotiated Independence, 1947)
- The **Indian National Congress (INC)**, founded 1885, spent decades pressing for self-rule.
- Britain, financially and militarily exhausted after WWII and facing a mostly pro-independence Parliament, negotiated withdrawal.
- **Partition**: fear of Hindu-majority rule led the Muslim-led **Muslim League** to demand a separate state → **Pakistan** was carved out alongside India in 1947.
- Partition triggered mass migration and communal violence between Hindus and Muslims; estimates of the dead range from the hundreds of thousands to over a million.

### Southeast Asia: Indonesia and Vietnam
- **Indonesia**: Sukarno declared independence from the Netherlands in 1945, immediately after Japan's WWII surrender. The Dutch fought to reclaim the colony until international pressure (including from the UN and the US) forced them to formally recognize Indonesian independence in **1949**; the conflict cost an estimated 100,000+ Indonesian lives.
- **Vietnam**: covered in 8.4 — France's refusal to grant independence turned decolonization directly into a Cold War proxy conflict.

### Africa: Contrasting Paths
| Country | Path | Key details |
|---|---|---|
| **Ghana** (Gold Coast) | Largely nonviolent | Led by **Kwame Nkrumah**'s Convention People's Party campaign of mass protest and "positive action"; independence in **1957** — the first sub-Saharan African colony to break free of European rule, becoming a symbol for the rest of the continent |
| **Kenya** | Armed uprising | The **Mau Mau Uprising** (1952–60) against British settlers and rule; Britain declared a state of emergency and ran a brutal counterinsurgency, including mass detention camps; independence came in **1963** under **Jomo Kenyatta** |
| **Algeria** | Prolonged, violent war | France refused to negotiate (over a million French settlers lived there); the **National Liberation Front (FLN)**, formed 1954, waged a brutal guerrilla and terror campaign; French reprisals were equally brutal, making it one of the bloodiest independence wars in history; President **Charles de Gaulle** finally negotiated Algerian independence in **1962** |
| **Belgian Congo** | Rushed, chaotic | Belgium granted independence in **1960** with almost no preparation or transfer of administrative experience; Prime Minister **Patrice Lumumba** was quickly caught in a Cold War-driven political crisis and assassinated in 1961 (with Belgian and US complicity, later confirmed) — an early warning sign of the instability explored in 8.6 |

### Middle East: Egypt and Israel
- **Egypt**: formally independent since 1922, but Britain retained major influence, including control of the strategically vital **Suez Canal**. President **Gamal Abdel Nasser** nationalized the Suez Canal Company in **1956** to assert full sovereignty; Britain, France, and Israel invaded in response, but US and Soviet pressure forced their withdrawal — a humiliating moment that confirmed Britain and France could no longer act as independent great powers, and that the superpowers now set the rules in the Middle East.
- **Israel**: Palestine, a British mandate after WWI, had been promised as a Jewish homeland under the 1917 **Balfour Declaration** while remaining majority Muslim and Arab. Jewish migration surged after the Holocaust; the **1948 UN Partition Plan** was accepted by Jewish leaders and rejected by Palestinians and neighboring Arab states, which then attacked the newly declared state of **Israel** — the start of a conflict that continues today.

> **Comparison point:** Ghana and India show that negotiated, largely nonviolent transitions were possible when the colonial power's political will to fight had collapsed. Algeria, Kenya, and Vietnam show the opposite: when the colonizer (or local settlers) refused to leave, decolonization became a prolonged war. The Congo shows a third failure mode — independence granted so abruptly that the state had no capacity to govern itself, opening the door to the Cold War intervention covered next.

## 8.6 — Newly Independent States

Gaining independence was only the first step — new states then had to build functioning economies and governments, often with borders and institutions inherited from the colonizer rather than designed for them.

### Nation-Building Challenges
- **Artificial borders**: colonial boundaries were drawn for administrative convenience, not around existing ethnic, religious, or linguistic communities — leaving new states with built-in internal divisions (e.g., the India–Pakistan and Israel–Palestine conflicts covered in 8.5).
- **Ethnic conflict rooted in colonial policy**: Belgium's colonial administration in Rwanda hardened a divide between Hutu and Tutsi populations (including issuing ethnic identity cards), a division that fed directly into the 1994 **Rwandan Genocide**, in which an estimated 800,000 people, overwhelmingly Tutsi, were killed — a stark example of how colonial-era social engineering could outlive the colonizer by decades.
- **Weak institutions and instability**: many newly independent states, like the Congo (8.5), lacked trained civil servants, military leadership, or stable political parties, since colonial administrations had deliberately excluded local populations from real governing experience — leaving them vulnerable to coups and superpower manipulation.

### Economic Strategies
- **Import substitution industrialization (ISI)**: many governments tried to reduce dependence on former colonizers by building domestic industries to replace imported goods, protected by tariffs.
- **Resource nationalization**: newly independent or newly assertive governments took state control of key industries built under colonialism — Nasser's nationalization of the Suez Canal (8.5) and Iran's Prime Minister **Mohammad Mossadegh** nationalizing the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company in 1951 (prompting a CIA/MI6-backed coup in 1953 that restored the Shah to full power) are two of the clearest examples of this pattern, and of how it could trigger direct foreign intervention.
- **India under Indira Gandhi**: India's first (and to date only) female prime minister pursued socialist five-year economic plans and embraced the **Green Revolution** — new high-yield seed varieties and chemical fertilizers that dramatically increased agricultural output and helped India avoid mass famine.

### Migration to the Metropole
- Colonial subjects migrated in large numbers to the **metropole** (the home territory of the former colonizing power) seeking economic opportunity: South Asians to Britain, Algerians and other former French colonial subjects to France, Filipinos to the United States.
- This migration maintained economic and cultural ties between former colonies and colonizers, while transforming once largely homogenous European societies into **multi-ethnic societies** — a demographic shift still shaping European politics today.

## 8.7 — Global Resistance to Established Power Structures After 1900

Not all resistance targeted colonial rule directly — many movements after 1900 challenged entrenched political, racial, or social power structures more broadly, using both nonviolent and violent methods.

> [!warning] AP Exam Required Names
> **Mohandas Gandhi**, **Martin Luther King Jr.**, and **Nelson Mandela** are specifically named in the AP Course and Exam Description as leaders of nonviolent resistance. Know all three — who they opposed, their key tactics, and their outcomes.

### Nonviolent Resistance
| Leader | Movement | Key tactics | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mohandas Gandhi** (India) | Indian independence, as a leading INC figure | **Civil disobedience**; the **Homespun Movement** (boycotting British textiles); the **Salt March** (1930) — a 240-mile march with tens of thousands of followers to defy the British salt tax, resulting in mass arrests, including Gandhi's own | Helped break Britain's colonial hold on India, culminating in independence (8.5) |
| **Martin Luther King Jr.** (United States) | Civil Rights Movement | Explicitly inspired by Gandhi; the **Montgomery Bus Boycott** (1955–56) against segregated public transit; marches and sit-ins | Supreme Court desegregation rulings; the **Civil Rights Act (1964)** and Voting Rights Act (1965) |
| **Nelson Mandela** (South Africa) | Anti-**apartheid** movement | Led the African National Congress (ANC) in strikes and boycotts; turned to sabotage/armed resistance after the **Sharpeville Massacre** (1960), where police killed 69 unarmed protesters; imprisoned over 20 years | Released 1990; won South Africa's first multiracial election in **1994**, ending apartheid |

### Violent Resistance and Counter-Resistance
- **Chile**: **Augusto Pinochet**, with covert US support, led a 1973 military coup against democratically elected Marxist president **Salvador Allende**. Pinochet's dictatorship suppressed opposition through mass arrests, executions, and torture — an example of an established Cold War power structure (the US, fearing a second Cuba) violently crushing a resistance movement rather than the reverse.
- **Iran**: after decades of Western-backed rule under the Shah (restored to power by the 1953 coup, 8.6), the **1979 Iranian Revolution** overthrew the monarchy and installed an Islamic Republic under **Ayatollah Khomeini** — a resistance movement against a Cold War client state that rejected both capitalist and communist models in favor of religious government.
- **Terrorism as resistance**: groups like Peru's **Shining Path**, Ireland's **IRA**, and the global network **Al Qaeda** (led by **Osama bin Laden**, itself an outgrowth of US-backed Afghan mujahideen networks, 8.8) used violence against civilians and states to pursue political goals — culminating, in Al Qaeda's case, in the **September 11, 2001** attacks that killed nearly 3,000 people.

## 8.8 — End of the Cold War

### Four Causes of Soviet Collapse
1. **The arms race, including Reagan's "Star Wars" (SDI)**: the Strategic Defense Initiative pushed the USSR to try to match US defense spending it could not sustain, accelerating economic decline.
2. **Soviet–Afghan War (1979–89)**: the USSR invaded to prop up Afghanistan's communist government; the US, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistan armed the **Mujahideen** resistance; the resulting decade-long war drained the Soviet economy and military morale.
3. **Gorbachev's reforms**: **Perestroika** ("restructuring") loosened central economic planning; **Glasnost** ("openness") allowed free speech and criticism of the government, which citizens used to voice long-suppressed grievances.
4. **Retreat from satellite states**: Gorbachev signaled the USSR would no longer use force to prop up communist governments in Eastern Europe, removing the fear that had kept those regimes in power.

### How It Ended
- Reform and independence movements swept the Soviet bloc once Moscow's backing was gone.
- **1989**: the **Berlin Wall** fell; Germany reunified soon after.
- **1991**: the Soviet legislature voted to dissolve the USSR, officially ending the Cold War and leaving the United States as the world's sole superpower.

## 8.9 — Causation in the Age of Cold War and Decolonization

Unit 8's events form a single interconnected chain of cause and effect, not a set of parallel stories:

- **WWII's end** left Europe's colonial powers financially and militarily exhausted (8.1) at the exact moment the **US and USSR emerged as ideologically opposed superpowers** (8.1–8.2) competing to fill the resulting global power vacuum.
- That superpower rivalry — not colonial withdrawal alone — is what turned many decolonization struggles into **proxy wars**: Vietnam's fight against France became a Cold War battleground once it split into communist and anti-communist halves (8.3–8.4); Cold War intervention (US and Belgian involvement in Lumumba's assassination, the US-backed coups in Iran and Chile) repeatedly destabilized newly independent or newly assertive governments (8.5–8.7).
- **Decolonization itself, in turn, reshaped the Cold War**: dozens of new states became prizes each superpower courted with aid, arms, and covert action, while many chose the **Non-Aligned Movement** instead (8.3) — refusing to let either bloc dictate their development. Newly independent states' economic choices (nationalization, import substitution) frequently provoked the very superpower interventions that then radicalized them further, as with Cuba (8.4) and Iran (8.6–8.7).
- The costs of managing this global competition — most directly the **Soviet–Afghan War**, fought to prop up a client state born of Cold War-era intervention — became one of the direct causes of the **Soviet economic collapse** that ended the Cold War (8.8).
- The **borders and institutions** colonial powers left behind (8.1, 8.6) — not the Cold War alone — also explain why instability (Rwanda, the Congo, India–Pakistan, Israel–Palestine) persisted well after 1991, showing that decolonization's consequences outlasted the Cold War that shaped so much of it.

> **Big Picture:** Trace any single event in this unit back far enough and it connects to both halves of the unit's title — the Cold War and decolonization did not just happen at the same time, they continuously caused and reshaped each other.
