# Unit 6: Consequences of Industrialization (1750–1900 CE)

Industrialization armed a handful of Western states (plus Japan) with the guns, steamships, and capital to dominate the rest of the globe. **Nationalism**, **Social Darwinism**, and the **civilizing mission** supplied the justification; the **Berlin Conference (1884–85)** carved up Africa with zero African input. Colonized regions were forced into **export economies** producing raw materials for the industrial core, while millions of people — pushed by famine and demographic pressure, pulled by cheap steamship fares — migrated across oceans as free, semi-coerced, and indentured labor. Resistance took every form, from armed revolt to new states to religious movements, but industrial weaponry usually won.

---

## 6.1 — Rationales for Imperialism from 1750 to 1900

### Nationalism
- Belief in one's own national greatness → desire for territory as proof of national strength → intense competition among European states to build the largest empire (a major driver of the **Scramble for Africa**, 6.2).

### Social Darwinism
- A misapplication of Charles Darwin's "survival of the fittest" (a biological theory) onto human societies and races.
- Produced **"scientific racism"**: pseudo-scientific claims that non-Western peoples were biologically or culturally like children who hadn't matured, and were therefore naturally subordinate.
- Industrialized states pointed to their own wealth and military power as "proof" they were the fittest — and therefore entitled to rule "unfit" societies.

### The Civilizing Mission
- Imperial powers claimed a moral **duty** to develop and uplift the peoples they conquered — this duty was popularized in English as the **"White Man's Burden"** (from Rudyard Kipling's 1899 poem of the same name).
- In practice: forced conversion to **Christianity**, western-style education, and suppression of local customs, languages, and religions.
- Example: US and Canadian boarding schools for Native children, built on the philosophy "kill the Indian in him to save the man" — forced assimilation stripped children of language and culture.

### Religious Conversion
- Missionary societies (many Protestant and Catholic) fanned out across Africa, Asia, and the Pacific with genuine — if paternalistic — zeal to convert local populations.
- Missionaries often built schools and hospitals alongside churches, which spread Western literacy and medicine but also functioned as a soft tool of cultural control, paving the way for formal colonization.

> **Big Picture:** These four rationales overlapped and reinforced each other — a missionary's conversion work, a settler's claim to "unused" land, and a general's claim to a "civilizing" war were all justified by the same underlying belief in Western superiority. On the exam, be ready to explain *how* an ideology (not just *that* it existed) made empire seem justified.

## 6.2 — State Expansion from 1750 to 1900

Industrial states expanded through several distinct methods — formal colonization, settler replacement, and the creation of new empires by previously non-imperial or newly industrializing powers.

### The Berlin Conference (1884–1885)
- Called by German chancellor **Otto von Bismarck** as European powers raced to claim African territory before rivals did.
- European delegates — with **no African representatives present** — drew borders across the continent using rulers and maps, largely ignoring existing ethnic, linguistic, and political boundaries.
- Result: rival groups were combined inside single colonies, while unified peoples were split across colonial borders — a structural cause of many post-independence conflicts in the 20th century.
- Companies chartered by European governments (e.g., the British **Royal Niger Company**) used vague "fill-in-the-blank" contracts, often misrepresented to or misunderstood by local chiefs, to legally claim land.

### The Boer Wars (South Africa)
- Britain fought the **Boers** (descendants of Dutch settlers, also called Afrikaners) for control of South Africa's territory and its diamond and gold wealth.
- Britain's "scorched earth" tactics drove both Boer and Black South African civilians into **concentration camps**, where disease and malnutrition caused a high death toll.

### Settler Colonies
- **Australia and New Zealand**: British settlers arrived in large numbers seeking farmland; imported Old World diseases devastated Aboriginal Australian and Māori populations, echoing the demographic collapse of Native Americans after 1492.

### The United States
- **Manifest Destiny**: the belief that US westward expansion across North America was divinely ordained; indigenous nations were pushed onto **reservations** (see 6.3).
- **Spanish-American War (1898)**: the US defeated Spain and took control of the **Philippines**, Puerto Rico, and Guam — extending US imperial reach into Southeast Asia and the Caribbean.

### Russia
- Expanded overland across **Siberia** to the Pacific Ocean, and added territory in the Caucasus and Central Asia.
- **Pan-Slavism**: the ideology that Russia had a duty to unite and protect Slavic peoples, used to justify expansion into the Balkans and conflict with the Ottoman Empire.

### Japan
- The **Meiji Restoration** (from 1868) rapidly industrialized Japan, which then used its new military and industrial strength to become an imperial power itself — expanding influence over **Korea**, **Manchuria**, and parts of China.
- Japan is the clearest example of a non-Western state adopting the same industrialization → imperialism pattern as Europe and the US (a strong comparison point for the exam).

### The Congo Free State — A Special Case
- **King Leopold II of Belgium** personally claimed the Congo as his own private property (not a Belgian colony) and ran it as a forced-labor extraction operation for rubber, using mutilation and killing to enforce rubber quotas.
- International outcry over the atrocities eventually forced the Belgian government to take formal control away from Leopold in 1908.

| Method of Expansion | Example |
|---|---|
| Formal territorial conquest | Berlin Conference partition of Africa |
| Settler replacement | Australia, New Zealand, US Manifest Destiny |
| Overland contiguous expansion | Russia across Siberia |
| Industrializing-then-imperial power | Japan (Meiji Restoration) |
| Personal/private colonial rule | Congo Free State under Leopold II |

## 6.3 — Indigenous Responses to State Expansion from 1750 to 1900

### Causes of Growing Resistance
- The **civilizing mission backfired**: Western-style education, meant to produce loyal, "civilized" colonial subjects, instead taught colonized elites Enlightenment ideas like **popular sovereignty** and **natural rights** — ideas colonized peoples then turned against their colonizers.
- European critics of empire also emerged: novelist **Joseph Conrad** (*Heart of Darkness*), economist **J.A. Hobson**, and **Karl Marx** all published critiques of imperialism's brutality and economic exploitation, feeding a broader anti-imperial intellectual current.

### Direct Armed Resistance
- **Yaa Asantewaa War (1900)**, also called the **War of the Golden Stool**: the Asante Kingdom (in present-day Ghana) fought Britain after the British demanded the Golden Stool, the sacred symbol of Asante sovereignty. **Queen Mother Yaa Asantewaa** rallied and led Asante forces. Britain's industrial weaponry ultimately won, but the resistance was fierce and became a lasting symbol of anti-colonial defiance.

### Creating New States at the Periphery
- **Cherokee Nation**: forced by the US government to relocate from the southeastern US to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma) along the **Trail of Tears**; the Cherokee organized their own constitutional government in exile and worked to preserve their language and culture.
- **Zulu Kingdom**: built a powerful, centralized military state on the edge of British-controlled South Africa and successfully resisted British invasion for a time (notably defeating the British at the **Battle of Isandlwana**, 1879) before eventually being defeated.

### Religious Resistance
- **Xhosa Cattle-Killing Movement (1856–57)**: after losing land and cattle to British encroachment, a young Xhosa prophetess claimed that killing all Xhosa cattle and destroying crops would cause ancestral spirits to rise, drive out the British, and replenish the herds. Hundreds of thousands of cattle were killed — but the promised deliverance never came, and the resulting famine killed tens of thousands of Xhosa and left the survivors dependent on British colonial authority.

| Type of Resistance | Example |
|---|---|
| Armed revolt | Yaa Asantewaa War (Asante vs. Britain) |
| Formation of a rival/refuge state | Cherokee Nation, Zulu Kingdom |
| Religious/prophetic movement | Xhosa Cattle-Killing Movement |
| Intellectual/political critique | Hobson, Marx, Conrad |

> **Common Mistake:** Not all resistance was armed, and not all resistance succeeded. The AP exam rewards recognizing *multiple forms* of resistance and their *mixed outcomes* — most direct resistance movements were eventually crushed by industrial military technology, even when they inflicted real damage first.

## 6.4 — Global Economic Development from 1750 to 1900

### From Mercantilism to Industrial Capitalism
- Industrializing states shifted their *domestic* economic philosophy from **mercantilism** (state-controlled trade aimed at accumulating gold/silver) toward **laissez-faire capitalism** and **free trade**, influenced by economist **Adam Smith**'s idea of the market's "invisible hand" — that individuals pursuing self-interest in a free market would collectively produce efficient, beneficial outcomes.
- **Industrial capitalism**: private individuals and corporations, not the state, owned the factories, machines, and capital that drove production — and captured most of the resulting profit.

### Global Economic Integration
- New transportation and communication technology tied the world's economies together into a single interdependent system for the first time in history:

| Technology | Effect |
|---|---|
| Steamships | Faster, more reliable ocean shipping of bulk raw materials |
| Railroads | Moved raw materials from interior regions to coastal ports for export |
| Telegraph (submarine cables from the 1850s–60s) | Near-instant communication let merchants and financiers coordinate prices and shipments across continents |
| Suez Canal (opened 1869) | Cut the sea route between Europe and Asia dramatically, boosting trade with India, East Africa, and East Asia |

- This **global economic integration** meant a famine, price shock, or war in one region could now ripple through markets on the other side of the planet.

### Export Economies as a Global Pattern
- Across colonized and semi-colonized regions, economies were restructured around exporting raw, unprocessed **commodities** to industrial cores, then importing finished manufactured goods in return — a continuity from the mercantilist colonial economies of c. 1450–1750, but now organized at a much larger, more industrial scale.
- Financing for this system came increasingly from Western **banks and joint-stock investment**, which funded railroads, mines, and plantations abroad in exchange for a share of profits — deepening colonized economies' financial dependency on the industrial core.

> **Key Irony:** Industrialized nations preached free-market capitalism and free trade at home, while imposing tightly controlled, mercantilist-style trade relationships on their colonies — banning colonial industries that competed with the home country and forcing colonies to buy the parent country's finished goods.

## 6.5 — Economic Imperialism from 1750 to 1900

**Definition**: Extending control over a state or region through **economic means** — trade, debt, unequal treaties, or corporate control of land and infrastructure — rather than outright military conquest or formal annexation.

### Unequal Treaties: Britain and China
- Britain ran a trade deficit with China: British consumers wanted Chinese tea, silk, and porcelain, but China would accept only silver in payment, not British manufactured goods.
- Britain's solution: smuggle **opium** grown in British India into China to reverse the trade imbalance. When the Qing government banned and confiscated the opium, Britain declared the **First Opium War (1839–42)**.
- Result: the **Treaty of Nanjing (1842)** — the first of many "unequal treaties" — forced China to open additional ports to British trade, cede Hong Kong, and grant British citizens **extraterritoriality** (immunity from Chinese law).
- A **Second Opium War** and the internal **Taiping Rebellion** further weakened Qing authority, leading Western powers, Japan, and Russia to carve China into **spheres of influence** — zones of exclusive economic privilege — without ever formally colonizing the country.

### Corporate Economic Imperialism: United Fruit Company
- The US-based **United Fruit Company** built railroads and ports across Central America in exchange for control of vast tracts of land for **banana production**.
- Fruit companies came to hold more effective economic power over these "banana republics" than the nominal national governments did — a textbook case of economic control substituting for direct military occupation.

### Coerced Cash-Crop Economies
- **India and Egypt**: both were forced into growing **cotton for export** to British textile mills. Britain simultaneously banned the import of finished Indian cotton textiles into Britain, deliberately destroying India's own textile manufacturing base and eliminating it as competition.
- **West Africa**: forced/incentivized production of **palm oil**, used to lubricate industrial machinery in Europe.
- **Congo Free State**: rubber extraction under Leopold II (6.2) is also a case of economic imperialism, since the coercive labor system existed to feed European industrial demand for rubber (tires, insulated wire), not to build a self-sustaining local economy.

> **6.4 vs. 6.5, the key distinction:** 6.4 describes the *system* — a newly integrated, capitalist global economy built on export production. 6.5 describes the *specific mechanisms* imperial powers used to force individual regions into that system: unequal treaties, corporate land grabs, and coerced cash-crop production.

## 6.6 — Causes of Migration in an Interconnected World

### Push and Pull Factors
- **Demographic pressure**: rapid population growth in industrializing Europe outpaced rural job availability, pushing people to emigrate.
- **Famines**: the **Irish Potato Famine (1840s)** killed roughly a million people and drove millions more to emigrate, most to the United States.
- **Technology**: railroads and steamships made long-distance migration dramatically cheaper and faster than sailing ships had, and — unlike most migration in earlier centuries — made **return migration** common, since migrants could realistically travel home again.

### Coerced and Semi-Coerced Labor Migration
- Britain **abolished slavery** across its empire in 1833, creating a sudden shortage of plantation labor in the Caribbean, Mauritius, and Fiji just as global demand for sugar and other cash crops kept rising.
- The solution imperial planters turned to was **indentured servitude**: workers (overwhelmingly from India and China) signed labor contracts — often exploitative and misunderstood, since many workers were illiterate or the contracts were misrepresented — binding them to years of plantation labor across the British Empire under conditions resembling slavery in all but name.
- **Convict labor**: Britain and France transported convicted criminals to **penal colonies** (Australia, French Guiana) for forced hard labor, another semi-coerced form of migration tied to imperial expansion.

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Voluntary** | Migrants chose to leave for economic opportunity, e.g., Irish and other Europeans to the US |
| **Indentured Servitude** | Indian and Chinese workers moved across the British Empire under exploitative labor contracts to replace enslaved labor after abolition |
| **Convict Labor** | Britain and France sent convicts to penal colonies (Australia, French Guiana) |

## 6.7 — Effects of Migration

### Effects on Societies People Left
- Migration was disproportionately male, producing a **gender imbalance** in sending communities — women took on traditionally male economic and household roles in migrants' absence.
- **Remittances** — money migrants sent home — became a major economic lifeline for sending families and regions, sometimes reducing the need for women and children to work and allowing children to stay in school longer.

### Effects on Receiving Societies
- **Ethnic enclaves**: immigrant communities such as Chinatowns and Little Italys formed in receiving cities, preserving migrants' home language, food, and customs while gradually blending with the surrounding culture.
- **Diaspora communities**: large-scale migration created durable, transnational networks of people who retained ties to their homeland even while permanently settled abroad — reshaping demographics in port cities, plantation zones, and industrial districts worldwide.
- **Labor organizing**: migrant workers, including Irish immigrants in the US, played a major role in forming early **labor unions** to fight for better wages and working conditions.
- **Nativism**: native-born workers, competing with immigrants for jobs, fueled backlash rooted partly in economic anxiety and partly in Social Darwinist racial prejudice (6.1).
  - United States: the **Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** banned Chinese immigration outright — the first major US law to restrict immigration by nationality.
  - Australia: the **White Australia Policy** (emerging in this era and formalized in 1901) restricted non-European immigration.

> **Big Picture:** Migration's effects ran in both directions across an ocean at once — a single migrant's move could simultaneously reshape gender roles at home (through remittances and absence) and provoke nativist backlash abroad (through labor competition).

## 6.8 — Causation in the Imperial Age

This unit's modules form one continuous cause-and-effect chain — a favorite structure for AP long-essay and DBQ prompts.

1. **Industrialization** (Unit 5) creates a hunger for cheap raw materials and new consumer markets that Europe, the US, and Japan cannot supply domestically.
2. **Ideology** (6.1) — nationalism, Social Darwinism, the civilizing mission, and religious conversion — supplies the moral and political justification for taking territory to meet that hunger.
3. **State expansion** (6.2) follows, through direct conquest, settler replacement, or (for Japan) industrializing fast enough to join the imperial powers rather than be conquered by them.
4. **Indigenous resistance** (6.3) responds in every conquered region — armed revolt, new refuge states, religious revitalization movements, and intellectual critique — but is generally overwhelmed by industrial military technology.
5. **Global economic restructuring** (6.4–6.5) locks colonized regions into export economies through unequal treaties, corporate land control, and coerced cash-crop production, integrating the whole world into one capitalist system centered on the industrial core.
6. **Migration** (6.6) follows the same economic logic: abolition of slavery creates a labor gap filled by indentured servitude, while demographic pressure and famine push millions of voluntary migrants toward industrializing economies that need workers.
7. **Migration's effects** (6.7) — remittances, diaspora communities, ethnic enclaves, and nativist backlash — permanently reshape the societies migrants left and the societies that received them.

> **Big Picture:** Every module in Unit 6 is a link in the same chain: industrial demand → imperial ideology → conquest → resistance → economic restructuring → migration → new demographic and cultural realities. When an essay prompt asks about a "cause" or "effect" in this unit, it is almost always asking you to isolate one link of this larger chain and explain how it connects to the links on either side of it.
