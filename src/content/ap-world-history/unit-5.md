# Unit 5: Revolutions (c. 1750 to c. 1900)

---

The Enlightenment gave people the intellectual tools — **natural rights, social contract, popular sovereignty** — to challenge monarchs, sparking a chain of **Atlantic Revolutions** (American, French, Haitian, Latin American) and, later in the century, **nationalist unification movements** in Italy and Germany. At the same time, Britain's unique combination of resources, capital, and geography triggered the **Industrial Revolution**, which spread unevenly across the world (fastest where governments actively drove it, as in Germany and Japan) and remade economies, class structures, and daily life. That transformation provoked fierce reactions — labor unions, Marxism, and defensive industrialization in Qing China and the Ottoman Empire — while leaving older hierarchies like **patriarchy, colonialism, and class inequality** largely intact.

## 5.1 — The Enlightenment

### What It Was
- Applied the rational, empirical methods of the **Scientific Revolution** to human society, government, and morality — if reason could uncover the laws of physics, it could also uncover the "natural laws" of politics and justice.
- Centered in **salons, coffeehouses, and printed pamphlets** across Western Europe (especially France, Britain, and their colonies) during the 18th century; spread rapidly thanks to the printing press and rising literacy.

### Core Enlightenment Ideas (all challenged monarchy and the status quo)
- **Natural rights**: All humans are born with inherent rights — life, liberty, and property (**John Locke**).
- **Social contract**: Government is an agreement between rulers and the ruled; a government that violates that trust (tyranny) can legitimately be **overthrown**.
- **Popular sovereignty**: Ultimate political authority belongs to **the people**, not to a monarch's "divine right."
- **Separation of powers**: Political power should be divided among branches to prevent tyranny (**Montesquieu**).
- **Democracy / representative government**: People should participate in government, directly or through elected representatives.
- **Liberalism**: Civil rights, representative government, private property, and free-market trade — a direct challenge to mercantilism and absolutism.

> [!note] Key Enlightenment Thinkers
> **John Locke** (natural rights, social contract) · **Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (popular sovereignty, *The Social Contract*) · **Baron de Montesquieu** (separation of powers) · **Adam Smith** (free-market capitalism, *The Wealth of Nations*, 1776 — the intellectual seed of 5.6 and 5.7 below).

### Effects
- **Expanding suffrage**: Enlightenment arguments for popular sovereignty fed directly into demands for a wider voting franchise after the American and French Revolutions.
- **Feminism**: **Olympe de Gouges** wrote the *Declaration of the Rights of Woman and the Female Citizen* (1791), pointing out that the French Revolution's promises of equality had excluded women.
- **Abolitionism**: Natural-rights arguments ("all humans are born free") fueled movements to end slavery in the Americas and serfdom in Russia (serfs freed 1861).

## 5.2 — Nationalism and Revolutions from 1750 to 1900

### Nationalism
- **Definition**: A shared identity built on common language, religion, customs, history, and — often — a common enemy, combined with a vision for self-government.
- **How it was used**: Leaders invoked nationalism to launch revolutions, added nationalist content to school curricula, and pushed for linguistic/cultural unity to bind fragmented peoples together.
- **Non-Atlantic example**: **Muhammad Ali** in Egypt exploited discontent with Ottoman corruption to build independent textile and weapons factories — nationalist-flavored resistance to imperial rule outside the Atlantic world.

### The Atlantic Revolutions
All four were connected by Enlightenment ideals of natural rights and popular sovereignty, and each influenced the next.

| Revolution | Cause | Key Document | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **American** (1775–1783) | 13 colonies resented British taxation and rule without representation | ***Declaration of Independence*** (1776) — social contract + popular sovereignty | New democratic republic; became a model that inspired the French and Latin American revolutions |
| **French** (1789–1799) | Financial crisis, food shortages, and resentment of absolutist monarchy and rigid social estates | ***Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen*** (1789) — rights of French men, transparent republican government | Monarchy overthrown; radical phase (Terror) followed by Napoleon; spread revolutionary ideals across Europe by conquest |
| **Haitian** (1791–1804) | Enslaved population in French colony Saint-Domingue absorbed French Revolutionary ideals of liberty and equality | No single founding document; leadership under **Toussaint Louverture**, later Jean-Jacques Dessalines | Defeated Napoleon's army; created the **first Black-led republic** in the Western Hemisphere; the **only fully successful large-scale slave rebellion** in world history |
| **Latin American** (1808–1826) | Enlightenment ideas + long-standing resentment of Spanish mercantilist control; triggered when **Napoleon invaded Spain (1808)** and destabilized colonial authority | ***Letter from Jamaica*** (1815) by **Simón Bolívar** — called for regional unity and criticized Spanish mercantilism | Long, bloody wars of independence; birth of numerous new republics across South and Central America |

> [!important] The Three Documents You Must Know
> ***Declaration of Independence*** (US, 1776) · ***Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen*** (France, 1789) · ***Letter from Jamaica*** (Bolívar, 1815). These are the AP's favorite document-based comparisons for this topic.

**Philippine Propaganda Movement** (late 19th century) — not itself a revolution: Filipino students who studied in Europe absorbed Enlightenment ideas and published pamphlets demanding political participation and reform from Spain. This intellectual movement laid the groundwork for the armed **Philippine Revolution** (1896).

### Nationalist Unification Movements
Nationalism didn't only produce revolutions against existing states — it also **fused fragmented states together** around shared language and identity.

- **Italy**: A patchwork of small kingdoms and city-states (plus Austrian-controlled territory in the north) unified through a mix of diplomacy and war, led by the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia (Count Cavour) and the guerrilla campaigns of **Giuseppe Garibaldi**; unification completed by 1871.
- **Germany**: 39 fragmented German states unified under Prussian leadership. Chancellor **Otto von Bismarck** used a deliberate strategy of "blood and iron" — three short wars (against Denmark, Austria, and France) — to rally German nationalism around Prussia, culminating in the proclamation of the **German Empire** in 1871.

> [!tip] Why This Matters for 5.6–5.7
> A newly unified, nationalist Germany moved immediately to catch up industrially with Britain using **state-directed protectionism** — the direct link between this topic and Germany's approach to industrialization below.

## 5.3 — Industrial Revolution Begins

### Why Britain Industrialized First
Britain enjoyed a unique convergence of preconditions no other country matched in the mid-1700s:

1. **Waterways**: An island nation criss-crossed by navigable rivers and canals → cheap, efficient internal transport for raw materials and goods.
2. **Natural resources**: Rich domestic deposits of **coal, iron ore, and timber** sat close together geographically, minimizing transport costs.
3. **Access to foreign resources**: Colonial control of India gave Britain a steady, cheap supply of raw **cotton** for its textile industry — the sector that industrialized first.
4. **Agricultural improvements**: The prior **Agricultural Revolution** (crop rotation, selective breeding, enclosure) raised food yields, freeing up labor from farming and supporting a growing population.
5. **Urbanization and labor supply**: Displaced rural workers (many pushed off land by enclosure) migrated to cities seeking factory wages, creating a large, mobile labor pool.
6. **Legal protection of private property**: Stable property law and a functioning patent system made entrepreneurial risk-taking (and reinvesting profit) safe and worthwhile.
7. **Accumulated capital**: Profits from the Atlantic slave trade, colonial trade, and a mature banking/credit system provided the startup capital for factories and machinery.

### How Production Changed
- **Factory system**: Concentrated production under one roof with machinery → mass production → falling per-unit costs, replacing small-scale artisan/cottage production.
- **Power source shift**: Early factories were **water-powered** (built beside rivers); **James Watt's improved steam engine** (patented 1769, widely adopted by the 1780s–90s) freed factories from riverside locations and could be sited near coal and cities.
- **Division of labor**: Work was broken into narrow, repetitive, specialized tasks rather than one worker crafting a whole product — faster output, but deskilled and monotonous labor.
- **Textiles led the way**: Inventions like the **spinning jenny** (Hargreaves), **water frame** (Arkwright), and **power loom** (Cartwright) mechanized British cotton spinning and weaving first, before industrialization spread to other sectors.

> [!warning] A Global Cost
> As British textile output surged, regions that had long dominated textile production for a global market — **India** and **Egypt** — saw their share of world manufacturing collapse, partly through deliberate British trade policy (tariffs favoring British cloth, suppression of Indian competition).

## 5.4 — Industrialization Spreads in the 19th Century

Britain guarded its industrial technology jealously (banning the export of machinery and skilled emigration for a time), but industrialization diffused outward over the 19th century — with very different levels of government involvement and success.

| Country / Region | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Belgium / France** | Belgium industrialized quickly (coal, iron, close to Britain); **France** was slower — it lacked easily accessible coal and iron, so the government took a more active role, sponsoring railroad and canal construction to compensate. |
| **United States** | Rich natural resources, political stability, and (after the Civil War, 1861–65, which spurred rail and manufacturing growth) rapid industrial expansion; combined with high wages relative to Europe, the US developed arguably the **highest average worker standard of living** among industrializing nations by 1900. |
| **Russia** | Heavily **state-driven** industrialization under the tsarist government, notably Finance Minister **Sergei Witte** in the 1890s; the **Trans-Siberian Railroad** (begun 1891) symbolized state-led development. Conditions for workers were brutal, sparking frequent strikes and uprisings — a direct contributor to the 1905 Revolution. |
| **Japan (Meiji Restoration, from 1868)** | The most successful case of **defensive, state-sponsored industrialization** in the world: the Meiji government sent officials abroad to study Western industry, built model factories (textiles, shipbuilding, arms) directly with state funds, then sold many to politically connected business conglomerates (**zaibatsu**, e.g., Mitsubishi), while also building railroads and a Western-style conscript army. |

### Compare: Successful vs. Struggling Defensive Industrialization
Not every state under Western pressure to modernize succeeded like Japan — two well-known contrasts:

- **Qing China — Self-Strengthening Movement** (1861–1895): Adopted Western military technology and some industry ("Chinese learning as the base, Western learning for practical use") while trying to preserve traditional Confucian social and political order. Conservative court factions resisted deeper reform, funding was inconsistent and often diverted (famously to rebuild the Empress Dowager Cixi's Summer Palace), and the effort proved too shallow — decisively exposed by China's defeat in the **First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95)**.
- **Ottoman Empire — Tanzimat Reforms** (1839–1876): Built factories and railroads, adopted Western-style law codes, and briefly issued a constitution (1876) with an elected parliament. Conservative and religious opposition pushed back, and Sultan Abdul Hamid II suspended the constitution in 1878 and reasserted **absolute monarchy**, cutting the reform movement short.

> [!important] Why Japan Succeeded Where China and the Ottomans Struggled
> Japan combined centralized state authority, a unifying nationalist goal (avoid the fate of China/India), and a political elite (former samurai reformers) willing to dismantle the old feudal order entirely — rather than trying to graft Western technology onto an unchanged traditional system.

## 5.5 — Technology of the Industrial Age

### First Industrial Revolution (roughly 1750s–1850s)
- **Power**: Coal-fired **steam engines** (James Watt) drove factory machinery, **locomotives**, and **steamships** — the first time power wasn't tied to human/animal muscle, water, or wind.
- **Building material**: **Iron** — bridges, machine frames, early rail track.
- **Transportation**: The first practical **steam locomotive** railways opened in Britain in the 1820s–30s, collapsing travel times and freight costs.

### Second Industrial Revolution (roughly 1870s–1900s)
- **Power**: **Oil** and the **internal combustion engine**; **electricity**, popularized by **Thomas Edison's** practical incandescent lightbulb (1879) → powered electric streetcars and subways, extending the industrial workday into the night.
- **Communication**: The **telegraph**, using **Morse Code**, allowed near-instant long-distance communication for the first time — critical for coordinating railroads, markets, and empires. Transoceanic cables (from the 1860s) linked continents.
- **Building material**: **Steel**, made affordable at scale by the **Bessemer process** (patented 1856) — stronger and more versatile than iron, enabling skyscrapers, stronger rail, and larger ships.
- **Maritime / global trade**: Iron-and-steel steamships plus the opening of the **Suez Canal** (1869) dramatically shortened shipping routes between Europe and Asia, accelerating global trade and imperial reach.

> [!note] First vs. Second, in One Line
> First Industrial Revolution = **coal, steam, iron, textiles**. Second Industrial Revolution = **oil, electricity, steel, instant communication** — and it is the Second wave that made industrial economies look recognizably modern.

## 5.6 — Industrialization: Government's Role

Governments took sharply different approaches to industrialization, ranging from hands-off to fully state-directed — and the AP loves to test this contrast.

### Britain: "Laissez-Faire" — But Selectively
- Influenced by **Adam Smith's** *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Britain's government publicly favored **laissez-faire** — minimal interference in markets, letting competition and self-interest allocate resources.
- In practice, Britain wasn't hands-off: it enforced contracts and property rights, strengthened **patent law** (1852) to protect inventors' profits, and funded some infrastructure.
- Britain's clearest free-trade action was repealing the **Corn Laws** (1846), tariffs that had kept grain prices (and bread prices) artificially high — a landmark shift toward free trade, though Britain kept protections for shipping and manufacturing (Navigation Acts) longer.

### Germany, Japan, Russia, and the US: State-Guided "Catch-Up" Industrialization
Later industrializers used the state deliberately to close the gap with Britain — the opposite of laissez-faire:

| Country | Government Strategy |
|---|---|
| **Germany** | Bismarck imposed **protective tariffs** on foreign industrial goods to shelter young German steel, chemical, and manufacturing firms from British competition — a classic "infant industry" strategy. |
| **Japan** | The Meiji state directly built and financed model factories, railroads, and a modern banking system before privatizing much of it to the *zaibatsu* (see 5.4). |
| **Russia** | The tsarist state financed and directed major projects like the Trans-Siberian Railroad and used high tariffs to protect fledgling Russian industry. |
| **United States** | Kept high **protective tariffs** for much of the 19th century (favored by the Republican Party) and granted vast public land to railroad companies to spur construction. |

### Government Reacting to Industrialization's Fallout
As industrial abuses became impossible to ignore, some governments began regulating the very markets they had unleashed:

- **Britain's Factory Acts**: The **Factory Act of 1833** banned employing children under 9, capped hours for children and teenagers, and created a factory inspectorate to enforce it; the **Factory Act of 1847** ("Ten Hours Act") capped the workday for women and young workers at 10 hours.
- **Expanding suffrage**: Britain's Reform Acts of 1832 and 1867 gradually widened voting rights to more of the middle and working classes.
- **Bismarck's welfare state**: Facing a rising socialist movement, Bismarck combined repression of socialist organizing with the world's first modern **social insurance system** — health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884), and old-age/disability pensions (1889) — a calculated move to win worker loyalty to the state rather than to socialism (see 5.8).

## 5.7 — Economic Developments and Innovations in the Industrial Age

### From Mercantilism to Industrial Capitalism
- Pre-industrial European economies were shaped by **mercantilism**: colonies existed to enrich the mother country through controlled trade and accumulated gold/silver.
- Industrialization, paired with Adam Smith's free-market ideas, pushed Western economies toward **industrial capitalism**: private ownership of the means of production, competitive markets, and profit reinvested into growth.

### New Financial Institutions
- **Joint-stock companies**: Spread the financial risk of large ventures across many investors, each owning **shares**; no single investor bore the whole loss if the venture failed.
- **Limited liability corporations**: Chartered by governments as legal entities separate from their owners — shareholders could lose only what they invested, not their entire personal wealth, which encouraged much bolder investment in risky industrial ventures.
- **Stock exchanges** (London's grew especially fast): Let shares be bought and sold, putting a live market value on companies and making industrial investment liquid and attractive to a much wider pool of capital.
- **Transnational banks and firms**: Institutions like the **Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation (HSBC)** and manufacturers like **Unilever** operated across borders, financing and supplying industry on a global scale.

### Concentration of Economic Power
- **Monopolies, cartels, and trusts**: As industries matured, dominant firms bought out or crushed competitors to control entire markets and set prices — e.g., **Standard Oil** in the United States under John D. Rockefeller.
- **Commodity chains went global**: Raw materials (cotton, rubber, palm oil) flowed from colonies and peripheral economies to industrial cores, were manufactured into finished goods, and were sold back — deepening economic dependency between industrialized and non-industrialized regions.

### Effects
- Falling production costs made consumer goods **cheaper, more varied, and more widely available** than ever before — the beginning of modern mass consumer culture (department stores emerged in this era).
- These same forces produced **sharp inequality**: enormous profits for owners and investors alongside poverty wages and harsh conditions for factory labor — the direct fuel for the reactions covered in 5.8.

## 5.8 — Reactions to the Industrial Economy from 1750 to 1900

### Working-Class Conditions
- Factory workers, including large numbers of women and children, lived crowded into **tenements** near factories; disease spread easily in these unsanitary conditions.
- Work was long (often 12–16 hour days before reform laws), dangerous (unguarded machinery), and low-paid.
- Public health surveys of the period (e.g., Edwin Chadwick's 1842 sanitary report) documented that life expectancy in the worst industrial cities like Manchester fell well below the rural/national average — grim evidence used by reformers to push for the Factory Acts and public health laws covered in 5.6.

### Working-Class Self-Help
- **Mutual aid / friendly societies**: Workers pooled dues to provide members with insurance against sickness, injury, or death — an early, voluntary safety net before state welfare existed.
- **Labor unions**: Organized workers to bargain collectively with employers for better wages, safer conditions, and shorter hours; often illegal or heavily restricted early on, unions gradually won legal recognition (Britain legalized unions in 1871).
- **Strikes**: A key tool of union pressure — increasingly common (and increasingly met with government or employer force) as industrialization matured.

### Socialism and Marxism
- **Utopian socialism** (earlier and more idealistic): Reformers like **Robert Owen** believed model communities with humane working conditions could prove capitalism could be made fair without revolution.
- **Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels**, ***The Communist Manifesto*** (1848): Argued industrial capitalism was inherently exploitative — the **proletariat** (working class) would inevitably rise up, overthrow the **bourgeoisie** (capitalist owners), and seize the **means of production**, producing a classless society. Marx called this **scientific socialism**, framing it as an inevitable historical process rather than a moral wish.
- **Socialism** is the broad umbrella term (economic system with some form of collective/social ownership); **communism**, in Marx's usage, is the specific strand that requires **violent revolution** to overthrow capitalism.
- **Anarchism**: A more radical current, rejecting the state itself (not just capitalism) as inherently oppressive; anarchists sometimes clashed violently with both governments and Marxist socialists over strategy.

> [!important] Cause and Effect: Marxism ↔ Government Reform
> Marxism's growing appeal among European workers is exactly what pushed governments like Bismarck's Germany (5.6) to combine repression of socialist parties with genuine welfare reforms — an attempt to defuse revolutionary pressure by making capitalism more bearable rather than waiting to be overthrown by it.

## 5.9 — Society and the Industrial Age

### A New Class Structure
- Before industrialization, social status mostly tracked **land ownership and noble birth**. Industrialization shifted the basis of status and power to **who controlled capital, factories, and machines**.
- **Industrialists / capitalist elite**: Factory and business owners who accumulated enormous new wealth.
- **Middle class**: A newly significant group of managers, professionals, shopkeepers, and smaller business owners — grew rapidly in size and political influence.
- **Working class (proletariat)**: Wage laborers dependent on factory work, with little control over conditions or pay — the group Marx (5.8) argued would eventually revolt.

### Urbanization
- Millions of people migrated from the countryside to fast-growing industrial cities in search of factory jobs — cities like Manchester and Chicago exploded in population within a few decades.
- Rapid, unplanned growth produced severe urban problems: overcrowding, pollution, inadequate sanitation, and repeated epidemics (e.g., cholera outbreaks tied directly to contaminated water supplies) — problems that eventually forced the public health and infrastructure reforms noted in 5.6.

### Family Structure and Gender Roles
- Before industrialization, the family was typically a **self-sufficient economic unit** working together (farm or home workshop). Industrialization moved paid production **outside the home**, into factories — reshaping family life around a wage-earning schedule.
- **Cult of domesticity**: A middle-class ideal that idealized women as the moral guardians of a separate, private home sphere, largely excluded from paid work, politics, and most professions.
- **Working-class reality diverged sharply**: poor and working-class women (and children) worked in factories and mines out of economic necessity — the "separate spheres" ideal was a middle-class luxury, not a universal experience.
- Early feminist voices (building on 5.1's Olympe de Gouges) began directly challenging both political exclusion and the domestic ideal in this period, though full suffrage for women would not arrive until the 20th century almost everywhere.

> [!note] A Testable Contrast
> The AP loves pairing "cult of domesticity" (middle-class ideal) against the lived reality of working-class women and children in factories — two very different gendered experiences of the *same* industrial economy.

## 5.10 — Continuity and Change in the Industrial Age

### What Genuinely Changed (c. 1750–1900)
- **Politics**: Monarchical absolutism gave way, in many places, to constitutional and republican governments built on Enlightenment ideas of popular sovereignty (5.1–5.2).
- **Economic structure**: Mercantilism gave way to industrial and corporate capitalism (5.6–5.7); production shifted from artisan workshops and farms to mechanized factories.
- **Social structure**: A new urban middle class and industrial working class displaced the old hierarchy of nobility vs. peasantry as the dominant social division (5.9).
- **Global connectivity**: Railroads, steamships, and the telegraph collapsed travel and communication times, binding the world into a single, faster-moving economic system (5.5, 5.7) — and setting the stage directly for the New Imperialism of the next unit.

### What Stayed the Same
- **Patriarchy**: Despite early feminist critiques (5.1, 5.9), men retained political and legal dominance almost everywhere; women gained little formal political power in this period.
- **Racial and colonial hierarchies**: Industrial powers used their new technological and military edge to deepen, not dismantle, colonial control and racial hierarchy — industrialization intensified imperialism rather than ending it.
- **Class inequality**: The specific hierarchy changed (nobility → capitalists), but a small elite still controlled most wealth and power while a much larger laboring population did not — inequality persisted in new form, not disappeared.

> [!important] Exam Framing
> When asked to assess the *extent* of change across this unit, the strongest answers argue that change was most dramatic in **technology, economic organization, and formal political structures**, while **patriarchy, colonial/racial hierarchy, and class inequality** proved far more durable — this "change on the surface, continuity underneath" argument is the highest-scoring pattern for Unit 5 CCOT essays.
